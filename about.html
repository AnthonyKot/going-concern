Method

How this book is built

Four decisions were made before the first chapter was written. Everything else follows from them, and where the book is weak it is usually because one of them is being violated.

1. Cases first, books second

The sibling volumes in this series were built against named source texts — Landau and Dirac, Nielsen and Chuang. Business has no canon of that kind. It has a very large number of books that agree with each other about things nobody has checked.

So the unit of evidence here is a company, not a framework. Every chapter is anchored on one real business, named and dated, with figures. The books appear at the foot of the chapter, in the reading list, where they belong: as places to go next, not as authorities to defer to.

This has a cost worth stating. A single case is not evidence of a general law, and this book does not pretend otherwise. What a case can do is show a decision being made under conditions the reader recognises, and show what it cost. That is a more honest thing to offer than a framework with a diagram.

2. One reader

An experienced software developer, around forty, fifteen or twenty years salaried, thinking about going out on their own — a consultancy, a small product, an agency, or buying something small that already works. Self-funded, heading for somewhere between one and twenty people, still doing the work. No outside investors, no board. The payroll is real and it clears on the same day every month whether or not the work came in.

That reader is unusually shaped. Fifteen years of building software leaves someone fluent in systems, constraints, iteration, measurement and technical risk — better than most business books assume, which is why nothing here explains what a feedback loop is. The gaps are somewhere else, and across a great many people they are the same gaps: building before proving demand, because building is the enjoyable part; reading interest as purchasing intent; avoiding direct selling and calling the avoidance a marketing strategy; pricing by effort rather than by what the result is worth; conflating revenue, profit and cash; automating a process before establishing that it should exist; remaining the indispensable technical bottleneck, which feels like responsibility; and underestimating distribution, management, and the value of capacity you are not using.

The book is organised around those, not around a curriculum.

It also assumes a mortgage. Writing for someone with fifteen years of salary behind them rather than for a twenty-four-year-old changes the risk arithmetic in every chapter, and it is why the book opens with five chapters on finding out cheaply whether there is anything here — one of them entirely about how to test a business without resigning — and closes with one on how to stop.

Four of the twenty-eight chapters — Deciding Not To, Leaving Well, Enough, and Knowing When to Stop — would be incoherent in a book written for the venture-track founder, which is precisely why they are here.

2b. Where jurisdiction changes the answer

Some questions have no general answer. What you may build while still employed, whether a non-compete binds you, what resigning does to your entitlements, when a contractor is legally an employee, what it costs to end an employment, who owns code written by someone you paid — these are matters of local law, and a chapter that hand-waves them has not helped anyone.

So chapters carry a boxed On Dutch ground section where it applies. The general argument stays portable; the local detail stays quarantined, dated, and sourced to an official body rather than to a blog. Every box names the professional whose job the question actually is. None of them constitutes advice, and none of them recommends a structure — the point is to tell you that an expensive question exists, not to answer it.

3. The four moves

Every chapter has the same shape, so that you can find the part you need without reading the parts you don't:

MoveWhat it does
The situationThe decision, stated concretely, in the second person. You are somewhere specific when this chapter starts.
What actually happenedOne company. Dated. With numbers. The load-bearing part — a thin case makes a thin chapter.
The reasoningThe transferable principle, with its boundaries named in the same breath as the principle itself.
MondayThree to five things you could do this week. Concrete enough to fail at.

4. The counter-case

This is the device that distinguishes the book, and it replaces the proof ledger that ran through the sibling volume.

A counter-case is a second, shorter story in which the advice of that chapter was followed — correctly, in good faith, by capable people — and the outcome was bad. Not a strawman version of the advice. The real version, applied well, failing.

The reason is that business writing has a structural problem it rarely admits to. Cases are selected after the outcome is known, which makes the genre a machine for converting luck into principle. A principle you cannot see failing is one you cannot use, because you have no way of telling whether you are inside its domain of validity or outside it. The counter-case is where the domain gets drawn.

Why the book sometimes owes one

There is a problem with this device, and it is better stated than hidden. Businesses that followed good advice and died mostly do not write about it. Failure is under-published, and the same survivorship bias that makes this genre unreliable also makes its correction hard to source: the success stories are abundant and free, and the cases that would falsify them often exist only as a company that quietly stopped filing.

Two responses were available. The first was to widen the definition until the available material fits — to let a story in which the advice was ignored and things went well anyway count as a counter-case. That would have been easy, and every chapter would have had one.

The second, taken here, is to hold the definition and record the shortfall. Where a chapter has a genuine counter-case it carries one. Where it does not, it carries a visible note saying what the counter-case would have to be and that it has not been found — chapter two and chapter one both currently owe one. A block that merely illuminates the case, such as what became of the idea afterwards, is labelled an aftermath and is not permitted to stand in for the real thing.

Four chapters in, the honest running score is this: no chapter yet carries a counter-case that meets the definition against its own central advice. One carries a real counter-case to a subsidiary claim. Four carry recorded debts. That ratio is expected to hold rather than improve, and the notes are a permanent feature of the book rather than an embarrassment to be tidied away before it is finished.

Chapter four is the reason that sentence is worded so carefully. It ran for two drafts asserting that its counter-case had done "exactly what this chapter asks" — while a later paragraph in the same block conceded the opposite. Scored against the chapter's own three-part test, the case met about one and a half parts. Every figure in it was correctly quoted and sourced throughout. The block is still there, relabelled as a limit: a real case that qualifies the advice without falsifying it.

An admitted gap is worth more than a filled one that does not hold, particularly in the part of the book whose whole purpose is to show you where to distrust it.

Sourcing

A figure earns its place in the prose if it comes from a company filing, a court document, a named journalist's reporting in an outlet that runs corrections, or a first-party statement made at the time. Regulatory filings are preferred over everything else, and this book leans on them heavily: a company describing its own capacity utilisation to the SEC is under obligations that a founder reminiscing on a podcast is not.

A founder's later account of their own numbers is weaker evidence, and is attributed as such in the prose — by his own account, as she later described it. This is not pedantry. Retrospective founder numbers are systematically flattering, and the flattery is usually in the direction of making the outcome look more deserved than it was.

The largest contamination risk in this genre is the round number that appears in every blog post about a company and traces back to nothing. Where a figure cannot be traced past the blogs it is either cut or carried with a visible mark until it can be. The mark stays in the published text on purpose.

Each chapter's reading list separates source — where the facts of the case came from — from further reading, which is where you go if the chapter interested you.

Checking the sources yourself

A book that claims to work from filings should let you confirm it without taking the author's word, so the checking is mechanical. The repository carries a register of quoted figures, each paired with the exact sentence that must appear in the document it is attributed to. A script downloads those documents from their original archives and confirms that every registered sentence is still there:

./verify.sh          # every chapter
./verify.sh 01       # one chapter

The four published chapters carry 107 such claims between them, against nineteen documents: four Webvan filings, Instacart's registration statement, two Dutch statutes, five founder posts, and the dataset Everpix published when it shut down — including three consumer-survey PDFs, which the script converts before matching rather than pretending to have read. Everything is inspectable: the source list, the claim register and the verification script are on GitHub, and the checks also run on a weekly schedule, because a citation can rot without anyone touching the repository.

This turned out to matter more than expected. Chapter 1 was first drafted from search results that cited SEC filings rather than from the filings themselves, and pulling the actual documents falsified four figures — among them the billion-dollar Bechtel construction contract that anchors almost every published account of Webvan's collapse. The company's own quarterly report states that it had "no obligation under the Bechtel agreement to build any distribution centers and, consequently, no capital commitment" until it chose to proceed with a given site. The famous contract was an estimate for a programme, not a debt.

That correction changed what the chapter argues. It is a small illustration of a large problem: a great deal of what business books assert about companies is downstream of other business books, and the citation chain terminates in nobody. The verification script exists so that this book's chain terminates in a document.

What the script cannot do

There are two kinds of accuracy here, and only one of them is mechanical.

Factual accuracy — the numbers, dates and quotations are present in the source they are attributed to. That is what the script checks, and it checks it exhaustively. Interpretive accuracy — the case actually supports the lesson the chapter draws from it — is checked by nothing but reading. A chapter can be assembled entirely from correctly quoted figures and still be wrong about what they show.

This is not hypothetical. Chapter four spent two drafts claiming that a case had followed its advice exactly when, measured against the chapter's own numbered test, it had followed about half. Every claim row passed throughout, and still passes. Which means the script's existence is itself a mild hazard: it produces a feeling of rigour that covers half the surface. It is worth saying so here rather than letting a green tick imply more than it earns.

How this book was made

Worth stating plainly, since the rest of this page is about not having to take things on trust.

This book was written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic: the chapters and this method page, and beyond the prose, the five-part structure, the chapter list, and the verification tooling. Successive drafts were reviewed by ChatGPT, which is where several of the more useful corrections came from: that the outline was missing retention, contracts, management and career-transition risk; that the landing page read like a book proposal; that a chapter cross-reference had gone stale after a renumbering; that unpublished chapter titles were failing contrast minimums. The book is edited, directed and published by Anthony Kot, who decides what it is for, who it is written to, and what stays in.

This arrangement is the reason the verification script exists rather than a nice-to-have beside it. A model writing about companies will produce fluent, plausible, well-shaped figures that came from nowhere — the first draft of chapter one did exactly that, taking four numbers from search results that cited SEC filings, one of which reversed the meaning of the case. Fluency is not evidence. So every figure in this book is pinned to a sentence in a primary document, and a script re-checks all of them weekly and fails loudly when one stops matching.

That is a fair summary of the whole method: the writing can come from anywhere, and the numbers have to come from a document you can open yourself.

What this book does not do

  • It does not tell you to quit your job.
  • It does not have a system, a framework with an acronym, or a diagram of four quadrants.
  • It does not sell anything. There is no newsletter, no course, and no consulting practice behind it.
  • It does not treat growth as self-evidently good, or smallness as a failure to grow.
  • It does not tell the Kodak story, the Blockbuster story, or any story involving a garage.
A standing caveat. Nothing here is legal, tax, or accounting advice, and the arithmetic in Part III is deliberately simplified to the version you can do in one column of a spreadsheet. Jurisdictions differ, and the differences are exactly the sort that get expensive. Where a chapter is about to cost you money, it says so and tells you who to ask.
