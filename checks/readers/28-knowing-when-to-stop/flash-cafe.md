### 1. Gut reaction after first read
It gave me a knot in my stomach reading about closing down before I’ve even pulled my first espresso shot. But it’s refreshingly blunt—no cheerleading or motivational nonsense, just the cold reality of how businesses actually die of a slow bleed. Still, with all the talk of million-dollar seed rounds and Dutch tax laws, I wondered what on earth this had to do with opening a high-street café in Yorkshire.

### 2. What landed — specific passages and why they worked for someone like me
* **"It has not failed. That is what makes it hard. Customers still pay, the people still come in, the bank still clears the wages most months."** I’ve seen this happen to three different bosses over twenty-five years: you convince yourself everything is grand because the till rings on a Saturday, while quietly going under.
* **"stopping well costs money and it has to be there on the day."** That’s proper trade sense. If things go wrong, you need cash left in the tin to pay your veg supplier, settle with the staff, and hand back the keys with your head held high, rather than doing a midnight flit.
* **"the weekly conversation with yourself or your partner has had the same single item on it for longer than you would admit — what more can we do?"** That captures the sheer exhaustion of managing a struggling site better than any spreadsheet could.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation
* **"we raised our seed round of almost $1M"** and **"a full pivot, to become 'the Shopify for digital products'"**. This is Monopoly money and tech jargon. If my footfall drops, I can’t "pivot" to an online software marketplace; I’m stuck with a five-year commercial lease, a bank loan, and an espresso machine.
* **"De turboliquidatie kun je niet gebruiken voor het beëindigen van een eenmanszaak..."** Dumping half a page of untranslated Dutch corporate law into the middle of the text completely derailed my concentration and felt totally irrelevant.
* **"Take chapter thirteen's cash... and divide it by the gap between a month's outgoings, counted as chapter sixteen counted them..."** Constant cross-referencing like an algebra textbook makes my eyes glaze over.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I nearly tossed it aside as another Silicon Valley manual written for app developers who play with other people's millions. On the second read, I noticed the candid note at the back where the author openly admits software founders are just the only ones who publish their figures, and that the case study had a huge safety net most readers don't get. I appreciated that rare honesty, even if the core example still felt completely disconnected from daily trading.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Since this is the final chapter, I’d actually go back to the beginning and read Chapter 4 to figure out my own day-one stopping rule before I sign my lease.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Shove the Dutch liquidation paperwork into an appendix, and replace the software startup example with an ordinary high-street business—like a bakery, pub, or dry cleaner—so those of us putting our redundancy money into physical premises can actually see ourselves in the advice.
