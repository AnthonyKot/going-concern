### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is one of the rarer business-book chapters that treats shutting down as an operational decision instead of a character test. The core rule—attach a number and date to the final move—felt useful, but the chapter buried it under cross-references and a very long Netherlands-specific legal detour.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“It is whether going on is a decision or the absence of one” landed hardest. I’ve watched teams keep projects alive because no one wanted to own the kill decision; I can easily imagine doing the same with my own money and employees involved.

“Every quarter spends something you will want later” also worked. Reserve, employee goodwill, and asset value are concrete things that decay while a founder tells himself he is being persistent.

The Binpress sequence was credible because it included actual numbers: revenue plateauing around $30K monthly after raising almost $1M, followed by multiple hires and a three-month pivot that changed nothing. “Most of the stuff we did had a positive effect” is especially believable: small improvements can disguise the absence of a viable trajectory.

Finally, “If [the move] has not moved [the figure] by [the date]” is actionable. That resembles how I’d define an engineering rollback threshold or terminate an experiment before sunk-cost reasoning takes over.

### 3. What didn’t

The Dutch winding-up box lost me almost immediately. “De turboliquidatie kun je niet gebruiken…” begins an enormous jurisdiction-specific section that excludes me as a US reader, despite explicitly telling me to skip it. Its length still interrupts the chapter’s momentum, and the raw `<em lang="nl">` fragments make it feel insufficiently edited.

The repeated references to “chapter sixteen’s sentence,” “chapter twenty-two’s three,” and “chapter twenty’s box” made the method feel unnecessarily dependent on remembering the author’s private vocabulary. By chapter 28 I might recognize it, but I should not have to reconstruct several earlier abstractions to understand a shutdown rule.

I also doubted “Binpress’s order is the one this book takes as general.” One founder’s retrospective can illustrate an order; it cannot establish that the order generalizes across bootstrapped companies, debt-heavy businesses, regulated firms, or US employment and bankruptcy law.

The “What would prove this chapter wrong” section sounds rigorous but largely immunizes the claim from evidence: the counter-case is declared “nearly unwritable,” then the rule survives because no published example appears.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I noticed the author carefully excludes healthy, deliberately non-growing businesses; this is not generic “quit sooner” advice. I also appreciated the reserve calculation and pricing-the-parts exercise more. Conversely, the evidentiary gap became clearer: the practical rule is sensible, but Binpress does not prove that a quarter or two is generally the correct evaluation window.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, if there were one, because the operational framing earns enough trust despite the overextended evidence.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Move the Dutch legal material to an appendix and replace it here with a one-page, jurisdiction-neutral shutdown checklist plus a clearly labeled instruction to obtain local legal and accounting advice.
