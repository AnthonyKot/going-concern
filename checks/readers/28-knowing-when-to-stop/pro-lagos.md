### 1. Gut reaction after first read
It felt like reading a manual for Silicon Valley tech bros while I’m out here trying to pay for diesel just to keep my phone charged and my ring light on. The scale of the money made me roll my eyes, but underneath all that grammar, the core warning about bleeding out slowly without a hard deadline actually struck a nerve.

### 2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me.
The absolute best part was: "Exhausted options are a date, not a feeling." Running an IG boutique in Lagos means running on vibes, prayers, and sheer stubbornness. I am always hoping the next bale clears customs faster, or the next promoted post goes viral. The realization that "Every answer costs another quarter, and every quarter spends something you will want later" felt like a slap in the face. My "reserve" isn't venture capital; it's my personal savings losing value to inflation every single day I stay stuck in the "what more can we do?" phase without a hard stop date.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation. Quote the spot.
I completely checked out during the Binpress story. Quote: "We had spent a little over half the money we raised... we raised our seed round of almost $1M." Who is "we"? I started this with my own pocket money. They hired a "developer evangelist"; I am fighting with dispatch riders on WhatsApp. Then there’s the whole attempt at "selling the assets only (site and code-base)" and the massive gray box about "On Dutch ground" laws. I felt totally excluded. The author assumes formal structures, willing corporate buyers, and a legal safety net that simply do not exist in my reality. If my business fails, I just sell my leftover clothes at a heavy discount and delete the Instagram page. 

### 4. What changed between read one and read two.
On the first read, the startup jargon and that weird Dutch legal section annoyed me so much that I almost missed the point. On the second read, I forced myself to look past the examples, and I realized the "Monday" action list at the bottom is actually useful. Even though I don't have "a lease" or "code" to sell, the instruction to "Price the parts now, while they are worth something" made me think about my unsold inventory. Valuing what my dead stock would fetch *today* rather than waiting until I'm desperate is smart, practical advice.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? One sentence.
Since the text says this is chapter 28 of 28, there is no next chapter, but if there was, I'd skip it unless it dropped the tech-bro fantasies and talked about a real cash-based hustle.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change.
Ditch the million-dollar software startup case studies and use a normal, cash-constrained retail business as an example so everyday founders don't feel completely alienated by the second page.
