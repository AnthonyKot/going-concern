**1. Gut reaction after first read**
I felt seen, but also a bit like a laboratory specimen. As someone trying to start a modest baking business here in the Netherlands without the safety net of my old corporate job in Kyiv, it was a relief to read a business book that admits most small trades don't have slick software or guaranteed salaries. However, the author’s academic tone made my daily survival feel like a theoretical puzzle.

**2. What landed**
The advice to measure the floor in units instead of a fixed money threshold landed perfectly: "how many of the thing you sell, at this week's cost of making it, cover this week's rent, this week's supplier and the minimum you take home." With supermarket prices constantly changing and my savings shrinking, static financial projections (like I used in my old marketing job) don't work for my home-kitchen budget. 

I also deeply appreciated the bluntness about risk: "there may be no salary to return to... the reader with none is running every experiment in this book at a higher stake than any of its cases did." It validated my anxiety. Western business books usually assume you can just "fail fast" and go back to a comfortable office job. As a refugee on temporary protection, I cannot.

**3. What didn't**
I felt alienated by the constant apologies for not having data, which framed my reality as an academic void. The author writes, "This is the one chapter in the book whose case is an absence, and the absence is the finding." My livelihood isn't an "absence"—it's just happening in Ukrainian Telegram groups instead of public stock filings. 

Also, the advice on finding customers—"The school gate at half past eight clears all four. So does the church group, the estate's chat"—felt out of touch for a foreigner. My Dutch is still clumsy, I don't go to the local church, and I am certainly not integrated enough to be in the Dutch neighborhood WhatsApp group. Building trust when you are an outsider is a much steeper climb than just standing at a school gate.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**
The first time, I was annoyed by the author's obsession with finding a "case" to prove their points, feeling it was too detached and clinical. The second time, I realized this is actually a sign of respect. The author refuses to feed me fake, American-style hustle-culture success stories, which I despise. By admitting what they don't know, they strip away the motivational fluff and leave me with a clean, strict framework—the Monday checklist—which I can actually use.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**
Yes, because the Monday checklist at the end convinced me the author respects my time and won't insult my intelligence with empty optimism.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**
Stop apologizing for the lack of a formal "case study" and just go interview three actual immigrants running cash businesses at a local market; our notebooks and bank apps are real evidence, even if they aren't published online for VC founders to read.
