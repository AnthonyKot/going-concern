### 1. Gut reaction after first read

I respected the author’s refusal to manufacture a case, but this felt like a long caveat inserted to defend the book’s methodology. The practical checklist was clear, yet I kept wondering why prescriptions like “six weeks” deserved confidence when the chapter explicitly says it has no evidence.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“The businesses that publish are the ones with the instruments already” is the strongest observation here. In tech, we routinely derive startup advice from unusually instrumented companies, then mistake data availability for representativeness. Naming that selection effect builds credibility.

“A screenshot of a transfer is not money until it clears; ‘I’ll take it’ in a chat…[is] worth zero” also worked. That is concrete, falsifiable, and maps cleanly to my world: verbal enthusiasm, survey responses, and even procurement interest are not revenue.

I also liked “things sold, never things made or bought in.” It captures a failure mode I recognize from software side projects: counting features shipped or inventory created instead of customer behavior.

Finally, “a blank cell is a thing you did not write down, not a zero” is excellent operational advice. It is precise and prevents bad data from acquiring false authority.

### 3. What didn’t

I am not the primary reader for cafés, dressmakers, or cash-at-the-door trades, so much of the middle became repetitive: each prior chapter’s instrument gets translated into stalls, faces, units, and notes files. I understood the pattern after three examples.

More importantly, the author sometimes turns acknowledged speculation into suspiciously exact instructions. “Six weeks before you read a direction” and “two attempts across a fortnight” appear unsupported. The chapter says “no figure…is a claim about the world,” but these figures still function as advice about the world.

“Yours is the kind that could not, or did not” also rubbed me wrong. Many small operators can measure but rationally choose not to collect detailed customer data because the administrative cost exceeds its value. Framing them as businesses that simply lack instruments feels mildly condescending.

“Same week last year” is unusable for a new business, while “faces seen before” is a weak demand proxy in tourist, event-driven, or infrequent-purchase businesses.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I saw that the repetition is partly deliberate: this is an audit of which earlier tools survive outside instrumented companies, not a standalone case chapter. The caveats held up better than I initially thought, especially the admission that salary assumptions raise the stakes for poorer founders. But the unsupported timing rules bothered me more, because the author’s evidence standard makes those arbitrary specifics especially conspicuous.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the methodological honesty earned another chapter, even though this interlude needed tightening.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Rewrite the “Monday” section to label every instruction either logically derived or unvalidated heuristic, and remove exact durations unless the text explains why those durations were chosen.
