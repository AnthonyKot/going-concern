### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is more credible than most delegation advice because it admits the evidence is weak and gives me an actual test to run. The procedure-versus-judgment distinction felt immediately useful, though the chapter repeats it enough that I started skimming near the end.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“The work moved and he did not” is the sharpest line in the chapter. I’ve seen exactly that failure mode: a task disappears from my queue, but every branch in its decision tree still interrupts me.

“A document does not count as written down until a named other person has used it, unsupervised, on a live instance” also works. That turns “we have docs” from an organizational virtue signal into a falsifiable claim. Recording elapsed time and correctness against my baseline is concrete enough that I could try it with an on-call runbook or customer escalation this week.

I also trusted the author more for saying the Gymdesk remedy’s “result is not yet in.” Business books routinely convert one founder’s retrospective into a universal law. Here, the author clearly separates Galperin’s observed problem from his unproven solution.

### 3. What didn't

“The dividing line is whether the task is the same shape twice” is memorable but too binary. In software, many tasks share a recognizable shape while still requiring judgment: incident response, code review, hiring, enterprise support. Good documentation can transfer heuristics, escalation thresholds, examples, and boundaries even when it cannot “install” intuition. The chapter risks convincing an owner to classify too much as judgment and preserve the very dependency it wants to remove.

I got bored during “Boundaries.” The extended inventory of what belongs to chapters seventeen, nineteen, twenty, and twenty-three reads like book architecture leaking into the argument.

“What would prove this chapter wrong” also feels strained. A bad documented handoff would not necessarily prove the chapter wrong, because the author can always say the procedure was incomplete or the task was really judgment. That makes the advice less falsifiable than the section claims.

Finally, “competent stranger” hides a major variable. Competent in the function, the industry, or this particular system? The answer changes what a successful handoff demonstrates.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I mainly saw a practical warning against documentation theater. On the second, I noticed the more valuable mechanism: the recipient edits the procedure before the founder does, converting each failure into shared operating knowledge. I also became less convinced by the procedure/judgment split; it works as a sorting prompt, but not as a clean model of real knowledge transfer.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promised chapter on the first hire is close enough to decisions I’ve considered personally.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change

Replace most of the “Boundaries” section with one worked handoff showing the original procedure, where the recipient failed, the two recorded numbers, and the exact revision they made.
