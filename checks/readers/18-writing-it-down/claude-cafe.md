**1. Gut reaction after first read**

The first line got me — a job that takes you fifteen minutes takes the new person an hour. That's every kitchen I've ever worked in, and nobody's ever put it that plainly to me. But then it turned into a lot of talk about deploy scripts and SaaS and folders in Google Drive, and I started thinking, this is a lad who writes software, what's he got to do with me and a coffee machine. Still, I didn't stop reading, which surprised me.

**2. What landed**

"It is also, left unexamined, the single most comfortable form of avoidance available to an owner who does not actually want to hand the decision over." That's a proper slap. I've watched managers do exactly that — write the folder, laminate it, and still stand over the poor lass on the till. I've done it myself.

"Handing a task over for real means watching someone else do it worse than you would, live, in front of a customer who might notice." That's the whole thing, isn't it. That's the bit that costs. Nobody says that out loud.

"I'll delegate once it's properly documented is not a plan. It is a stopping condition with no way to fail it." I understood that immediately, no jargon needed.

And the Monday bit: hand it over, leave the room, write down the time and whether it came out right. That I can do on a Tuesday morning with a pen. "Wrong turns are the point" — yes.

The knowledge-versus-intuition split is right too. I can write down how to do the cash-up. I cannot write down how I know a table's about to kick off before they've said anything.

**3. What didn't**

"why the deploy script has that one flag nobody remembers adding" — I don't know what that is and it told me who this book thinks it's for. Same with "self-funded B2B SaaS at close to the reader's scale." I had to guess at that. And "over 10 FTE" — say ten staff.

The whole "What would prove this chapter wrong" section lost me. "note the asymmetry honestly" — that's the TED talk voice creeping back in. I skimmed it both times.

And the honesty gets a bit much. You tell me three separate times that this fella never actually proved his system worked — in Move 2, in the Boundaries, and in the Note at the bottom. After the second time I thought, well why are you telling me this then. One clear line would do.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the case study isn't really the point — the point is the sort. Procedure on one side, judgment on the other, and don't waste a quarter writing up the second column. First read I thought this was a chapter about writing manuals; it isn't, it's about not kidding yourself. I also noticed the SOP thing that actually matters: the reps write the entries themselves, on the ticket in front of them. That's the one bit I'd genuinely nick — my staff write the notes, not me. It's buried in a long quote and I missed it entirely first time.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — next one's about the first hire and I'm three weeks off making mine.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Bring "the people using it write it, on the case in front of them" up out of the quote and make it a headed point of its own, in your words, with a non-software example — the barista who writes down the fix for the grinder the day she finds it. It's the most useful idea in the chapter and it's currently hiding inside a block quote about knowledge bases.
