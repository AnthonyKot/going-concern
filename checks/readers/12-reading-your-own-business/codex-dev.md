## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core argument is useful: annual accounts are historical compliance artifacts, not an operating system. I liked the practical three-number framework, but the chapter spends too long proving a narrow point about Dutch filings before reaching it. As a US-based software developer considering a side business, I felt more like an observer than the intended reader.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“The subtler failure is to read the first two, understand them properly, and believe you have therefore read the business.” That distinction feels real. In tech, I’ve seen teams understand quarterly metrics perfectly while missing what is currently happening with customers, pipeline, or delivery.

“Available cash is what has already happened. Committed demand is what is going to. The operating quantity is happening now.” This is the chapter’s clearest and most memorable model. It gives me three time horizons rather than three arbitrary KPIs.

“Signed, not proposed, not verbally agreed” also worked. Side-business forecasts are especially vulnerable to counting enthusiastic conversations as revenue.

“A spreadsheet is sufficient and a dashboard is procrastination.” That is blunt, credible advice for someone like me, because building instrumentation is exactly how a developer can avoid confronting weak demand.

## 3. What didn’t

I got bored during the extended Dutch statutory discussion: “Micro (art. 2:395a): assets not more than €450.000…” I understand why the evidence is there, but the chapter makes me absorb legal-form names, thresholds, filing deadlines, and quoted Dutch before delivering advice that is mostly jurisdiction-independent.

“The figures are in the box” and repeated references to bv, nv, eenmanszaak, vof, KVK, and Burgerlijk Wetboek made me feel excluded. The chapter eventually admits, “The regime described here is Dutch,” but by then Dutch compliance has occupied much of the argument. For a US reader, an equivalent framing around LLCs, S-corps, tax returns, and internal books would make the problem immediately recognizable.

I also doubted the prescription precisely where the author does: “the three weekly numbers are reasoning, not evidence.” I respect that disclosure, but it substantially weakens the chapter. There is no worked example showing how the three measures reveal something the P&L misses, nor a failure case where the dashboard gives false confidence.

Finally, “Choosing this measure is the only part of the exercise that requires real thought” sounds glib. Defining available cash and committed demand can be difficult in a software business with annual prepaid contracts, cloud commitments, refunds, deferred revenue, and uneven founder compensation.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, the Dutch material felt like a long detour. On the second, I saw that it establishes the book’s negative claim carefully: public filings were never designed to manage the business. I also noticed the author is unusually candid about the lack of evidence, which increased my trust in the author while decreasing my confidence in the specific three-metric prescription.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promised explanation of why profit diverges from cash is more directly relevant to a prospective founder than this chapter’s filing regime.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace half of the Dutch legal-detail section with one worked example of a small software consultancy tracking cash after obligations, signed backlog, and billable hours for six weeks—including one misleading signal and how the owner catches it.
