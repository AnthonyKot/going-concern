**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I nearly put it down at "eenmanszaak." I'm opening a café in Lancashire, not Leiden, and half the chapter is Dutch law in Dutch. But the bit underneath the law — nobody is going to hand you the numbers that actually run the place, you have to build them yourself — that I recognised from twenty-five years of watching owners find out in March what went wrong in October. So: annoyed, then grudgingly nodding.

**2. What landed**

"Some months after the year has ended you receive a careful description of a period you can no longer do anything about." That's every set of year-end accounts I've ever seen an owner squint at. True, and it's said plainly.

"Not the bank balance alone, which flatters — it contains VAT you are holding, payroll taxes, and customer deposits that were never yours." I've seen a manager spend the VAT. I've seen a *good* one do it. That line is worth the price of the chapter to somebody like me who's about to have one account with everything sloshing about in it.

"A spreadsheet is sufficient and a dashboard is procrastination." Thank you. I hate spreadsheets but I hate being sold software more.

The "photograph, not a film" line for the balance sheet — first time anyone's explained the difference between the two statements in a way I'll remember.

And the honesty in "What this chapter owes": "the three weekly numbers are reasoning, not evidence." I don't get that from the people who want to sell me a course. It made me trust the rest more, not less.

**3. What didn't**

The whole of Move 2 and the "On Dutch ground" box. "Micro (art. 2:395a): assets not more than €450.000…" and pages of it. I understand it's a Dutch book and they say so in Boundaries, but I skipped a third of the chapter and felt like I'd wandered into someone else's country's paperwork. If the point is "the public filing tells you nothing," one paragraph does it.

"Chapter three's distinction turned on your own forecast" — I haven't got a clue what that refers to, and it's dropped in like I should.

"Committed demand. Contracted work, confirmed orders, bookings or subscriptions." A café has none of those. My demand is whoever walks past at half eleven. The chapter admits "a template is least likely to fit you" but doesn't then say what a walk-in trade watches instead. That's my situation exactly and it's left blank.

"Trace five lines back to reality… with your bookkeeper if that is what it takes." I haven't got one yet. It's me and a shoebox.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the sentence that saves the chapter for me and missed first time: "if you are a sole trader you publish neither, which changes who can see your position but not what you need to run it." So the Dutch stuff isn't really about me but the conclusion still is. I also noticed the third number — "deliberately not units made" — and thought: for me that's covers served, not scones baked. That's actually a useful thought, and I only got it because the second read gave me room to translate.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — the next one's called "Profit Is Not Cash" and that's the thing I'm most frightened of.

**6. One thing I'd tell the author to change**

Move the Dutch statute into the box entirely and give the space back to the reader who has no contracts: tell a walk-in business — a café, a shop, a hairdresser — what their "committed demand" line is, or admit there isn't one and say what to watch instead.
