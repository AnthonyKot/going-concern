**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I felt like I had opened the wrong book. The first move tells me I am forty-ish, with a mortgage, dependents, and fifteen years of a reliable salary, and then the hero of the chapter is a software engineer in Nagoya whose product sold while he slept. I sell clothes from a phone in Lagos. By the Dutch civil-code section I was not confused — I was done being the audience.

**2. What landed**

“Nerve is what people call the decision afterwards, once they know how it turned out.” That is every “just take the leap” reel I have been sent, and it is a relief to hear it named as after-the-fact storytelling.

“One good month proves you have friends. Twelve months proves you have a market. The interesting number is what the business does in a month when you do nothing.” That is fashion on Instagram. A week of orders after a post is not a business. If I go quiet on WhatsApp, sales die.

“Anything requiring you to answer a phone during working hours, hold stock, or show up.” That is my whole operation: stock I paid for in naira that is already worth less, a generator, a delivery guy who might not come, customers who want a reply now.

“Treat any single revenue or profit figure from a small business — including your own — as provisional.” I have screenshotted a good week and treated it like a year. The Bingo Card restatement — revenue off by two percent, profit by twelve — made that concrete.

Galperin going back to a salary instead of burning the thing down. That option exists here too, if I can find the salary.

**3. What didn't**

“You are forty-ish. You have a mortgage, possibly people who depend on you, and fifteen years of being good at something that pays reliably.” I am twenty-four. I do not have a mortgage. I may not have a salary. That is not a metaphor. It is a door shutting.

Then: “time is precisely what a forty-year-old has less of than the twenty-four-year-old the rest of this genre is written for.” So the author knows I exist, and wrote me out of this chapter on purpose.

Bingo Card Creator on five hours a week is not transferable to clothes. His January work sold in June. Mine sits in a bag in a room that may not have light.

“On Dutch ground” — articles, *nietig*, unemployment benefit, *arbeidsrechtadvocaat*, “an hour is cheap relative to the mortgage.” That is another country glued into the middle. I have no unemployment net. A lawyer’s hour is restock money. Inflation means “savings divided by floor” is not a plan; the floor moves every month.

The “when the business cannot accumulate” standard still assumes I have a job to resign from and buyers who will sign, wait, and pay in August. My buyers pay on WhatsApp or they disappear.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read I filed it as another salary-to-startup chapter for people with desks. Second read I saw that the “cannot accumulate” section is actually my trade, and the author admits “no case in these pages supports this version.” That honesty held up. So did the restated numbers, and the admission that Galperin’s six-month rule would have killed a business that later worked. The Dutch block still felt like a different book.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**

One more, because the closing ladder is sharper than the case study — then I am gone if chapter six starts with mortgages again.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**

Do not open a chapter on leaving well by telling me I am forty with a mortgage. Make the stock-phone-show-up business the main case, with a real example, not the exception you confess you could not find.
