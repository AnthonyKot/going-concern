**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Well, it's honest, I'll give it that. It doesn't shout at me to "take the leap," which is what everyone in my life has been doing since the redundancy came through. But by the middle I was thinking: this man built a bingo-card program in Japan on five hours a week. I'm signing a lease on a corner unit with a fryer. What's this got to do with me?

**2. What landed**

"The salary is not the enemy here. It is the cheapest funding you will ever have access to." I've been told for months that staying in work was cowardice. That one line put it straight.

"Calculate your floor, not your salary." I know my floor to the pound — I've never thought of it as a business number, just what I need for the month. Having it called the right number was a relief. And "considerably higher than people guess" — true, I did it in my head and had forgotten the car insurance.

"You will want to leave before the evidence arrives. ... It looks like a very good month, a large customer, a bad week at work, or a reorganisation." That's exactly what a redundancy is. He's describing me and he doesn't know it.

"a hope with a date on it." I'd wear that on a badge.

**3. What didn't**

The whole McKenzie section. The dollar figures to the cent — "$31,156.18 net of refunds, spent $12,630.47" — I skimmed on the first read and only caught the point on the second. Then a paragraph on whether his profit was restated by twelve per cent. I don't care. Nobody opening a café cares.

"So will anything requiring you to answer a phone during working hours, hold stock, or show up." That's me, in a throwaway clause, and then the author says "That case gets its own answer further down." The answer further down is for "a consultancy, an agency." Signed pipeline from independent buyers who know you're unavailable? People don't sign to buy coffee in August. I felt the author had never stood behind a counter.

"On Dutch ground" — I'm in Yorkshire. Half a page of Dutch law with the Dutch words left in. I know it says "None of this is advice," but I turned the page thinking this book wasn't written for me.

And "No case in these pages supports this version" — I respect the honesty but it's a bit like the surveyor saying he's not looked at the roof.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time, the Gymdesk bit at the end was the best thing in the chapter and I'd nearly skipped it. "It was how long its owner needed to learn marketing." Six months of runway, wrong about the business, went back to work and kept going. That's the honest version of what could happen to me, and the idea that failing the date means "a return, not the end" is actually usable — I could go back to managing someone else's place; I've done it for 25 years. I also noticed the "bounded experiment" is meant for people like me, it's just buried under consultancy talk.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but cautiously — the next one's called "Who Is This For," and after this chapter I'd like the author to answer that about me.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Replace the consultancy in the "cannot accumulate" section with a shop or a café — someone who has to be there, has stock, and can't get a "signed pipeline." Show what the standard looks like for us, because that clause about "show up" is most of the businesses on my high street.
