## 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is unusually sober for a chapter about quitting a job. I trusted it more because it treated resignation as an evidence problem, not a personality test, though the chapter became less useful when it shifted from a well-documented software case to unsupported prescriptions and Dutch employment law.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“You do not need courage to resign. You need evidence” immediately addressed the part of startup culture I distrust: retroactively presenting survivorship as bravery. The reframing—“What would have to be true before leaving is the lower-risk option?”—gave me a question I could actually use with a mortgage and dependents.

“The salary…is the cheapest funding you will ever have access to” also worked. That is a concrete financial claim, not motivational filler, and it correctly accounts for equity, interest, and benefits.

The strongest material was the McKenzie case. Four years, roughly five hours per week, actual sales and profit, and even conflicting restatements made it credible. Calling the evidence “not a good month” but “a shape” captured how I evaluate production systems and business metrics: repeated behavior matters more than a spike.

The distinction between businesses that compound on part-time work and those that sell the founder’s hours was useful. “What did this business earn last month from work I did more than sixty days ago?” is the best diagnostic in the chapter.

## 3. What didn’t

“One good month proves you have friends. Twelve months proves you have a market” is memorable but too absolute. Twelve months can still represent one channel, one unusually large customer, underpricing, or a temporary demand cycle. The chapter’s own insistence on careful evidence makes this slogan feel under-evidenced.

The consultancy alternative—signed pipeline, runway, bounded experiment—is plausible, but the author then admits, “No case in these pages supports this version.” I appreciated the honesty, but at that point I was reading a framework derived from analogy, not the evidence-led standard the chapter had established.

“On Dutch ground” lost me almost completely. Detailed quotations from Dutch statutes are irrelevant to my employment contract, health insurance, non-compete exposure, and unemployment rules in the US. After being addressed so precisely as a forty-ish salaried engineer, I suddenly felt like the intended reader lived somewhere else.

The closing recap was longer than I needed and repeated much of Part I’s machinery.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, Gymdesk seemed to weaken the chapter’s threshold advice. On the second, I saw the more careful point: the consequence of failure need not be killing the business; it can be returning to salary. I also noticed how unusually candid the author was about missing evidence: the consultancy framework has no supporting case, and the true counter-case to the central claim “is still owed.” That raised my trust even while limiting my confidence in the conclusions.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the evidence discipline is strong enough that I want to see whether it survives beyond one unusually favorable software business.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace the Dutch-law section in the main narrative with a jurisdiction-neutral resignation checklist, and move country-specific statutes into clearly labeled regional appendices.
