**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I nearly did skip it, which I suppose is the point. It's about a man selling email software to bloggers, and I'm opening a café. But the bit about closed tabs being a no with no reason stuck — I've had that. People read the menu in the window and walk off, and I never find out why. That's the first time a business book has said something I'd said myself.

**2. What landed**

"Ask a successful founder what worked and you will often be told the name of an output of success, described as its cause." I've heard it for twenty-five years from owners: "It's the atmosphere." No it isn't, the atmosphere was built by the same people they later made redundant. That line felt like being let in on something.

"Is anything frustrating you with MailChimp?" — one question, no pitch. I can do that. Standing outside my unit asking people where they get their coffee now and what annoys them about it. That's a Tuesday, not a strategy.

The switching-cost bit: "the prospect placed all their possible objections on a single thing." I know that one. Customers don't leave because of five things, they leave because of one, and they'll tell you four polite ones first. And him doing the switch himself for free — that's what a good manager does, you just do the thing.

"If you cannot fill five lines, that is the finding: you have been collecting silent nos and calling it market research." Ouch. Honest.

**3. What didn't**

"Move 1 The situation", "Move 2", "Move 3", "Boundaries" — these headings feel like a lecture theatre. I don't know what a "move" is here and nobody tells me.

"MRR" is used about forty times and defined once, sideways, in a quote. Then a strip of numbers — "Jul 2013 · peak, then decline $2,480" — that I skimmed past like a spreadsheet, which it is.

"This is a prosumer sale." No idea what that means. And the following line — "Neither a €40 impulse purchase nor a €40,000 procurement" — that's where I felt I'd walked into the wrong room. My sale is a £3 flat white. The author says "the asking generalises; the cadence does not," which I think means "this still applies to you, sort of," but I had to read it three times.

The counter-case section admits the book can't find anyone this didn't work for. Fair enough for honesty, but it left me thinking, so why am I trusting one man's blog post?

**4. What changed between reads**

Second time I saw the Thailand section properly. First read it felt like a detour; second read it's the most useful bit for me. "An instrument you have to hold cannot be left running." I've watched owners who were the whole business, and I've watched what happened when they got ill. I'm about to be that person. It's flagged for chapter seventeen, which I'll actually look for now.

I also noticed the author's own doubts — "Two things changed in the same fortnight, again" — and trusted him more for it, not less. He doesn't oversell.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, mainly because the next one is called "What to Charge" and that's the thing keeping me up at night.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Put one example in here that isn't software. Half a page: the café, the plumber, the dressmaker — someone who has to ask for money across a counter rather than by email. The method's there; I just had to translate every sentence of it myself, and a reader less stubborn than me would've closed the tab. Which, as you say, tells you nothing.
