### 1. Gut reaction after first read (2-4 sentences)
I felt a mix of validation and deep annoyance. The idea of just asking people face-to-face makes sense to my professional background in Ukraine, but the casual mention of throwing $50,000 at a failing project made my stomach drop. It reads like a game played by people who have a financial safety net I simply do not have here in the Netherlands.

### 2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me
The part about "Every closed tab is a no with no reason attached" really struck a chord. In my situation, I cannot afford to waste time guessing what a potential Dutch client wants while my limited savings slowly drain. Also, the strategy of finding a list small enough to finish by hand ("Paleo recipe blogs run by women") is highly practical for me. It means I don't need a huge marketing budget to start; I just need to be very specific and use my time, which is the only resource I actually have. Finally, the realization that "switching... is so much work" and offering to do it for them for free to remove the objection is exactly the kind of unscalable, hard labor I am willing to do to get a foothold in a new country.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation. Quote the spot.
I completely lost trust at this line: "on January 1st, 2014 I invested $50,000 into ConvertKit." That is not "bootstrapping" to someone living under temporary protection with family obligations; that is a lottery win. The author casually mentions that "books were paying" while his main tool failed for 18 months. I don't have a passive income stream or a $50k cushion to keep an experiment alive for a year and a half. I felt excluded because the advice assumes you can just survive a long period of failure without ending up on the street. It is exactly that American hustle optimism where financial risk is treated as a fun learning curve, rather than an existential threat to your family.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I was so distracted and irritated by the $50,000 cash injection that I almost dismissed the whole chapter as rich-guy fiction. On the second read, I forced myself to look past the money and focus on the mechanics of *how* he got those first few customers before the cash infusion. I realized that sending 100 targeted emails and asking "Is anything frustrating you with..." doesn't actually cost money. I also appreciated that the author admitted his own growth stalled when he went on vacation to Thailand—it grounded the advice and showed a rare moment of honesty that the founder is the bottleneck, which reflects the reality of a solo worker like me. 

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? One sentence.
I would read the next chapter on "What to Charge" because pricing is exactly where I feel most insecure as a foreigner entering a local market, but I will keep my guard up.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change.
Put a massive disclaimer right at the start when you talk about "bootstrapping" so readers know you had another successful business funding this 18-month decline, because hiding that $50k lifeline until the middle of the story feels like a betrayal to those of us operating on actual zero.
