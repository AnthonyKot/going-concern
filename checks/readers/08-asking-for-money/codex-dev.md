### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is sharper than most founder-sales advice because it admits the evidence is messy and gives me something concrete to do. But it takes a long chapter to establish a fairly compact point: early sales conversations are primarily an information-gathering mechanism, not a scalable revenue engine.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“Every closed tab is a no with no reason attached” is an excellent framing. As an engineer, I’m accustomed to instrumenting systems, but conversion analytics are observability without diagnostics. The line “converts a no into a sentence” made the value of founder-led sales click without pretending I should enjoy selling.

The distinction between causes and consequences also worked: “Word of mouth, brand, community, inbound. All real, all downstream.” I’ve heard successful founders retroactively describe inbound as if it were an acquisition strategy available on day one. Calling out that survivorship-shaped advice earned trust.

The best evidence was the migration story. Prospects said switching was too much work; ConvertKit removed that burden; migrated accounts reportedly churned at 1.5% rather than 5.5%. That is a credible chain from objection to operational intervention to measurable behavior—not just “talk to customers” folklore.

I also appreciated the explicit limitations: “The asking generalises; the cadence does not,” and the admission that positioning and direct sales changed simultaneously. Business books rarely volunteer their confounds.

### 3. What didn’t

The chapter repeats its thesis too many times. By the “Monday” section, I already understood that conversations expose objections, narrow niches create actionable prospect lists, and direct sales does not scale through the founder. The Thailand section then restates the scalability qualification at considerable length.

“You are not too sophisticated for it; you are avoiding it” talked down to me. I may resist sales, but a three-column Trello board is genuinely inadequate for some products, especially when I’m tracking multiple stakeholders, security review, integrations, procurement, and long enterprise cycles.

“If your buyer definition cannot be turned into a hundred names, it is still too wide” is memorable but overconfident. A developer tool can have a precise buyer category while its users are pseudonymous, globally dispersed, or visible only inside companies. Enumerability may indicate a reachable niche, not necessarily a correctly defined buyer.

I also doubt the instruction to “find your single objection.” Sometimes there is a load-bearing objection; sometimes five objections represent five segments, or “switching cost” is a polite way of saying the product is not compelling enough. The chapter eventually admits the missing counter-case, but only after presenting the pattern as an operating rule.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I mainly noticed the repetition and the unusually practical sales advice. On the second, the source discipline mattered more: the author flags the date error, distinguishes contemporaneous evidence from retrospective method, identifies causal confounds, and explicitly names the absent failure case. That made me trust the author more, while trusting the universal-sounding prescriptions less.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promised pricing chapter addresses a decision where I want this same combination of numbers and skepticism.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Thailand and counter-case material by half and move their essential caveats beside the claims they qualify, so I encounter “single objection” and “direct sales scales” with their limitations immediately.
