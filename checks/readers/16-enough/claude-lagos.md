**1. Gut reaction after first read**

The core idea hit me hard — nobody has ever told me to write down what my life costs and make the business answer to it. But the whole thing is in euros, mortgages and pensions, and the example is a man in America earning $200K a year from a bookmarking site and calling it "enough." I read that number twice. In my world €45,000 a year is not a floor, it is a dream, and the arithmetic assumes a currency that stands still.

**2. What landed**

"A business has no opinion about how much money you need. It will absorb capital, hours and attention up to whatever limit you set, and if you set none it will find the limit experimentally — usually at the point where something in your life breaks." That is exactly what my business does. I restock, I boost posts, I answer DMs at 1am, and the limit I found was my health.

"Subtract what already covers it… leaving it out is how a business gets asked to carry a household it was never the only support for." I live with family and I do not pay rent yet. I had been measuring the business as if it must carry everything, and feeling like a failure. It is not carrying everything. That reframe alone was worth the chapter.

"A threshold rewritten in the month it is about to be tested is not a threshold, it is a mood." I have done this maybe five times. Fair hit.

And "count hours against a stated ceiling the same way you count euros against a floor" — my real shortage is hours, not naira.

**3. What didn't**

"Count the floor from twelve months of statements. Download the year, total the outgoings." I do not have twelve months of statements that mean anything. Half my sales come in cash or transfers to my personal account, mixed with family money, and — the bigger problem — what the household spent twelve months ago is not what it costs now. Inflation here has been brutal. Last year's outgoings, added up, tell me almost nothing about this year's floor. The chapter never says the word inflation once, and that is a big absence for a chapter about a number that is supposed to hold still for a year.

Same for the annual review. "Once a year, on a date chosen now" is a cadence for a stable economy. My cost of goods can move in three months. An annual review of my floor would leave me eight months behind.

"Pension payments your employer used to make, sick pay you no longer get, holiday you now fund yourself" — I have never had an employer. That whole line reads to someone who resigned from a corporate job in Europe.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read, I nearly dropped it at the euros and Pinboard. Second read I saw the chapter is honest about its own limits — "flat is cheap in some businesses and expensive in others," "one case, and it is the easy kind," and the admission that quiet failures don't publish anniversary posts. That honesty bought back a lot of trust. I also caught the distinction I'd skimmed: the labour charge and the household floor are two different tests that can each pass while the other fails. That's a real idea, and it works in any currency.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — the bottleneck chapter is my actual problem and I want to see if that one is written for someone without staff.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Say what to do when the currency moves. One paragraph: how to set an enough number, and how often to recount it, when last year's statements are not evidence about this year's costs — a quarterly recount of the floor, or the floor stated in stable-currency or in goods rather than in a fixed figure. Right now the whole mechanism rests on "last year's spending is a fact," and for me that is the one thing it is not.
