### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is unusually disciplined for a business book: it treats “enough” as an operating constraint, not a lifestyle slogan. I came away with a useful framework, although the chapter takes too long to extract it from one unusually favorable solo-SaaS example.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“A threshold rewritten in the month it is about to be tested is not a threshold, it is a mood.” That hit. I’ve moved goalposts on side projects—first revenue, then growth, then “strategic potential”—because admitting the original test failed felt worse than continuing. Requiring a threshold, consecutive periods, a deadline, and a precommitted consequence is concrete enough to use.

The distinction between a business that covers the household floor and one that covers the true labor charge is also strong. “Your household is fine and the business is quietly being subsidised by you” describes a lot of supposedly profitable developer businesses. A side project earning $80,000 can feel successful while paying badly for 1,500 senior-engineer hours.

“Time is the second ledger” worked for me too. My limiting resource is more likely to be evenings, attention, or tolerance for support work than cash. Keeping hours as a parallel ceiling makes the framework relevant to someone who already has a comfortable salary and isn’t forced to make the business pay the mortgage immediately.

Finally, “whether you are building a practice or an asset” is exactly the next question I’d want answered.

### 3. What didn’t

The Pinboard section is overextended. Once I understood “stable revenue can be the product,” I didn’t need every annual figure and then several paragraphs qualifying what could or couldn’t be inferred. “Two records, one sequence, and no arrow between them” is intellectually careful, but it also exposes how little the example proves.

More importantly, Pinboard is almost maximally unlike the business I might start: one person, low infrastructure cost, loyal users, no disclosed dependants, and revenue already well into six figures. The chapter eventually admits, “One case, and it is the easy kind,” but too late. I spent much of the case waiting for evidence from a consulting practice, developer tool with platform risk, or SaaS with employees.

“Almost every reader of this chapter has some” other income felt presumptuous. Plenty of founders are the sole household earner, and a partner’s salary is not automatically dependable business-risk capacity.

The Monday checklist is useful but repetitive. By then, the four-number model and annual review rule had already been explained several times.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I thought the chapter’s claim was mainly “choose how much money is enough.” On the second, I saw the more valuable claim: enough is a versioned decision with evidence requirements and an expiration date. I also noticed that the author explicitly acknowledges survivorship bias and says what would falsify the advice. That increased my trust, though it didn’t repair the thin evidence base.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the practice-versus-asset question is more consequential to me than Pinboard’s revenue history.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Pinboard narrative by half and replace that space with one worked example of a salaried senior developer evaluating a side SaaS: household floor, partner income, taxes, labor charge, hour ceiling, required revenue, review date, and miss consequence.
