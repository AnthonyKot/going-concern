### 1. Gut reaction after first read
It feels very pragmatic and grounded, entirely avoiding the American hustle culture that I find so naive and exhausting. But as someone whose baseline of safety was erased in 2022, the idea of cleanly calculating a "household floor" and confidently setting a "review date" feels like a luxury for people with stable passports and predictable futures. Still, the cold arithmetic appeals to my former professional life.

### 2. What landed 
The line "if you do not name it the business will name it for you" is exactly what I fear; my Dutch language classes, family obligations, and local bureaucracy already consume me, and I cannot afford a business that eats whatever time is left. I also deeply appreciated the strict reliance on reality: "budgets are aspirations and statements are evidence." In my old accounting career in Kyiv, this was a given, but it is comforting to see it demanded here. Calculating the gap between what my household needs and what other income (like my partner's part-time job) provides is the exact survival math I need to measure my risk.

### 3. What didn't 
The entire Pinboard case study felt alien and disconnected from reality. I almost stopped reading when I saw revenue numbers like "$117K, $178K... against funding of zero." This is a tech worker doing "artisanal SaaS stuff" who can casually take a "year of travel for a political organising project." If my business stalls, I cannot take a "reverse sabbatical"; I risk missing our Dutch rent and failing my parents. The author admits this is an "easy" case, but it made me feel the advice assumes a comfortable Western safety net I simply do not have. 

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
The first time, the software example made me defensive and ready to dismiss the chapter. The second time, I looked closer at the author's warning: "flat is cheap in some businesses and expensive in others." I realized that if I start a local service business here, Dutch taxes, inventory, and overhead mean a flat revenue line will actually slowly bankrupt me. The author wasn't just praising the tech company; he was illustrating a mechanism of control, and warning me to figure out my own specific mechanics.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because "You Are the Bottleneck" sounds like the exact trap I will fall into by trying to do absolutely everything myself to save precious capital.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Replace or supplement the Pinboard example with a traditional business (like a local consultancy or a shop) where the owner has actual fixed overhead and family dependents, because a zero-cost software project has no real-world stakes compared to signing a commercial lease.
