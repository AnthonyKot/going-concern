**1. Gut reaction after first read**

This one's about me, not about some app. The idea that the business will decide how much of my life it takes if I don't decide first — that's the first thing in this book that made me put the mug down. But it's a long chapter for what's basically one instruction, and there's a stretch in the middle where a man in America with a website I've never heard of gets more room than I do.

**2. What landed**

"A business has no opinion about how much money you need." That's true of restaurants I've run for other people — the building never once asked whether I'd had a day off.

"Naming it once. Worse, because it feels finished." I'd never have thought of that as the danger. I'd have thought naming it was the whole job.

The four numbers, laid out plain: floor, what else covers it, the throw-off, the gap. I've got a husband on a wage and a small pension coming. Nobody has ever told me to *subtract that* before working out what the café has to earn — "leaving it out is how a business gets asked to carry a household it was never the only support for." That's the sentence I'd write on the wall.

And Monday 1: "Not a budget, not an estimate you feel good about — what the household actually spent." I know exactly which of those I'd have done.

**3. What didn't**

Pinboard. Four long paragraphs, revenue figures for eleven years, "$117K, $178K, $181K, $175K, $193K, $160K and $234K" — I glazed over. One man, no staff, no stock, no rent, no gas bill, no chef walking out on a Friday. The author knows it and says so in the Boundaries — "near-zero marginal cost… A business with payroll, stock or premises has fixed costs that rise on their own." So why is he the whole example? Where's the café? That admission arrives after I've already been asked to sit through his numbers, and it half-undoes them.

Then "Time is the second ledger" — one short paragraph telling me to count hours, then straight to "That is why the money comes first." My hours are the thing most likely to break me. It gets a stub.

Small one: the money's in euros throughout and I'm in Yorkshire. €45,000, €70,000. Doesn't stop me reading, but it keeps telling me the book isn't for me.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I thought the four-numbers list was the chapter. Second time I saw it's the *review date* — "a threshold rewritten in the month it is about to be tested is not a threshold, it is a mood." That line does more work than the arithmetic.

Second read I also noticed "What would prove this chapter wrong" — the point about businesses that stayed small and got overtaken not writing anniversary posts. An author admitting his evidence is only from survivors buys back a lot of the trust the Pinboard section spent. I skimmed straight past it the first time; it should not be at the back.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — "whether you are building a practice or an asset" is the question I've been avoiding since I handed my notice in.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Replace half the Pinboard arithmetic with one worked example that has staff, stock and a lease — someone's floor, their partner's wage subtracted, the throw-off in actual money. Show me the five-line page filled in, not just described, because I don't know what "grossed up" looks like until I see a number under it.
