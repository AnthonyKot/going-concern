**1. Gut reaction after first read**
It sounds logical and safe, which I appreciate. I am tired of reading American business advice that assumes I have a huge budget to burn on ads just to "move fast and break things." However, reading about a guy selling bingo software to teachers in 2008 felt completely disconnected from my reality of trying to rebuild a serious professional life in Europe today.

**2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me**
The distinction between building an asset and renting traffic makes perfect sense to my accounting background. I immediately connected with this: "What accumulates rewards compounding effort and punishes only neglect; what is contemporaneous must be re-earned every month for the same result, spending the one resource a person alone cannot spare." With my temporary status and family obligations, my risk tolerance is near zero. I cannot afford to pay rent to Google or Facebook every month just to exist. If I spend my limited time, it must build a permanent foundation—like a client list or a reputation—that nobody can switch off. 

**3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation. Quote the spot**
I almost lost interest during the long breakdown of web traffic from 2009. But where I really felt the advice excluded me was here: "At $29.95 a stranger can decide alone in four minutes." If I start a financial or marketing consultancy here, nobody is hiring a foreign professional on an impulse. It requires trust, meetings, and proving I understand local regulations. The author admits this limitation—"The chapter's arithmetic does not survive a €12,000 sale"—but then he just moves on. He tells me what *doesn't* work for expensive services, but leaves me completely in the dark about what *does* work if you don't have thousands of people searching for you every day.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**
On the first read, I was frustrated that the author seemed to be pushing an outdated Google search strategy. On the second read, I paid more attention to the "Boundaries" section where he explicitly writes, "The tactic is closed... Nothing in this chapter recommends it." I realized I had confused the *example* with the *principle*. The lesson isn't about writing cheap articles for Google; it is about finding channels that accumulate value over time. That made the chapter actually useful to me, even if I still have to invent my own methods.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? One sentence.**
Yes, because the next chapter is called "Asking for Money," and learning how to confidently price my services as a newcomer in the Dutch market is my biggest anxiety right now.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change.**
Cut down the 15-year-old bingo software statistics and add at least one modern case study of a service-based business, because right now it reads too much like an internet history lesson instead of a practical guide for today.
