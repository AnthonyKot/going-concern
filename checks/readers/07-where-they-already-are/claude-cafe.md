**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I got through it, which surprised me. It's about a man selling bingo-card software to teachers on the internet, and I'm about to sell bacon sandwiches on a high street in Darlington, so I spent a good while wondering why I was reading it. But the two-kinds-of-channel idea — what are you left holding when you stop — I understood that straight off, and I'd not thought about it that way.

**2. What landed**

"Spend nothing on writing in March and you still have March's pages; spend nothing on AdWords and you have March's silence." That's the whole chapter in one line, and it's how I'd say it. I've watched owners pay for Facebook ads every month for years and have nothing to show for it the day they stop. Meanwhile a place I ran for eight years had a regulars' book — birthdays, kids' names — and that never stopped paying.

"It costs me about 24 cents to get a trial ... but only 2 cents to remind someone that their account still exists." Even I can do that sum. Reminding people you already know beats shouting at strangers. I know it from the floor; nice to see it in numbers.

"The question is not whether a channel earns its money but whether it earns the hours you must keep feeding it." Yes. I'm one person. Hours are the thing I'm short of, not opinions.

The Monday list is the best bit. "Write down where your last ten customers actually came from" — I can do that on the back of an order pad. And "if I stop this month, what do I still have in March?" is a question I'll actually use.

**3. What didn't**

"Trial → purchase 2.33% → 1.7%" and the whole section on the rate that fell for the right reason. I don't have a rate. Nobody in a café has a rate. I read it twice and it still felt like a spreadsheet argument for people who have spreadsheets.

"What your buyers search for, which twelve people they listen to" — my buyers don't search for anything. They walk past. The chapter half admits this in the Boundaries bit ("a business selling to four hundred logistics firms... has no long tail to harvest") but it's the logistics firms it thinks of, not me. I'm not in the book at all.

"Hello World attached to a random number generator" — no idea what that means and it's in quote marks like I should.

The Reading section with disputed figures to the penny — "$31,156.18 ... against $31,855.08" — I skipped. That's the author showing his working to other authors.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I took it as a chapter about the internet. Second time I noticed the author keeps saying the tactic is dead ("Nothing in this chapter recommends it") and that the four questions apply to anything. I tried them on the school-run mums at the primary across the road: right people, yes; gatekeeper, no; repeated, every day; cheap, free. That's a channel, and I'd never called it one. I also noticed the Appointment Reminder story is really the honest bit — the same clever man, same tricks, and it didn't work because the customers weren't looking. That stuck more than the success.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, because the next one's called "Asking for Money" and that's the bit I'm frightened of.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Put one non-internet example in the main body, not the footnotes — a shop, a trade, a café, anyone whose customers don't type — and run the four questions on it, so people like me don't have to do the translation ourselves.
