## 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is more rigorous than most business-book writing: one small software business, actual numbers, and explicit limits on what the evidence proves. The central distinction—channels that accumulate versus channels that disappear when spending stops—is useful, but the chapter takes too long to cash that insight and occasionally turns one founder’s old SEO history into more framework than the evidence can support.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“The question is not whether a channel earns its money but whether it earns the hours you must keep feeding it.” That’s the line I’d keep. As an engineer with a demanding job and finite side-project energy, hours are the real constraint; I can afford modest ad spend more easily than another recurring operational obligation.

“Owning an article is not owning its rank” also earned trust. The author catches the usual “owned media” sleight of hand: if Google controls discovery, I own an artifact, not distribution. That is exactly the kind of correction missing from most founder advice.

The falling conversion-rate section was concrete and credible. “Manage the product of ratio and volume” is a useful antidote to dashboard theater, and the 17,000-to-72,000 trial growth makes the argument testable rather than inspirational.

Finally, “the skill was never ‘distribution’—it was one channel in one market” felt true to my experience. Technical people routinely overgeneralize one successful playbook into a portable competency.

## 3. What didn’t

I got bored during the repeated restatement of accumulating versus contemporaneous channels. By “Spend nothing on writing in March…” I understood it; subsequent paragraphs mostly rephrased the same point.

“You have enough for two” is asserted too confidently. Two may be a good forcing function, but there’s no evidence here that two is generally optimal for someone with a full-time job, an enterprise product, or an existing professional network. For me, one serious channel might already be the realistic maximum.

The Monday instruction to “Pick the two you will practise for six months” also conflicts with “judge whether you got better, not whether it paid.” Skill improvement without business signal can become a sophisticated way to waste six months. I’d want predefined leading indicators and an exit criterion.

Appointment Reminder is interesting, but it doesn’t validate the framework because, as the author admits, McKenzie “did not do the work.” The “counter-case this chapter does not have” is admirably candid, yet it exposes the evidentiary gap: the chapter has one success, one non-test, and no failure that actually challenges the thesis.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I mainly retained “build assets, don’t rent traffic.” On the second, I noticed the author substantially weakens that slogan: SEO was still rented dependency, accumulating channels can be taken away, paid acquisition can sensibly run unattended, and the old tactic is explicitly closed. That nuance holds up better than the headline, although it also makes the binary framing feel oversold.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the evidence discipline and admissions of uncertainty are strong enough to outweigh the repetition.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut roughly a third of the middle exposition and replace it with a compact comparison of three modern cases—including one where a practiced accumulating channel failed—with costs, hours, leading indicators, and stop conditions.
