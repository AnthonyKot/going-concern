**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Halfway through I realised this chapter isn't written for me — it's written for someone hiring in the Netherlands. The task-or-job distinction is genuinely useful and I felt it land, but then a third of the page is Dutch statute quoted in Dutch, and I'm scrolling past it. The Galperin story is fine but he's at $19.5k MRR before his first hire; I'm nowhere near that and the chapter never says what someone below that does.

**2. What landed**

"The test is what happens if the person is not there on Monday. If a deliverable is late, you bought a task. If the day does not run, you have a job, whatever the paperwork says." That's the whole chapter in three sentences and I can run it today. I have a girl who packages and does drop-offs — if she doesn't show, my day does not run. I've been calling that "help." It's a job.

"the hours are worth more the longer the same person does them, so that buying them fresh each time throws away the part you were paying for" — this is exactly why I stopped using new dispatch riders. They don't know which customers accept calls and which only chat.

"A hire funded by the growth it is supposed to produce is a job bought on a task's reasoning" — I have made this mistake. I hired someone to "grow the page" and paid her from money the growth was meant to bring.

"the platform contractor who has covered the same shift for a year is a job wearing a task's contract, and the arrangement has already given you most of a job's dependence without any of its commitment" — the honesty of this is good. It names what I'm doing.

**3. What didn't**

The Dutch box. "De arbeidsovereenkomst is de overeenkomst waarbij de ene partij..." — I'm reading a business book and now there's a paragraph I literally cannot read. It says "If you are not hiring in the Netherlands, skip to Monday," which is honest, but then Monday itself says "on Dutch ground the box's presumption is written around exactly that pattern, and the form is a question for the lawyer before it is a question for you." So the skip doesn't work — the exit routes back in.

"Ask your accountant for the loaded monthly cost of one employee." I do not have an accountant. Nobody I know at my size has one. This is an instruction to spend money to find out whether I can spend money.

"Set it against the lowest four weeks of the last twelve months in your weekly cash figure." My lowest four weeks were lowest because of a naira slide and two weeks with no power for the steamer. Nothing to do with demand. In Lagos the worst month is not a demand signal — it's a currency and infrastructure signal, and the chapter treats the low month as if it were a fact about the business.

Also: "you probably had one some months before you noticed." Fine. But every worker here is off the books, paid weekly in cash, and no statute is coming for me. The chapter's whole enforcement half assumes a state that shows up.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second read I saw the chapter is honest about its own gaps — "One thing this case cannot tell you is where to hire first," and the whole "What would prove this chapter wrong" section admitting it can't find the counter-case. I trust the author more for that. I also caught that the first hire was someone who "used to write for us" — convert, don't recruit. First read I skimmed that as a detail; second read it's the most portable advice in the chapter, and cheaper than any of the arithmetic.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — the task/job test is worth the price of the Dutch parts, and chapter twenty is where the hard conversation lives.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Give the low-month test an escape hatch for people whose worst month was caused by currency or power, not customers: say plainly that you're testing the floor of *demand*, and if the low month was an outage or a devaluation, name the second-lowest and say why. And give the "ask your accountant" step a version for someone who doesn't have one — wage plus what percentage, roughly, so I can do it on my phone.
