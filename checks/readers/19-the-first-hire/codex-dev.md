### 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core idea is useful: don’t treat a recurring human obligation like another SaaS subscription. But the chapter turns one founder anecdote and a Netherlands-specific legal issue into a framework that feels more definitive than the evidence supports.

### 2. What landed — specific passages and why they worked for someone like me

“A hire funded by the growth it is supposed to produce is a job bought on a task’s reasoning” landed hardest. I’ve considered hiring for a side business based on the revenue that person would supposedly unlock; this sentence identifies the circularity cleanly.

“The wage is the small part of the cost. The obligation is the large part” also works. The loaded cost matters, but so do management time, reduced flexibility, and the psychological weight of making payroll. Those are exactly the parts founders tend to omit from spreadsheets.

I also liked the recommendation to “offer it first to the person you already buy it from.” Prior work is much better evidence than an interview loop, and the author correctly avoids pretending Galperin’s hiring order proves which role should come first.

### 3. What didn’t

The binary test—“If a deliverable is late, you bought a task. If the day does not run, you have a job”—is memorable but too neat. Plenty of employees own deliverables, while critical functions can legitimately be handled by agencies or rotating contractors. Dependency, continuity, control, and employment status are related questions, not one question.

The chapter begins with recurring work having “no judgment in it,” then uses content marketing and audience understanding as its main example. Those plainly require judgment. That mismatch made me doubt the framework.

As a US reader, I felt the Dutch legal box consumed too much of the chapter. I was explicitly told, “If you are not hiring in the Netherlands, skip to Monday,” which meant skipping a large, visually authoritative section and then encountering Dutch thresholds again in the action list. It made the chapter feel locally patched rather than written for me.

Finally, Gymdesk is thin evidence. Revenue rose after the hires, but nothing demonstrates causation or even that the first hire met the proposed financial test.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I appreciated the epistemic caveats more: the author admits the case cannot determine hiring order, labels Galperin’s account weak evidence, and explicitly asks what would prove the chapter wrong. That restraint holds up. What held up less was the repeated conversion of a useful heuristic into a rule; the more often the task/job distinction appeared, the more edge cases I saw.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the transition from making the hire to managing the person is consequential enough that I’d continue.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace the Monday binary with a short decision table covering continuity, accumulated context, managerial control, substitutability, financial runway, and jurisdiction; move the Dutch statutes to a localized appendix.
