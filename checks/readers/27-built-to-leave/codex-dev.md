## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core test—could a stranger run and understand this business without me?—is sharp and immediately relevant to someone like me who has considered turning consulting or a SaaS side project into something sellable. But the Black Hops story felt longer and less conclusive than the lesson it was carrying.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“What is written down, priced, and legible without you in the room” is the strongest line. In engineering terms, that distinguishes a system from a heroic maintainer: documentation matters, but so do observable numbers and explicit operating decisions.

“The failure is building the first while believing you are building the second” also worked. The practice-versus-asset distinction cuts through the usual business-book promise that every owner should build a scalable company. A lucrative practice may be exactly what I want; I just should not assign it a software-company valuation in my head.

The most useful exercise was giving the accounts to an experienced outsider “without asking me anything.” That is concrete, falsifiable, and uncomfortable in a productive way. I could actually do it.

I also respected the sentence, “The transfer was clean. What transferred was smaller than what had been priced.” It captures how operational independence and business quality are separate questions.

## 3. What didn’t

The chapter leans too hard on Black Hops without establishing the outcome that matters. It explicitly admits that “nothing here shows whether the business the manager took over survived being handed to him.” That leaves me with evidence of weak financial visibility and questionable crowdfunding valuation, not evidence that the business was or was not built to leave.

The narration of dates, titles, prior chapters, executive résumés, and competing profit measures became procedural. By “Dan Norris has been in this book three times,” I felt I was being asked to manage the book’s internal continuity instead of learning the principle.

Some advice is stated more confidently than the evidence supports: “If the list has one entry, or none, you have been absorbing costs.” Not necessarily. Automation, purchasing leverage, product mix, or deliberately lower margins could explain stable prices. Tell me to inspect gross margin and unit economics; do not infer the answer from price-change count alone.

The “What would prove this chapter wrong” section also rigs the test. A documented, founder-independent business can fail after transfer for dozens of reasons unrelated to hidden founder relationships. The proposed counterexample is too narrow to falsify the broader claim.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I appreciated that the author carefully avoids claiming Norris’s departure caused the loss and openly states the case’s limitations. I also noticed, though, that these caveats weaken the case more than the confident framing acknowledges. The chapter’s best argument is conceptual; the featured evidence mostly proves that outsiders can uncover accounting and pricing problems.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the practice-versus-asset distinction is useful enough that I would read the final chapter.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Black Hops narrative by a third and use that space for a worked example of an owner’s one-page handover, including the exact financial metrics an outsider should be able to reconstruct.
