### 1. Gut reaction after first read
I nearly shut the thing the minute it started wittering on about sixty-seven-million-dollar valuations and equity crowd-funding, because none of that has anything to do with opening a small café with redundancy money. But that opening bit about who takes over when you're ill stopped me cold—in twenty-five years of hospitality, I’ve seen too many places collapse the second the head chef or owner ended up in hospital with all the supplier knowledge locked in their head. It felt stiff and over-intellectual, but it struck an uncomfortable nerve.

### 2. What landed
* **The illness reality check:** *"whoever is standing there when you are ill — and on that day the business will be worth exactly what a stranger can run and read."* Having managed kitchens and front-of-house for decades, I know the panic when a manager goes down and nobody knows the alarm code, the prep quantities, or which dairy supplier does the Thursday delivery.
* **The pricing cowardice:** *"The business in six years just had one price increase."* and *"you have been absorbing costs... and the stranger will find it in the margin before you do."* That is the single biggest trap in food service; owners get scared of upsetting the regulars by adding 20p to a flat white while electric bills and bacon prices shoot through the roof.
* **The personality trap:** *"The beer transfers; the three blokes do not"*. If my café only works because I'm behind the counter charming the locals, I haven't built a viable trade; I've just bought myself an exhausting job that'll die when my knees give out.

### 3. What didn't
* **The startup jargon:** *"raised more than $2.2 million from 996 investors in a day at a valuation of more than $67 million... with an EBITDA the year before of $1.76 million."* That sounds like Silicon Valley nonsense or a TED talk; I don't care about corporate regulatory filings when I'm trying to figure out commercial kitchen lease terms and VAT thresholds.
* **The constant syllabus homework:** *"the last three years of chapter twenty-two's figures, the price history, chapter twenty-four's four agreements, chapter twenty-three's reserve count..."* It feels like being scolded by a schoolmaster for not having your study binder in order, which makes someone like me who hates paperwork want to throw the book across the room.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I felt defensive and annoyed, assuming the author was just another consultant writing for tech firms and corporate buyouts. On the second pass, I noticed where they admitted *"The limit of the case is its size"* and explicitly validated running a "practice" that just provides a solid, honest living rather than a sellable empire. The "Monday" instruction to hand my numbers to the shopkeeper two doors down to see what they can't make sense of went from sounding like an academic exam to a dead-simple, practical test I could actually do.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because despite the boardroom jargon, the underlying warning about not burying your head in the sand is too sharp to ignore when you're putting your life savings on the line.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Swap the multi-million-pound Australian brewery story for a normal high-street baker or sandwich shop, and strip out the endless references to past chapter numbers so it reads like straight advice rather than a training manual.
