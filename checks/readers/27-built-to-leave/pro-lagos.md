### 1. Gut reaction after first read
My first thought was, "Here we go again, another book about guys raising millions." I almost checked out when they started talking about $67 million valuations and corporate regulators because that is a completely different universe from selling clothes on Instagram in Lagos. But by the end, a genuine panic set in: if I get sick tomorrow, my business literally dies because it only exists in my head and my WhatsApp chat history. 

### 2. What landed
The reality check about pricing and absorbing costs hit hard: *"The business in six years just had one price increase."* In Nigeria, inflation and currency crashes are a daily reality; if I waited six years to raise prices, I would be bankrupt in three months. 

Also, the brutal truth of this line: *"the business will be worth exactly what a stranger can run and read. Not what you know about it."* Right now, my entire inventory, supplier list, and customer preferences are just me scrolling through my DMs and trusting my memory. If my phone gets stolen in traffic, my "asset" goes to zero. The distinction between a "practice" (buying myself a stressful job) and an "asset" (something that outlasts me) made me realize I've been lying to myself about what I'm building.

### 3. What didn't
The entire Black Hops case study felt alien. *"Black Hops at sixteen million dollars of revenue, with a board and a regulator and a crowd of shareholders, is not the reader's business..."* If the author knows it's not my business, why spend 70% of the chapter talking about it? I don't have a board, an external head of finance, or shareholders; I have a dispatch rider who stops picking up calls as soon as it starts raining. 

Furthermore, the Monday action item to *"Give the monthly page and the last two years' accounts to one person who has run a business... or the owner of the shop two doors down"* is a joke. This assumes a level of trust and formal business environment that doesn't exist in my market. You do not show your real numbers to strangers here unless you want your exact business model copied by next week or you want to be extorted.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I was too impatient and irritated by the corporate jargon (EBITDA, equity crowd-funding) to see the value. On the second read, I ignored the Australian brewery completely and focused on the mechanics of the "stranger's read." I realized that even at my tiny scale, the warning applies. I need to write down my supplier contacts, pricing margins, and standard responses on actual paper or a cloud doc, because right now my business has a catastrophic single point of failure: me.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
I would, but only because I'm desperate to see if they finally give practical steps on how to build these systems with zero budget, instead of just analyzing rich founders who messed up.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Scrap the massive brewery story and replace it with an example of a single-person, cash-strapped hustle that successfully documented its messy processes and transitioned into an asset, so readers at the bottom can actually see the bridge to the next level.
