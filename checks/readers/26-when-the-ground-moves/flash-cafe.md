### 1. Gut reaction after first read
I was ready to roll my eyes at another know-it-all manual, but that opening hit me right in the chest because I’ve lived through roadworks choking off trade before. The core point—stop panicking, don't immediately slash your staff or prices, and look at what your customers are actually dealing with—is bloody good common sense. But all the tech-firm acronyms nearly lost me halfway through, and it felt like a book written for people sitting behind laptops rather than someone wiping down tables and watching the till.

### 2. What landed
* **The opening diagnosis:** *"The road outside is dug up for a year... The shock is not yours, and the first mistake is to treat it as if it were"* rang completely true. In hospitality, when takings plummet from something outside, bad owners blame the chef or the waiting staff instead of looking out the front window.
* **Holding your nerve on cuts:** *"The cut, if it comes, comes on that date on the reading, not in week one on the fear."* That’s proper management. Knee-jerk panic—sacking your best barista or cheapening your ingredients the second trade dips—ruins a place before the storm even passes.
* **The honesty about survival:** Acknowledging that *"the vendor to the same gyms that did not survive has left no post"* made me trust the author. Most business books pretend their little frameworks guarantee you’ll survive, but this admitted you can do everything right and still go under if the road stays shut.

### 3. What didn't
* **Tech jargon and irrelevant models:** Stuff like *"consistent $1k+ MRR growth"* and *"churn from March to around August"* made my eyes glaze over. A one-man software outfit with no payroll or stock has nothing in common with a high-street café risking a bank loan.
* **Dismissive hand-waving at real businesses:** Saying *"the café whose office customers are at home may find they still eat lunch... here the book is guessing on your behalf"* felt lazy. Don't spend five pages pulling apart gym software and then give me a half-baked guess about the trade I actually work in.
* **Impractical advice for foot-traffic retail:** Telling me to *"Offer the pause before it is asked for"* makes sense for gym memberships or software subs, but you cannot put a flat white or a bacon sandwich on a "dated freeze." 
* **Constant homework references:** Being told every two paragraphs to check *"chapter twenty-two"*, *"chapter twenty-three"*, or *"chapter eleven's columns"* felt exhausting and patronising.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I assumed the author was holding up this gym software founder as some heroic genius we're all meant to copy. On the second read, I realized the author was actually skeptical of him—pointing out that the fellow's timeline was foggy, his vaccine check feature couldn't have existed in 2020, and there’s no proof his new features actually saved the business rather than gyms just reopening anyway. 

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Aye, I would, because despite the annoying tech examples and endless cross-referencing, there is a rare, no-nonsense honesty here that doesn't treat business like a fairy tale.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Stop leaning entirely on one-man software companies to prove your points, and give a proper, worked-out case of a physical high-street shop or kitchen dealing with actual rent, perishable stock, and wages.
