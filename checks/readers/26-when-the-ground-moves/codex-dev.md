## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The central reframing—an external shock happens to customers first, then propagates into your revenue—felt useful and immediately applicable. But the chapter stretches one founder’s retrospective into fairly confident operating advice, especially around delaying cuts, without enough evidence to justify that confidence.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“What can our customers no longer do, what can they still do, and what do they now need” is the strongest passage. As an engineer, I recognize this as debugging the upstream state change instead of blindly tuning the component showing the symptom. It also maps cleanly to a SaaS side business: interview customers about changed workflows before discounting or shipping the roadmap faster.

“Freeze before cancel” is concrete and commercially smart. A dated, reversible pause preserves the relationship without pretending current usage will recover immediately. I could actually implement that in billing and measure reactivation.

I also respected the caveat that Gymdesk’s recovery is “reported, not explained.” The author explicitly admits that gyms reopened independently, the churn rate is missing, and this is survivorship-biased founder testimony. That restraint earned more trust than the success story itself.

Finally, “the workaround with money in it” is memorable. Asking what customers did last week and what the workaround costs is much better than asking hypothetical willingness-to-pay questions.

## 3. What didn’t

“The cut, if it comes, comes on that date on the reading, not in week one on the fear” sounds cleaner than reality. Customer discovery, product adaptation, and reversible cost controls can run concurrently. Waiting until a calculated runway date may make later cuts deeper, especially when churn is still developing and revenue lags the shock.

“Build the smallest thing in the third column, within a month” also overreaches. Five interviews and a populated column do not establish that the proposed feature will retain customers or generate revenue. Gymdesk’s capacity limits and online training are plausible, but the chapter supplies no adoption, retention, or feature-level revenue data.

I got bored during the repeated qualifications about what the founder’s account does and does not prove. The intellectual honesty is good, but the same limitation is stated several times.

The chronology weakened my confidence: the vaccination-check feature is acknowledged as not being from 2020, while vaccines are described as “starting to become available” as 2021 closed. That timeline feels muddled and makes the case harder to audit.

The many references to earlier chapters also made the advice feel dependent on a private vocabulary I had to reconstruct.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I mainly retained the customer-first framing and the pause mechanism. On the second, I noticed how much of the prescriptive framework—the ordering of discovery before cuts, proactive freezes, and the one-month build deadline—is the book’s invention rather than something demonstrated by Gymdesk. I also appreciated more that the author labels that boundary, even while occasionally writing past it.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because the framework is useful enough to continue, though I’d keep separating evidence from the author’s extrapolation.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace the blanket “cuts wait” rule with a decision table covering runway, shock reversibility, leading indicators, and reversible versus irreversible cuts, then show how Gymdesk’s known numbers would populate it.
