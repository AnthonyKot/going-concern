### 1. Gut reaction after first read
When I saw the title "When the Ground Moves," my mind went straight to February 2022, so reading about a software founder's gym clients closing in 2020 felt almost trivial at first. However, the tone surprised me: it was calm, cold, and completely free of the toxic "hustle and pivot" cheerleading I usually hate in business books. It treated external crisis as a concrete operational problem rather than an inspirational test of character.

### 2. What landed
The sober financial discipline resonated with my background and my current need to protect every euro.
- *"The cut, if it comes, comes on that date on the reading, not in week one on the fear."* Having lived through sudden upheaval, panic makes you want to freeze or slash everything immediately; anchoring cost cuts to a calculated runway date offers real, steadying control.
- *"Take chapter thirteen's cash — the bank balance less what is held for the tax authority..."* Coming from an accounting background and navigating the Dutch system (*Belastingdienst*), I appreciated that it explicitly separates tax obligations from usable cash before calculating survival time.
- *"...the vendor to the same gyms that did not survive has left no post."* The author’s direct admission of survivorship bias made me trust them; they aren't pretending this case is a guaranteed formula.

### 3. What didn't
The reliance on a solo software developer makes the advice feel detached from the reality of running a low-margin, physical small business.
- *"...there was, by the same document, almost nothing to cut... Gymdesk had no reserve of either kind to hold in 2020 except its owner, and the post shows that one being spent..."* For someone with family obligations, limited savings, and temporary legal status in the Netherlands, "spending the owner" is not a viable strategy. Burning through personal health and time with no margin for error is a luxury for young solo founders without dependents.
- *"The same move should exist for a business with no software in it, and here the book is guessing on your behalf: the café whose office customers are at home may find they still eat lunch..."* Admitting the book is "guessing" here felt dismissive. A physical business has fixed Dutch commercial rent, inventory waste, and strict local municipal regulations—you cannot simply reconfigure lunch service overnight the way a developer writes code for capacity limits.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On my first read, I felt alienated by another tech-founder case study and assumed the author was glorifying a standard startup survival story. On the second read, I realized the author is actually stripping away the founder's self-congratulatory narrative, repeatedly noting that the evidence is weak and that the only thing worth keeping is the disciplined sequence: observe the customer, offer a pause, check the cash runway, and decide without panic.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because its strict, unromantic method for measuring financial runway respects my low tolerance for risk far more than standard business advice.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Replace the speculative café and tradesman examples with a real, documented case of a non-software, low-margin business managing a shock, including actual cash numbers rather than admitting the book is guessing.
