**1. Gut reaction after first read**

The opening got me — "you have started arranging your week so that you do not have to say so." I've done that. I've done it three times for other people's businesses and once I did it for eleven months. But by the time I hit the Dutch legal box I was completely lost, and I mean lost in the sense of "am I even the person this was written for."

**2. What landed**

"The month is not a plan. It is what an owner does when the decision has been made and the conversation has not, and it feels like fairness — they deserve more time — while actually being the opposite." That is exactly it, and nobody's ever put it to me that way. I always thought I was being kind.

The forcing function idea, done plainly: "it is not you who decides that this is the week, but the calendar." I hate spreadsheets but I understand a date in a diary. That's a thing I can actually do on Monday.

"Early on, I used to wait until the trial period was over to give feedback. This helped nobody." True of every manager I've worked under, including me.

And the honesty of "'The conversation' doesn't get any easier. Not the third time, the fifth time or the tenth time." Every book I've ever half-opened promises it gets easier. This one didn't lie to me.

The bit about fit being "the word under which an owner's discomfort with someone unlike them gets recorded as a finding" — that's the sharpest thing in the chapter. In hospitality, "not a fit" gets said about a lot of people who are just not like the boss.

**3. What didn't**

The Dutch box. Half a page of Dutch statute I can't read, quoted rather than translated, and I'm in Yorkshire. It says "If you are not employing in the Netherlands, skip to Monday" — but the box is the biggest thing on the page, and Move 3 keeps pointing at it ("it is the box's", "on Dutch ground the date is only partly yours"). By the end I felt like a guest at someone else's chapter. I've got real law of my own — statutory notice, two years before unfair dismissal bites, an employment contract I'm supposed to give in eight weeks — and this chapter doesn't tell me it exists or that I should go find it.

Then Monday says "on Dutch ground call the lawyer before you say it" — three times. Nothing tells me whether *I* need one. I'd have read that as "you don't," which would be wrong.

Second thing: the evidence is one bloke, a software company, remote hires, "a paid consulting gig." I can't run a four-week paid trial on a barista before I employ her; I need someone on the coffee machine Saturday morning or I lose the trade. The chapter half-admits this by saying the trial isn't the Dutch trial period, but never says what the shape becomes for someone hiring hourly staff who start next week.

Also the sentence about where the date goes — "and whether you set it when you hire, while you still can, or leave it to be set by the week you finally cannot stand it" — I had to read three times. It's showing off.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I thought the Dutch box was an appendix I could ignore. Second time I saw the argument actually runs through it — "told in good time, given a real chance to improve" is the reason the feedback rule matters, and it's buried in a language I don't read. Also second time I noticed "What would prove this chapter wrong" admits the evidence only shows one side. I respect that more than I expected to. It stopped it sounding like a TED talk.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — for the Monday list and that line about the month, and because "Meetings That Aren't Theater" is a title I'd have written myself.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Put one paragraph before the box for the rest of us: "If you employ people outside the Netherlands, the shape holds but the law doesn't — before the conversation, find out what your own country requires by way of notice, warning, and written record, and budget an hour with an employment solicitor." Otherwise Monday item 5 quietly tells everyone outside Holland they can just say it.
