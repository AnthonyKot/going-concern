## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core idea is useful: delaying a people decision burns management attention, and a precommitted review date can force clarity. But the chapter stretches one founder’s anecdote into a general operating rule, then buries it under a long Netherlands-specific legal section that has little relevance to me.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“The month is not a plan” landed hardest. I’ve absolutely watched managers—including me—grant another month because initiating the conversation felt worse than absorbing the ongoing drag. Describing the hidden cost as “managing your own discomfort about a sentence you have not said” is sharp and accurate.

The instruction to record “what was asked, what was done, what it cost” is also genuinely useful. It converts vague “fit” concerns into observable work and provides a check against affinity bias. Likewise, “act on the trend, not the last one” captures why contemporaneous notes beat reconstructing a performance narrative from memory.

I appreciated the warning that early slowness may be “the transfer happening, not failing.” That distinction matters in engineering, where capable senior hires can need months to understand the architecture, incentives, and organizational boundaries.

## 3. What didn’t

“A trial of two to four weeks judges whether the person and the work suit each other” is far too confident. Two weeks can reveal communication habits and obvious dysfunction; it cannot reliably reveal whether someone can navigate a large codebase, build trust across teams, or deliver a six-month technical initiative. Strong senior candidates with good jobs also won’t accept a temporary consulting audition, as Turnbull himself partly acknowledges.

The claim that “the expensive mistake…is not the wrong hire” overreaches. A wrong hire at staff or management level can distort architecture, hiring, and team culture well beyond the cost of delayed action.

The Dutch-law box stopped the chapter cold. I understand why it exists for the book’s jurisdiction, but several screens of untranslated statutory language felt like source notes pasted into the main argument. As a US reader, being told to “skip to Monday” effectively meant skipping a substantial portion of the chapter.

Finally, one 2015 founder post from a small helpdesk company cannot carry this much prescription. The chapter admits the evidence is weak, but only after repeatedly presenting the “fixed date” as the remedy.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I noticed more restraint than I initially credited: the warning about bias disguised as “fit,” the distinction between a decision date and a dismissal date, and especially “What would prove this chapter wrong.” That section openly identifies the missing counterfactual evidence. It improved my trust in the author, but also made the preceding certainty feel less earned.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the practical framing is strong enough to continue, though I’d remain skeptical of claims generalized from one founder story.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace the long statutory box with a short jurisdiction warning and use the space for two contrasting cases—one where a fixed early date prevented drift, and one where a slow-starting hire succeeded after three to six months.
