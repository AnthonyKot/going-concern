## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core idea landed: if I’m the person absorbing every surprise, the business doesn’t actually have slack—it has an unpriced dependency. But the chapter takes a long, somewhat circular route to a fairly compact argument, and the WP Curve evidence doesn’t fully support the confidence of the prescription.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“You are the reserve” is the sharpest line in the chapter. I’ve seen exactly this in engineering teams and in my own side-business experiments: the system appears efficient because one experienced person quietly handles escalations, weekends, and undocumented edge cases. Calling that capacity “unpriced, unsized, and spent first” makes the hidden cost concrete.

“Reserve is sized, named, and bought in units you can stop” also worked for me. That’s operationally useful. It separates deliberate headroom from vague overstaffing and addresses my immediate objection: unused salaried capacity can become an expensive permanent commitment.

The caveat that “the fifteen-to-$23,535 ratio…is not a benchmark for anything” earned credibility. Too many business books would turn one offshore WordPress support company’s staffing ratio into a universal formula.

The strongest practical exercise is the two-week absence drill: “write down what broke, in order, and put a monthly cost beside each line.” That produces evidence I could use to decide whether I need documentation, a contractor, cross-training, or actual additional capacity.

## 3. What didn’t

I got bored during the long reconstruction of WP Curve’s June 2014 post. Details such as affiliates, blog images, Xero bookkeeping, and the “4 hour work week, baby!” aside consume a lot of space without proving the central claim.

The chapter repeatedly implies a connection between rapid hiring and Happy % falling from 96% to 82%, then carefully admits the evidence does not establish one: it is “the only figure on the page that could have shown it, which is not the same as showing it.” That’s intellectually honest, but it leaves the case doing less work than the surrounding prose suggests. The decline might reflect request mix, measurement noise, customer selection, onboarding problems, or something else entirely.

“The reserve is the one unit beyond that pace” also feels asserted rather than derived. Why one person? A Bay Area engineer may cost $300K fully loaded; a contractor may take months to acquire system context. The right reserve depends on probability, recovery time, skill substitutability, margins, and contractual consequences—not merely one increment beyond current demand.

Finally, “take [the fortnight]” is unrealistic for many owner-operated businesses unless the chapter first distinguishes a controlled drill from abandoning customer obligations.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I thought the chapter was making a stronger empirical claim than it had earned. On the second, I noticed how explicitly it limits itself: “one month of reserve being built and no month of it being drawn on.” That restraint improved my view of the author, but also made the mismatch clearer—the case illustrates delegation and growth strain, not that the proposed reserve survives a shock.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because the framework is useful enough to continue, though I’d want tighter evidence and fewer callbacks.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the WP Curve section by half and replace that space with a worked example calculating reserve options—employee, contractor, cross-training, or owner time—using cost, time-to-stop, recovery time, and the expected loss from a named shock.
