**1. Gut reaction after first read**

The opening got me — "every hour sold, every person busy, the calendar full three weeks out and you proud of it" — that's the shift rota I've built a hundred times and been pleased with. Then it went off to a website company in Australia with fifteen developers in the Philippines and I stopped seeing myself in it. By the end I could tell there was a good idea buried in there but I'd had to dig for it with a spoon.

**2. What landed**

"You already know the failure from the inside, because you are the reserve. When someone is off, you cover. When the order doubles, you work the weekend." That's twenty-five years of my working life in three sentences and nobody's ever put it to me that way. I've always thought of it as being a good manager. It's not — it's the business having no other way to cope.

The waste-versus-reserve line: "An idle afternoon nobody owns is waste. The same afternoon, held so that the one person who knows the supplier can be away for a fortnight without the business stopping, is a reserve, and the difference is entirely in whether it was decided." That's clean. That I could explain to my sister.

And Monday item 5 — put a fortnight in the calendar and take it, write down what broke, in order, price each line. That's a real thing I could do that would tell me something I don't already know. I liked that the author admits he can't prove the chapter: "the honest guess at why it is hard to find is that a reserve which failed looks, afterwards, like a cost that should never have been carried." Nobody's tried to sell me anything there.

**3. What didn't**

The case study. Not one line of it is my world. "24 / 7 unlimited WordPress small fixes for $69 / month" — I had to read that twice to know what they sold. Then MRR, ARR, "$20,833 / month", "57 net customer increase", "another month of data from Hively". I don't know what Hively is and the author doesn't say. The author does eventually admit the numbers don't transfer — "a flat-fee support business staffed largely in the Philippines has a cost per person yours almost certainly does not" — but that's buried near the end of Move 3, after I've already waded through it.

And the prose fights me. "Reserve is bought in units you can stop buying, funded from revenue that already repeats, and sized against a shock you have named; capacity bought in any other way is a bet on demand, and chapter one already priced that." I know what it means now. I didn't the first time.

The chapter cross-references are a thicket — one, ten, fourteen, sixteen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty-two, twenty-eight. That paragraph beginning "Two other chapters own the edges" told me nothing except that other chapters exist.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the actual argument, which is that the 82% happy score and the record revenue month are the same event and the company didn't notice. First read I skimmed past that as more numbers. It's the sharpest thing in the chapter and it's sitting in the middle of a paragraph, unlabelled. I also realised the "hire one more than the pace needs" instruction is the author's, not something the company did — the text does say so, but it takes two reads.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but I'd skim the case study, and I'd resent having to.

**6. One concrete thing to change**

Open Move 2 with one plain sentence — my chef is off for a month, or my Saturday doubles — before you go anywhere near WordPress and MRR. Give me my own version first, then use theirs as the evidence. As it stands you spend your best goodwill, earned in Move 1, on a company I can't picture.
