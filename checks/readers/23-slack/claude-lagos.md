# Report — Lagos, 24, clothing business on Instagram/WhatsApp

**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I understood the idea in the first two paragraphs and I liked it — hold back capacity on purpose, because right now the only spare capacity in my business is me, and I'm already spent. Then the whole middle became a company with fifteen staff and $23,535 a month, and I stopped being in the room. By the end I had one useful sentence and a lot of somebody else's monthly report.

**2. What landed**

"You already know the failure from the inside, because you are the reserve. When someone is off, you cover. When the order doubles, you work the weekend. When the process breaks, you are the process." That is my life exactly. Nobody has written it that plainly for me before — my tailor goes to the village for a burial, and there is no business until she comes back.

"An idle afternoon nobody owns is waste. The same afternoon, held so that the one person who knows the supplier can be away for a fortnight without the business stopping, is a reserve, and the difference is entirely in whether it was decided." That is a real distinction and I can use it tomorrow. My aunt would call my idle Tuesday laziness; now I can tell her what it's for.

"Reserve is bought in units you can stop buying" — this is the sentence I'd keep. In Lagos you never commit to anything you can't put down in a month. Inflation eats a fixed cost you signed in January by June.

Monday item 1 — the three sentences, "write what would break first" — costs nothing and I can do it on my phone tonight.

**3. What didn't**

The case. Four long paragraphs of "MRR grew by 22% to $23,535", "team to 15 people", "90% of the Xero bookkeeping", "hiring every 2 weeks". I don't have a team, an admin in the Philippines, Xero, affiliates, or a blog. The author sees this and half-admits it — "a flat-fee support business staffed largely in the Philippines has a cost per person yours almost certainly does not" — but that one line doesn't rescue three pages built on a company nothing like mine.

Monday item 5: "Put the fortnight in the calendar this month, with a date, and take it." Two weeks. Away. This month. My customers DM me at 11pm and buy from the next girl if I don't reply by morning. There is no version of my business where I disappear for fourteen days and come back to it. Give me the two-day version first and I'll believe you.

Monday item 3 offers "a contractor on a standing arrangement" — a standing arrangement means a retainer, and a retainer means cash I don't have, in a currency losing value while it sits. The chapter says reserve is funded "from revenue that already repeats." Mine doesn't repeat; it spikes at Christmas and Sallah and dies in between. Seasonal, not steady, is my whole shape, and it isn't here.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read, I thought the case was the chapter. Second read, I saw the argument is actually in Move 3 and Monday, and the case is just illustration — and honestly it holds up better than I gave it credit for. I also caught "Reserve is spent silently" the second time, which I'd skimmed past: my December hires get eaten the same week I hire them. That's the truest thing here. And I noticed the author admits the case never shows a reserve being *tested* — I respect that, but it also means I'm reading three pages of a thing that proves nothing.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — the argument is real even when the example isn't mine.

**6. One concrete thing to change**

Replace half the WP Curve report with one owner-operator who has no staff: where does a one-person business hold reserve, and what does the drill look like at two days instead of two weeks?
