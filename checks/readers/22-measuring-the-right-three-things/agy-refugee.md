**1. Gut reaction after first read**
I felt a mix of professional recognition and deep irritation. The core accounting logic made sense to my background, but using an American software startup making over a million dollars a month as the main example felt completely disconnected from my reality of trying to start a modest, safe business here in the Netherlands. I almost tuned out when the Silicon Valley jargon started flowing.

**2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me**
The psychological advice about not lying to yourself resonated strongly. "A figure that needs you to explain it is one more thing that only you can do" is brilliant; it reminds me that a real business must eventually survive without my constant micromanagement, which is vital since I have family obligations to juggle. I also deeply appreciated the rule to "fix the definition before anyone is judged by the figure, in writing." In my past corporate life in Kyiv, I saw how easily managers manipulate definitions to make bad months look good. For a low-margin business where I absolutely cannot afford to lose my limited savings, forced honesty is survival.

**3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation. Quote the spot**
The entire extended example about ConvertKit alienated me. When the author quotes, "Because our beginner customers churn more often than our larger customers, user churn at ConvertKit is always higher than revenue churn," I felt excluded. I am looking to build a local service business where losing a customer means an empty chair or unsold inventory, not "user churn" on a software dashboard. The American software-as-a-service model assumes high scale and abstract metrics, which is the exact opposite of my cautious, physical reality on a temporary protection visa. It felt like the kind of blind hustle optimism I try to avoid.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**
The first time, the tech jargon ("MRR", "quick ratio") distracted me so much I missed the author's actual framework. On the second read, I realized the author explicitly dismisses those complex software metrics for normal readers: "Net revenue against recurring is a distinction a subscription business needs and most of this book's readers do not." I saw that the core triad—movement, loss, and concentration—is actually very practical and maps perfectly to a traditional, boring business, even if the case study doesn't. 

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? One sentence.**
Yes, because Chapter 23 is titled "Slack," and with my current life situation, I desperately need to know how to build breathing room into a business.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**
Cut the lengthy ConvertKit quotes and instead use an example of a normal, non-digital business—like a local accounting firm or a wholesale bakery—to show exactly how "movement, loss, and concentration" apply to someone whose revenue doesn't come from automated monthly credit card subscriptions.
