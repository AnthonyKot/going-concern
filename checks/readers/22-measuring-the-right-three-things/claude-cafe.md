**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Halfway through I forgot this was meant to be for me. It's a long chapter about a bloke who sells email software to bloggers, and I run a café — well, I will do, in about six weeks. The bit that made me sit up was near the end: don't let the person who gets paid more when the number goes up be the one reading the number out. That's the only sentence I'd have written on my hand.

**2. What landed**

"A figure that needs you to explain it is one more thing that only you can do." That's the whole problem of me stepping back, put plainly. I've watched managers hand a staff member a takings sheet and the poor lad just nods.

"Losing five customers is one thing if they were the five smallest and another if one of them was the account that paid for the shop's rent." I know exactly who that is for me already — it'll be the two offices that order lunch in. And I like that the chapter says count customers lost *and* money lost as two separate numbers. Nobody's ever told me that; I'd have counted one.

"What counts as a lost one and on which day" — the definitions page, dated, changed only with a note saying when and why. That's the most useful practical instruction in the chapter and it costs nothing to do. Same with "A number nobody had to falsify. They only had to define it." I've seen that with wastage sheets. Nobody lied. The definition of wastage just got helpful.

And the last Monday item — after a quarter, write down what surprised you, and if none of the three moved first, they're the wrong three. That's the author admitting his own three might not be mine, and it made me trust him more, not less.

**3. What didn't**

Move 2 is four hundred words of somebody else's business and I nearly stopped. "Quick Ratio = (New MRR + Expansion MRR) / (Churned MRR + Contraction MRR)" — I don't need this, and the chapter tells me two pages later that I don't need it. Why put it in front of me at all? "Buffer at 1.08 and Hubstaff at 1.38" means nothing to me and reads like showing off.

"Net revenue against recurring is a distinction a subscription business needs and most of this book's readers do not." Good — but then take it out of the chapter, don't parade it and then excuse it.

And the concentration figure. My worry isn't three big customers, it's that half my trade is people walking past on a Tuesday and I won't know a single one of their names. "The share of the week's money from the top customers as you defined them in item one, with their names" — I can't. I'll have a card machine and a till. The chapter is honest that it's a compression of Barry's seven, but it never quite admits that a café has an anonymous till and no account names.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I thought the ConvertKit section was the chapter. Second time I saw it's just evidence, and the actual argument is Move 3, which is short and good. I also caught the November annual-plans bit properly — one decision, three numbers moving three ways, nobody lying. That's the point of the chapter and I sailed straight past it first time because I was drowning in MRR.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — I'd skim Move 2 and read the rest twice.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Give the three numbers once in cash-business terms before you give them in ConvertKit's — new against returning, customers lost and money lost, and for a walk-in trade something I can actually count as concentration (biggest day part? standing orders? the office accounts?). Then let Barry be the illustration instead of the entry.
