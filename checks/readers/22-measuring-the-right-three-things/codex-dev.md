### 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core idea is good: totals are lagging summaries, and definitions matter more than polished dashboards. But the chapter takes a long, SaaS-heavy route to arrive at something I’d summarize as “track movement, loss, and customer concentration—and define them before incentives corrupt them.” I also don’t quite believe these are literally three numbers.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“A figure that needs you to explain it is one more thing that only you can do” landed hardest. That’s exactly how founder dependency shows up in engineering and business operations: the dashboard exists, but the real interpretation layer is still one person’s undocumented context.

The annual-plan example was concrete and useful: “net revenue up, MRR down, churn up and then down again.” I’ve seen this kind of metric disagreement after pricing changes, migrations, and enterprise contract timing. It demonstrates why a metric can be calculated correctly and still produce a misleading story.

“A number nobody had to falsify. They only had to define it” is memorable without being cute. The advice to date definitions and change them publicly is operationally sound—basically version control for business semantics.

I also appreciated the admission that the chapter could be wrong: the three metrics might miss correlated customer risk or declining acquisition quality. Business books rarely specify their own failure condition.

### 3. What didn’t

I got bored during the long ConvertKit inventory: quick ratio, Buffer comparison, Hubstaff comparison, ARPU, customer split. Much of it is later discarded, so it reads like research notes the author couldn’t bear to cut.

The title promises three things, but “customers lost and money lost, as two numbers” already makes loss two things. Movement is also “new against returning,” concentration requires a percentage plus customer names, and cash remains weekly. In practice this is a compact dashboard, not three numbers. The forced neatness made me trust the prescription less.

The non-subscription accommodation—“the breakout is coarser and still exists”—is too brief. My hypothetical side business might involve projects, consulting, usage billing, or a marketplace, where weekly churn is meaningless or dangerously noisy.

I also doubt “the person should be someone the numbers do not pay.” In a two-to-five-person company, nearly everyone’s week improves when revenue, retention, or concentration improves. Independence sounds desirable, but the chapter doesn’t identify a realistic role.

Finally, “a person reading seven numbers aloud is reading none of them” talks down to the reader. Seven well-designed metrics are not inherently incomprehensible; the issue is whether they drive decisions.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I saw a good principle buried under an overlong case study. On the second, I noticed how carefully the author limits the claim: Barry did not endorse three metrics, the evidence is a founder post, cadence should follow the operating cycle, and quarterly surprises are explicitly used to invalidate the selection. Those caveats increased my trust, though they also weakened the confident “right three” framing.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because the author is unusually willing to state where the evidence ends.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace half the ConvertKit walkthrough with one worked non-subscription example showing the exact three definitions, one Monday reading, and the decision each number triggered.
