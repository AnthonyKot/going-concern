### 1. Gut reaction after first read
It reads very dry and analytical, like looking at an accounting spreadsheet rather than a business book about people. I appreciate the lack of empty "you can do it!" cheering, which usually annoys me in American business books, but the intense focus on one American coding school felt far removed from the small, practical business I might actually start here in the Netherlands. Still, the core message about not ignoring your silent failures made my practical brain nod in agreement.

### 2. What landed
The idea of actively tracking people who vanish really resonated with me. The passage, "Of everything in these reports, the line worth stealing is 'Could Not Contact' ... It is a published count of the people who went silent," made perfect sense. As someone who has had to rebuild life from scratch and watch every euro carefully, I know that ignoring bad news doesn't make it disappear; it just makes you vulnerable. If I start a small service business, I cannot afford to live in a fantasy where a quiet customer is a happy customer. Furthermore, the quote "Naming them as their own line is the cheapest integrity move" appeals to my current lack of capital—building integrity that costs nothing but a spreadsheet column is exactly my kind of strategy.

### 3. What didn't
I completely tuned out during the endless breakdowns of percentages about the Turing School. "Employed in-field fell from 80.6% to 63.1%, while still-seeking rose from 9.0% to 26.2%." I got bored here because it felt like reading someone else's audit report for a high-priced tech bootcamp, which has zero connection to the kind of modest, low-risk local business I would run to support my family. Additionally, the line "A school is an unusually measurable business. The outcome is a job — dated, binary, verifiable" made me doubt how applicable this all is to me. If most businesses aren't like this, why did the author spend the entire chapter analyzing one that is? It felt like an academic exercise disconnected from the messy reality of starting out. 

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
During the first read, I was frustrated by the heavy focus on the coding school's data and almost dismissed the chapter. But on the second read, I forced myself to look past the specific percentages and noticed the structural advice much more clearly, especially the "Monday" checklist at the very end. I realized the author isn't expecting me to run a school; they are just using it to prove that you can measure outcomes if you strip away the excuses. The instruction to write down "By [date], this customer will be able to [observable thing]" suddenly clicked as a highly useful, grounding exercise for someone trying to figure out what they are actually selling in a new country.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because the author treats business like a realistic mechanism to be managed rather than a dream to be chased, which suits my cautious mindset right now.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Shorten the tech-school data dump and provide a parallel example of a mundane, everyday small business—like a local cleaner or a freelance bookkeeper—so ordinary people trying to build something basic can see how this strict outcome-tracking actually looks without a dedicated admissions department.
