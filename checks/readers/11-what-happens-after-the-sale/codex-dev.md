### 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core distinction—payment, use, completion, and outcome—felt obvious once stated, but useful enough that I immediately mapped it onto software products I’ve worked on. The chapter earned some trust by publishing awkward denominators and limitations, though it spent too long extracting one lesson from one coding school.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“Quiet reads like contentment in every system you are likely to have built” is painfully accurate. In SaaS, dashboards are full of active accounts, renewals, and ticket counts, while customers who quietly abandon the workflow disappear without creating an event. I’ve seen teams interpret low support volume as satisfaction when it really meant disengagement.

“Count from the population that paid, not the population that finished” also landed. That is a crisp warning against survivor-biased metrics, and it applies directly to onboarding funnels, enterprise rollouts, courses, and consulting engagements.

The strongest practical idea was giving “Could Not Contact” its own line. That is cheap, implementable, and harder to game than a satisfaction survey. Likewise, defining success “with a date and a test in it” is the kind of constraint I could use when evaluating a side business.

I also respected the restraint around 2020: “the labour market is the obvious candidate” followed by an explicit refusal to claim causation. That made the author sound more credible than the usual business-book case-study narrator.

### 3. What didn’t

The Turing example was overworked. After the graduation and employment numbers established the distinction, sections like “Who is allowed to count as a success” and “Hold the delivery still” mostly restated it. I started skimming.

The line “If you cannot write it, you do not know what you are selling, and neither does the buyer” is too absolute. Plenty of valuable products—developer tools, management coaching, security platforms—produce several contingent outcomes. A buyer may know exactly why they’re purchasing without accepting one dated, binary result.

I also doubted the claim that the three reporting practices “are free.” For twenty low-value customers, repeated follow-up, classification, and interpreting outcomes costs founder time and may annoy buyers. The chapter later admits that possibility, but the Monday checklist treats measurement as nearly frictionless.

Finally, “Ask the five most recent leavers” fits subscriptions, but not every small business has identifiable “leavers.” That advice feels shaped around recurring services while pretending to be universal.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I noticed how much qualification the author had included in “Boundaries” and “The counter-case this chapter does not have.” Those sections softened several objections I had formed earlier and increased my trust. But they also exposed the chapter’s structural weakness: the caveats arrive after the broad prescriptions, and the missing counter-case is exactly the evidence I wanted before accepting them.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the framework is useful enough, and “Reading Your Own Business” sounds closer to the decisions I’d actually face.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Turing analysis by a third and use that space for a contrasting B2B software or consulting example where the outcome is diffuse, jointly caused, and expensive to measure.
