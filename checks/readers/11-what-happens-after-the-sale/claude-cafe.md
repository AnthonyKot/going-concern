**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I'll be honest, when it opened on a software school in Denver I nearly put it down. I'm opening a café, not a coding bootcamp. But by the middle I'd stopped noticing that, because the idea underneath is one I've lived: the customers who go quiet are the ones you've lost, and nobody ever counts them. Twenty-five years of managing and I never once saw a manager track that.

**2. What landed**

"They do not complain. They go quiet, and quiet reads like contentment in every system you are likely to have built." That's exactly it. A table that never comes back doesn't send a letter. I've watched owners look at a full Saturday and think all's well while the Tuesday regulars drifted off.

"Count from the population that paid, not the population that finished. Everything else is arithmetic performed on survivors." Short, plain, and I'll remember it. For me it's: everyone who walked in, not everyone who ordered a second coffee.

The "Could not contact" bit — "the cheapest integrity move available to a business with twenty customers. It costs nothing but a column." I liked that the author's honest that these people simply went missing, rather than pretending to know why. And "Ask the five most recent leavers one question. 'What were you hoping would be different?' Past tense, no defence, no offer." I could actually do that. I've stood at enough doors asking "was everything alright?" to know that question gets nothing.

The 2019 to 2020 bit, where the school did as well or better on everything it controlled and the results still fell off a cliff — that's a comfort, oddly. I'll do my job right and some year the outside world will still kick me.

**3. What didn't**

The numbers. "71.83% 83.10%", "60.2%", "79.5%", "within 150% of the published length", "315 days is 150% of 210". I glazed over. It's exactly the spreadsheet stuff I hate, and when the table appeared with arrows in it I skipped it wholesale. Two decimal places on a school of eighty-six people is fussing.

"Define the outcome before the sale, in a sentence with a date and a test in it." Fine for a job course. What's the outcome of a bacon bap? "By Tuesday this customer will be able to…" what? The author admits it — "Most businesses sell something whose result is diffuse" — but then gives me nothing for a place like mine. The Monday list still says "One line per product." I've got forty products.

"The counter-case this chapter does not have… the debt stands." Sounded like the author talking to other authors. Not for me.

**4. What changed between reads**

Second time, I read the Boundaries section properly and thought better of the author — they're careful to say the three reports don't line up with each other and that one school proves nothing about the industry. First time I'd taken the 2020 collapse as a clear story; second time I saw they were saying "we don't know why." I also noticed the five columns in the Monday bit are actually the whole chapter in one line: used it, got it, came back, told anyone, why they left. Should have been at the top.

I still couldn't tell you what "renewal sits inside the second test" means. Read it three times.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, mostly on the strength of the Monday list, and because "Reading Your Own Business" sounds like it might finally be about a business like mine.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Put one paragraph in "What this looks like with twenty customers" for a walk-in business. Tell me what the outcome sentence is for a café — the same faces on a Tuesday, someone bringing a friend, whatever it is — because right now the whole chapter is aimed at people who sell one big thing once, and I sell small things forty times a morning.
