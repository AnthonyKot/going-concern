## 1. Edits

### Edit 1 — qualify the opening principle

**BEFORE**

> Every business book eventually says that you cannot create demand, only find it.

**AFTER**

> Every business book eventually says that you cannot create demand, only find it, but demand does not sit in the world as a fixed quantity waiting to be discovered: "find it or don't" misses how repositioning an identical product for a different buyer can turn an indifferent market into an eager one, removing friction — a form that takes two minutes instead of twenty, delivery on Thursday instead of "within three weeks" — routinely converts people who wanted the thing in principle into people who buy it, and naming a category can assemble a demand that was real but had no word for itself, and therefore no way to search for you.

### Edit 2 — state the defensible iron law

**BEFORE**

> The principle underneath all of this is usually called the iron law of the market, and it says that no quality of execution will save a business selling something nobody wants. That is true. Keep it. It disposes of a large category of doomed ideas at no cost, and if you take nothing else from this book, take the habit of asking the question before rather than after.

**AFTER**

> The principle underneath all of this is usually called the iron law of the market, and it says that creating awareness of a product people would like if they knew about it is an ordinary, winnable job, but no quality of execution will save a business by manufacturing a preference that is not there or making people value something they fundamentally do not want. Most businesses that believe themselves to be doing the first are doing the second; the reason to keep the distinction sharp is that the failure mode looks identical from inside for about eighteen months. That is true. Keep it. It disposes of a large category of doomed ideas at no cost, and if you take nothing else from this book, take the habit of asking the question before rather than after.

### Edit 3 — build the irreducibly large case into the fixed-versus-variable advice, and attach solvency to timing

**BEFORE**

> Before you have real demand data, the correct instinct is to convert as much as possible of your cost base from fixed to variable, even when the variable option is more expensive per unit. Renting is worse than owning at scale and better than owning at uncertainty. Contractors cost more per hour than employees and can be stopped. Before the evidence is in, worse unit economics you can switch off are usually worth the premium over better ones you cannot.

**AFTER**

> Before you have real demand data, the correct instinct is either to convert as much as possible of your cost base from fixed to variable, even when the variable option is more expensive per unit, or, when you cannot build a quarter of a steel mill or license half a pharmacy and the minimum viable physical footprint is necessarily large, to demand far more evidence before committing and secure the demand contractually before the capital goes in rather than after. Which is chapter three. Renting is worse than owning at scale and better than owning at uncertainty. Contractors cost more per hour than employees and can be stopped. Before the evidence is in, worse unit economics you can switch off are usually worth the premium over better ones you cannot: you cannot control when a market arrives, but you can control whether you are still solvent when it does.

### Edit 4 — make early demand part of the second clause’s reasoning

**BEFORE**

> The first clause protects you from building something nobody wants. The second protects you from a different failure, and a harder one to see coming: building the right thing on a cost base that requires a level of demand you have not yet demonstrated. That failure is the subject of this chapter. It is difficult to recognise from inside because everything except the arithmetic looks like a business that is working.

**AFTER**

> The first clause protects you from building something nobody wants. The second protects you from a different failure, and a harder one to see coming: demand can be real and simply early, while the right thing is built on a cost base that requires a level of demand the market cannot yet supply. Webvan's market existed in 1999. It did not exist at 1999's cost of labour, smartphone penetration, or consumer habit. Being ten years early is indistinguishable, from inside the business, from being wrong — the spreadsheet looks the same, the bank balance looks the same, and the market research says people want it, which they do. That failure is the subject of this chapter. It is difficult to recognise from inside because everything except the arithmetic looks like a business that is working.

## 2. Where each limit now lives

1. **Execution changes demand.** The edited opening sentence now carries this limit inside the categorical “cannot create demand, only find it” claim. It is the direct host because that formulation is what repositioning, friction removal and category naming qualify.

2. **Execution cannot manufacture fundamental preference.** The edited iron-law sentence distinguishes awareness from preference while stating the law itself. The eighteen-month diagnostic remains in the same argumentative paragraph.

3. **Some businesses cannot start small.** The first edited sentence under “Variable beats fixed until you know the number” gives equal weight to two courses: reduce fixed commitments, or obtain stronger and contractual evidence when reduction is physically impossible.

4. **Small commitments obey the same arithmetic.** This is already carried by “When you sign a five-year lease…” and “Every fixed cost you take on has a number hidden inside it,” followed by the observation that most owners cannot state that number. The newly attached solvency sentence also preserves the smaller operator’s exposure to being early, so this paragraph can simply be deleted.

5. **Demand can be real and early.** The edited sentence explaining the second clause now makes timing part of the cost-structure failure it describes. The Webvan sentences supply the full distinction between eventual appetite and demand that the current economics can serve.

6. **The business controls solvency, not market arrival.** The final edited sentence in the variable-cost paragraph now makes survival through uncertain timing part of the case for cancellable commitments.

## 3. Loss

The chapter loses the rhetorical pause and numbered map supplied by a conspicuous boundaries section.
