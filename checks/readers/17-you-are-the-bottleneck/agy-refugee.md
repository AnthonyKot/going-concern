**1. Gut reaction after first read**
My first thought was that this is written for American software engineers who want passive income, not for someone trying to build a stable life from scratch in Rotterdam. The jargon annoyed me, but the underlying anxiety the author targets—what happens if you disappear for a week?—hit a nerve, because as a refugee with temporary protection, I have zero safety net. 

**2. What landed — specific passages and why they worked**
The absolute best part was the distinction between a heavy workload and a fragile business. "A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you." This speaks directly to my risk aversion. I don't mind working sixty hours a week if it means stability for my family; I am used to hard work. But the idea that the business "stops for a fortnight when you are in hospital" terrifies me. The rule to fix "Access first" because "losing it usually has the shortest fuse" is incredibly practical advice that I can use immediately.

**3. What didn't — where I felt excluded**
I almost tuned out entirely during "Move 2." The book uses examples like "Bingo Card Creator" and migrating "onto modern versions of PHP and MySQL." I had a professional career in Ukraine, but I am not a hacker. I felt completely excluded when the author assumed, "The developer's instinct is to solve all of this with code." I don't have that instinct, and it made me feel like I had picked up a manual for a club I don't belong to. It felt like that American hustle optimism where everyone is just one app away from quitting their day job, which is very disconnected from my reality.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**
On the first read, the heavy focus on code and servers made me think the method didn't apply to a normal service business. On the second read, I forced myself to ignore the tech examples and focused only on the "Monday" checklist at the end. Stripped of the software jargon, I realized the framework is actually brilliant. Ranking tasks by "tolerable delay rather than by how much time it takes" completely flipped how I was thinking about organizing my future work.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**
Yes, because chapter 18 is called "Writing It Down," and I am hoping it offers the concrete, step-by-step systems I need right now.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change.**
Replace at least one of the software case studies with a normal, traditional business—like a local shop, a consultancy, or an accounting practice—so that ordinary people building standard livelihoods don't assume this book isn't for them.
