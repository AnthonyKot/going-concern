## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core distinction—workload versus dependency—felt immediately useful and uncomfortably relevant to how I’d probably build a side business. The chapter is sharper than typical “work on the business, not in it” advice, though the middle spends too long litigating two old software-business anecdotes that cannot actually prove its thesis.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you.” That is the chapter’s best line because it corrects a mistake I could easily make: congratulating myself for automating a business down to five hours while remaining its only administrator, incident responder, and product decision-maker.

The instruction to ask “what waits?” also works. Ranking items by tolerable delay, rather than effort, is concrete and counterintuitive. I can picture discovering that a ten-minute certificate renewal matters more than a two-day reporting task.

“Automation can exchange visible labour for concealed dependence” landed hardest. I have built exactly that kind of system: low-touch until it fails, at which point the debugging burden is concentrated in the person who wrote it. Requiring “a second reader before it needs a new feature” is a memorable engineering standard, not business-book fog.

I also appreciated: “Being the bottleneck is a legitimate choice.” That keeps the argument from becoming the usual founder fantasy that every profitable activity must be turned into a scalable machine.

## 3. What didn’t

The McKenzie/Pinboard comparison becomes over-lawyered. After presenting the cases, the author repeatedly retreats: “missing evidence rather than demonstrated dependence,” “neither owner ran the experiment,” and finally “the evidence here is thinner than the argument.” The intellectual honesty is good, but by the third qualification I wondered why I had read so much case detail when neither case tests the claim.

The advice also quietly assumes another trustworthy person exists. “Add a second authorised person” and “get a second person into the next ordinary conversation” are sensible for a staffed company, but my likely side business would be me plus software. Who is this person—employee, contractor, spouse, cofounder? What access should they receive, what does it cost, and how do I avoid creating a security or continuity risk through them?

The Monday section gets repetitive. After the absence table, the next six instructions mostly unpack categories already explained. I would rather see one completed example than another restatement.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I thought the chapter was making a stronger empirical claim than its evidence supported. On the second, I noticed how explicitly it labels the four categories “a framework” and invites disagreement. I also better appreciated the nuance that relationships share access’s urgency but not its remediation timescale. Still, the disclaimers do not fully repay the space devoted to the cases.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the undocumented-automation problem is close enough to home that I would read the promised chapter on operational knowledge.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace half the McKenzie/Pinboard analysis with a completed absence table for a realistic solo software business, including affordable mitigations when there is no employee available to become the “second person.”
