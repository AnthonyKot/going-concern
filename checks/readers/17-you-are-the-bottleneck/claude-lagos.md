**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I felt seen for about two paragraphs and then watched the chapter walk off into somebody else's life. The split between "workload" and "bottleneck" is genuinely new to me — I'd never separated them, and I recognised myself instantly in the five-hour owner who is still a total bottleneck. Then it became two men in software, one in Japan, one running a bookmarking site, and I was reading about PHP migrations. By the end I had one useful idea and a lot of scrolling.

**2. What landed**

"A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you." That's my whole business in one line. I'm on my phone maybe four hours a day but nothing — not a refund, not a price for a custom fit, not a supplier chasing me on WhatsApp — moves without me.

"Rank what you find by tolerable delay rather than by how much time it takes." This changed my order of operations. Packing orders eats my day; the thing that actually kills me is a customer waiting eight hours for a yes on a discount and buying from another page instead.

"Access fails mechanically… Relationships fail socially: the customer, supplier or adviser will deal only with you." My tailor deals with me. Nobody else. He doesn't know my sister's name.

"illness does not care which model you picked." Blunt, true, cheap to act on.

**3. What didn't**

Move 2 is nearly the whole middle of the chapter and it's two software owners describing their own hours. "much of the core code on the site dated back to 2009-2010" — I have no code. I have a phone, a WhatsApp Business account and a notebook. Ten minutes of my reading went to a problem I will never have, and the author himself then admits neither case proves the point. If the evidence doesn't establish the argument, why is it the longest section?

The access advice assumes an economy I don't live in. "Move them to the business, add a second authorised person" — my "business" isn't registered, the bank account is my personal account with my BVN attached, and no Nigerian bank is adding a second authorised person to that in an afternoon. The Instagram account is in my name because there's no other option. Saying "for access items this is usually an afternoon" made me distrust the rest of the Monday list; that afternoon does not exist here.

"Automation can exchange visible labour for concealed dependence" — I understood it, but this is a section for someone who writes code. My automation is a saved reply and a WhatsApp catalogue.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read I thought "write the rule" was filler. Second read it's the strongest instruction in the chapter for me — a discount ceiling and a refund limit are one paragraph I can send to the girl who helps me pack, and it removes the thing that most often waits for me. I also noticed the chapter quietly ranks recurring work last, which is exactly where I've been spending all my energy. And I saw "What would prove this chapter wrong" and respected it — the author knows what he hasn't shown.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — "Writing It Down" is the category the chapter says is slowest and worst, and that's my tailor and my supplier list, so I want it.

**6. One concrete thing to change**

Cut Move 2 roughly in half and spend the space on a non-software, non-Western example — an owner whose accounts are legally in their personal name and can't be moved. Either that, or add one sentence in the access section admitting that "put it in the business's name" isn't available to everyone, and say what to do instead: written recovery details, a trusted second person who *can* reach the account, a list of who holds what. Right now the cheapest advice in the chapter is the piece I can't follow.
