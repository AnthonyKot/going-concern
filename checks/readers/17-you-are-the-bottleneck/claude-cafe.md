**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Right, this one actually got me in the chest a bit. I've spent 25 years being the person everyone waits for on a Saturday night, and I've never once heard it named as a *thing* separate from working long hours. But then it goes off into software blokes in Japan and PHP migrations and I stopped seeing myself in it. Ended up somewhere between "that's true about me" and "this book isn't for me."

**2. What landed**

The split at the top is the whole value: "A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you." I've worked for an owner who came in Tuesdays and Fridays and looked like a genius, and the place ground to a halt every time his phone was off. That paragraph — "it runs on your five hours" — explains a man I resented for six years.

"Exceptional decisions... are rare, which is why they never look urgent enough to delegate, and they queue behind you." That's the refund I'd have given, the supplier who short-delivered, the one who wants the back room for a wake. Never big enough to write down. Always me.

Access first, because it's cheap — that's the one I'll actually do. Bank, card machine, the Instagram, the supplier accounts, all in my name with only me able to get in. Twenty minutes of honesty and an afternoon of fixing. And "illness does not care which model you picked" is the sentence that made me sit up, because my sister had a fall last year and was out four months.

"A rule written calm is more consistent... it is also blunter" — yes. That's why I've never written them.

**3. What didn't**

Move 2 is where I went to make a brew. "much of the core code on the site dated back to 2009-2010" and "performing kidney transplants on a playing mariachi band" — I'm sure that's clever if you know what PHP is. I don't, and after two long sections of software men I genuinely wondered whether the author has ever met somebody with a fridge, a lease and eleven staff.

And this: "the weekly deploy." Twice. There's no deploy in a café. Same with "the automation you wrote and never documented" in Monday 5 — I haven't written any automation, so is that Monday just not for me? The whole "Automation can exchange visible labour for concealed dependence" section opens with "The developer's instinct" and I thought: fine, so I'm reading over someone's shoulder.

Also — "a certificate that expires." Mine's a food hygiene rating and a premises licence, which would have been a better example and one you actually can't trade without.

**4. What changed between reads**

Second read I noticed the honesty, and it bought back a lot of goodwill. "Neither owner ran the experiment this chapter is about: being unavailable" — the author knows the evidence doesn't prove the case and says so out loud, twice. I've never seen that in anything that sounds like this. And I understood the ranking rule properly the second time: "A four-hour task that cannot wait a day outranks a two-day task that can safely wait a month." First time I skimmed it as filler. It's the actual instruction, and it says my kitchen ordering matters more than my bookkeeping.

Second read I also saw the Boundaries section is talking directly to me — "the owner is the product" — and that's a real question for a café built on me knowing everyone's name.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — the next one's called Writing It Down and that's the bit I'm worst at.

**6. One concrete thing to change**

Swap one of the two software cases for a business with premises and staff, or at minimum put a non-software example in every one of the four categories and in the Monday list — as it stands the framework is general and every single illustration is a man alone with a computer.
