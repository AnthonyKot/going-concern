### 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core test—what protection actually does when the feared event occurs—is sharp and useful. But the chapter spends too long restating it, while relying on one founder anecdote that explicitly does not reveal whether the decision worked.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“Write the event, not the fear” landed immediately. As an engineer, I can work with a concrete threat model—who does what with which asset—far better than with generalized founder anxiety.

The distinction between buying protection and buying “standing” was also valuable: “the protection is the process, priced separately and paid later.” I’ve signed NDAs and reviewed IP clauses, but I haven’t always mentally attached enforcement cost, management distraction, discovery, and the chance of collecting anything. Writing the second price beside the filing or contract price is a genuinely useful move.

I also trusted the author more for admitting, “Studio Neat’s post is not that case, and it is not the opposite either.” That is unusually honest for a business book. The evidence shows the decision, not the outcome.

### 3. What didn’t

The claim that “A copy takes the customers who were never choosing you” is much too confident. A well-funded competitor can copy the product, bundle it, out-distribute me, undercut me, and take customers who previously chose me quite deliberately. In software, switching costs, integrations, procurement relationships, platform access, and sales reach can matter more than whether customers liked my email response.

The follow-on assertion—“at this scale it rarely is” in the object alone—also feels unsupported. For a small SaaS company, the object may include years of specialized engineering, data, workflow design, or technical performance. “Ship the next version” is not necessarily a defense against a larger company with more engineers and an existing channel.

I got bored during the repeated explanations of patents as permission to sue. Studio Neat’s quotes make the point once; the chapter then makes it several more times. Meanwhile, patents’ possible value in fundraising, acquisition diligence, deterrence, defensive portfolios, or licensing is barely considered.

Monday item 3 sounds empirical but asks for a counterfactual the records cannot answer. Returning-versus-new revenue does not tell me which customers a future copy would take.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I thought the chapter was basically anti-patent with disclaimers. On the second, I noticed it is more disciplined than that: it repeatedly limits the evidence, distinguishes jurisdictions, and tells me to consult an expert. I also noticed that its strongest idea is threat modeling and full-cost accounting, while its weakest idea is presenting customer loyalty as the default small-business moat.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because the author’s evidentiary honesty outweighs the repetition and overreach.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace one-third of the Studio Neat repetition with a contrasting case where a small company enforced—or regretted not obtaining—IP protection, including actual legal cost and business outcome.
