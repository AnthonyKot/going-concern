### 1. Gut reaction after first read
I rolled my eyes at first because it sounded like more Silicon Valley tech-bro theory about patents and Kickstarters. But once it stripped away the legal illusion and said protection is just the right to spend money on lawyers, it hit home hard. For someone hustling on Instagram in Lagos where the courts take a decade, accepting that outrunning the copycats is my only real defense is actually a huge relief.

### 2. What landed — specific passages and why they worked for someone like me.
The realization that legal instruments don't stop theft, they just "give you the right to start an expensive process you have not budgeted for." That is the absolute truth in Nigeria. If a local supplier steals my dress design, suing them is a joke—I don't have the cash, and our justice system is notoriously slow. Also, the line, "A copy takes the customers who were never choosing you." I felt that in my chest. Let the bargain hunters buy the cheap knockoffs; my real WhatsApp list buys from me for my styling, my customer service, and the fact that my dispatch rider actually shows up on time. 

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation.
I got irritated when the book leaned on "Studio Neat" and their Kickstarter hitting its goal in "eighty-five minutes." That frictionless world doesn't exist for me; I'm fighting crazy inflation and fluctuating exchange rates just to import fabric. Also, the Monday action item to "Ask an expert once... in the hour chapter twenty-four told you to buy." Please. I am bootstrapping this from my Infinix phone. I am not paying a Nigerian lawyer's consultation fee just to be told I can't afford to enforce a non-disclosure agreement against my former assistant. It assumes a level of institutional trust and spare cash I simply don't have.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two.
On the first read, I was so distracted by the American context (patents, Kickstarters, US dollars) that I almost wrote the whole chapter off as irrelevant. On the second read, I realized the author is actually validating my reality. The book is actively telling me *not* to waste my scarce capital on legal illusions and to pour it back into my next clothing drop instead ("putting money into the thing that cannot be copied"). It confirmed my natural survival instinct: just keep moving faster than the thieves.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because if chapter 26 is about "When the Ground Moves," I want to see if the author actually understands what it takes to survive when your economy's ground is constantly shaking.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change.
Stop using software patents and Kickstarter tech-bros to explain basic business survival; give an example of a regular retail or service business getting ripped off, because we are the ones who desperately need to hear that we shouldn't blow our last funds on useless exclusivity clauses.
