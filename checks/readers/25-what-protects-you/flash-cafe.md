### 1. Gut reaction after first read
It’s much more down-to-earth than I expected from a book that chops things into "Moves," though it still smells faintly of an American tech seminar. The core point hit me like cold water: buying a fancy legal document doesn't stop bad luck; it just buys you an expensive ticket to a courtroom you can't afford. For someone putting her redundancy money and a bank loan on the line for a café, it was a massive relief to hear that I don't need to throw thousands at lawyers just to feel safe.

### 2. What landed
* **"the filing does not stop the copy; it lets you hire a lawyer to argue about the copy."** 
* **"On the morning the person you trained opens the list at a competitor's desk, what does the non-disclosure agreement do — stop the opening, or give you a claim about it?"**
* **"...the one kind of protection at this scale that has to be earned rather than bought — being the one the customers choose, and come back to."**

In 25 years running dining rooms, I’ve seen head chefs walk out on a Friday afternoon with half the kitchen staff and the house recipes. A non-disclosure agreement in a drawer doesn't cook the Saturday night service. Real protection on a high street isn't paper; it’s regular customers who come back because the coffee is hot, the staff are warm, and the food is consistent.

### 3. What didn't
* **"From chapter eleven's renew-and-reorder column and chapter twenty-two's returning-against-new figure, with the concentration figure's names beside them..."**
* **"...a small moulded clamp that reached its funding goal eighty-five minutes after one link in October 2010..."**

All the constant cross-referencing to previous chapters and spreadsheet columns made my eyes glaze over—I'm opening a café, not sitting an accounting exam. Basing the whole argument on two American lads selling plastic phone clamps on Kickstarter felt miles away from my reality. Selling plastic bits over the internet to Germany isn't the same as dealing with local footfall, supplier bills, and council rates.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I skimmed right past the section titled *What would prove this chapter wrong*, thinking it was academic padding. On the second read, that part actually won me over. The author admits they don't know if Studio Neat’s brand actually survived the knockoffs long-term, and confesses they couldn't find a small-business case study for insurance claims. Most business gurus pretend they have a flawless formula; admitting what the evidence doesn't prove made the author feel honest rather than preachy.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because despite the annoying spreadsheet talk, it respects my intelligence and might stop me blowing my savings on useless paperwork.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Drop the fixation on online gadget startups and give at least one grounded, physical example—like a bakery, a mechanic, or a café—so regular people risking their life savings on the high street don't feel like this book was only written for tech kids.
