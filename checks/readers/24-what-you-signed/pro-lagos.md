### 1. Gut reaction after first read
I felt a mix of anxiety and exhaustion. The core message hit hard—that unwritten rules will bite me eventually—but then the chapter swerved into a massive block of foreign legal jargon that made my eyes glaze over. It felt like another business book written for someone in a stable country with a corporate lawyer on speed dial, not someone selling clothes via WhatsApp in Lagos where "the law's default" is basically whoever shouts the loudest. 

### 2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me
The absolute truth of this line got me: *"An agreement you did not write down is not an agreement without terms. Its terms are the law's default plus the other side's memory, and they are read out on the day of the dispute by whoever has the most to gain from their version."* 

That is exactly how survival works here. When a dispatch rider ruins a delivery or a tailor messes up a batch of fabric, it always turns into a shouting match based on "memory." I also loved the advice to move terms *"from the invoice to the quote."* I usually send my no-refund policy after they've paid the deposit, and customers always fight me on it. Moving it upfront costs me zero naira and actually makes practical sense for my WhatsApp hustle.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation
I completely checked out during the giant grey box about "Dutch ground." Quoting the *"Auteurswet"* in actual Dutch? Come on. I’m hustling on IG with terrible electricity and inflation eating my margins; I do not have the patience for European copyright law. 

I also felt totally excluded when the author said: *"Take the sentence, the number and your legal form to an insurance adviser and ask what a policy against it costs."* An insurance adviser? For an IG clothing vendor in Nigeria? If a batch of clothes gets ruined, I eat the cost or fight the supplier. Small business insurance for this level of trade essentially doesn't exist here, and pretending everyone can just *"buy one hour"* with a lawyer ignores how prohibitively expensive and intimidating that is when you are strictly cash-constrained.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, the Dutch legal text made me want to close the book, and I almost dismissed the whole chapter as Eurocentric garbage. On the second read, I skipped the legal box entirely and realized the underlying framework—categorizing risks into makers, customers, platforms, and liability—is actually brilliant. I realized the author was just using the Dutch law as a placeholder to prove a point: that default laws exist, even if you don't know them. The Monday action plan at the end is actually something I can execute this week from my phone, even without a lawyer.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? 
Yes, because even though it assumes I have structural resources I don't, the raw logic about protecting my business from bad memories is too valuable to ignore.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Cut the untranslated Dutch legal text completely and replace it with a simple, universal story of how a default law destroyed a small business; right now, that giant box is a massive roadblock that makes impatient readers like me want to quit.
