### 1. Gut reaction after first read

I came away with one genuinely useful framework buried inside an overlong legal chapter: makers, customers, dependencies, and liability. It made me want to audit my hypothetical side business, but the extended Dutch-law block caused me to skim, and several universal-sounding claims felt stronger than the evidence presented.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“Nothing has gone wrong…is the only time this chapter is cheap” is sharp and credible. I’ve seen teams postpone ownership, vendor, and liability questions because production is working; once a relationship deteriorates, every missing document becomes leverage.

The best practical point is: “find the sentence that makes it yours.” That is specific enough to act on. Software people routinely confuse paying for development with owning the code, designs, domains, deployment accounts, and underlying licensed components.

“Move your terms from the invoice to the quote” also works. It converts an abstract enforceability warning into a concrete workflow change, including preserving the dated email as evidence.

Finally, I respected “What this chapter owes.” Admitting that no primary-document case was found—and labeling the ordering and thresholds as the book’s own rules of thumb—is unusually honest for a business book.

### 3. What didn’t

I checked out during the long Dutch statutory quotations beginning “The Auteurswet starts from the maker.” As a US reader, I understood why the jurisdiction-specific evidence was there, but several screens of untranslated law made me feel like an accidental visitor rather than the intended reader. A concise English explanation with the original language in notes would have been enough.

“Every jurisdiction has a version of the same test” and “Paying the invoice transfers nothing” sound too categorical. The chapter later admits that defaults vary, so these lines undermine its otherwise careful boundaries. In the US, I’d immediately want distinctions among copyright assignment, work made for hire, implied license, ownership of physical deliverables, and third-party components.

“A professional’s hour is owed when…” is appealingly concrete but commercially naive. A competent attorney may need onboarding, document review, conflicts checks, and follow-up; “one hour” risks becoming another reassuring slogan. The triggers also ignore cumulative exposure from many small recurring transactions.

I also don’t buy the fixed ordering that puts liability last because it is “least likely.” For food, healthcare, security, infrastructure, or professional advice, catastrophic liability may deserve first position.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I noticed how disciplined the chapter is about separating its sourced Dutch claims from its unsourced heuristics. That raised my trust. But I also noticed that the strongest, most memorable universal claims are precisely where the sourcing is weakest, so my confidence in the four-part checklist increased while my confidence in its ordering and thresholds decreased.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the liability chapter is the obvious test of whether this framework becomes operational or remains cautionary prose.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Dutch-law box by two-thirds and use that space for one fully worked US-style example showing the actual documents, disputed clause, dollars at risk, and what the lawyer changed.
