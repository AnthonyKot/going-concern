## 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is substantially better than typical founder advice because it treats early customers as evidence about operations, not proof of product-market fit. I trusted the author’s skepticism, but the chapter felt overbuilt around a case that does not actually document its first ten customers. By the end, I wanted the operating worksheet more than the extended WP Curve analysis.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“Ten is a review point, not an evidentiary threshold” is exactly right. As an engineer, I recognize the distinction between observing a system and pretending a tiny dataset supports a forecast.

The five opening questions are strong, especially “How much unpriced custom work appeared once you started delivering?” That is the side-business trap I would personally worry about: believing I built a product when I really created a second job whose support burden is hidden.

“Repeatability is not something you discover… it is something you protect by declining the eleventh when it is shaped differently” was the sharpest line. Turning down bespoke work while revenue is tiny is a real decision with an immediate cost, not motivational theater.

I also liked “Blank cells are missing instrumentation, not zeroes.” That is concrete, technically literate, and immediately usable. The distinction between “not yet observable” and zero is equally good.

Finally, the “Boundaries” section increased my trust. The author admits that the seven-customer figure is inferred, the revenue combines businesses, the reporting series has a suspiciously placed gap, and the founders had preexisting distribution.

## 3. What didn’t

The title promises “The First Ten Customers,” but the useful evidence comes after “roughly fifty conversations” and “more than 150 customers.” The author acknowledges this, but that does not fix the case mismatch. I kept wondering why I was reading this particular example.

The repeated jurisdictional language—“chapter eleven owns” retention, “Part II owns” the first two—became tiring. “This chapter stops at…” sounds like internal book architecture, not help for me. I do not need the conceptual borders policed this often.

The revenue chronology is too long relative to what it proves. The sequence from $476 through $9,812, plus reconstructed timing and multiple customer counts, made me expect a quantitative conclusion that never really arrived.

The advice also fits a labor-heavy service more cleanly than a software product. “Value the hours at chapter nine’s replacement cost” is sensible accounting, but for someone with a highly paid engineering job, replacement cost, opportunity cost, and cash cost can produce radically different decisions. The chapter does not acknowledge that choice.

“The dependency is strict” also overstates things. In software, I can learn about retention, usage, and even rough unit economics during pilots before acquisition is remotely repeatable.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I appreciated how carefully the author limits the claims; the “counter-case this chapter does not have” is unusually honest. But I also noticed that those caveats quietly concede the central weakness: this is not evidence about ten customers, and the recommended action threshold remains subjective—“when a pattern is visible.” My trust in the author increased while my confidence in the case decreased.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because retention is where I would expect this framework to face a harder and more relevant test.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the WP Curve chronology by half and replace it with one completed, anonymized customer-record table showing promised scope, actual hours, exceptions, margin, segment, and the resulting decision.
