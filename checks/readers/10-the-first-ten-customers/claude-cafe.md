**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Somebody's finally said out loud that ten customers isn't a market, which I've known from a quarter-century of watching owners get giddy after a good opening fortnight. But then it walked me through six months of some Australian lad's WordPress subscription numbers and I lost the thread. I don't sell WordPress. I'll sell flat whites and bacon baps, and nobody's going to "cancel inside the month."

**2. What landed**

"Ten is a review point, not an evidentiary threshold." That's exactly right. I've seen a launch week go beautifully because the owner's mates all turned up, and by week six the place was empty.

"Asking finds the buyers who say no. Delivering finds the buyers who said yes and should not have." I read that twice. It's true of a café: the people who come in wanting a full breakfast at half eleven on a Tuesday and then complain about the wait — you find out who they are by serving them, not by asking them.

The bit about turning down "tens of thousands of dollars worth of one off work" — that's the outside-catering trap. Every café gets asked to do the christening buffet, and it's good money and it wrecks the kitchen for a day. The line "the cost is paid immediately, in cash, while the benefit is a process you can still describe in a year" is the truest thing in the chapter.

And "Blank cells are missing instrumentation, not zeroes" — I'd never have put it that way but I know exactly what it means. If you didn't write down that the coffee machine went down Thursday, it didn't go down.

**3. What didn't**

The revenue ladder. "May 2013 (pre-launch) $476… December $9,812." I glazed over. Then the author spends a paragraph telling me the figures aren't even that business's own figures — "The revenue figures are company-wide, not WP Curve's" — so why am I looking at them?

"Carry in the segment and refusals from chapter six, the acquisition mechanism from seven, the ask and objection from eight" — I'm being told to extend a spreadsheet I don't have. If I've started here, this Monday section is a locked door.

"Value the hours at chapter nine's replacement cost." That's jargon to me. I know what my labour costs; I don't know what "replacement cost" is meant to add.

And "did delivery reveal that you had sold to the wrong person" — in a café, I don't pick who walks in. The whole "stop selling to a segment" idea assumes I'm choosing customers. I'm not, mostly. The nearest I get is what's on the menu and what hours I open.

**4. What changed between reads**

Second time, I saw that the author is honestly nervous about the case — the "Boundaries" section spends five paragraphs undermining its own evidence, including "the gap sits exactly where the hardest stretch would be." First read that annoyed me; second read I respected it. Also, "They were not starting cold… if you are starting without one, expect the same process to run slower" — I missed that first time, and it's the most relevant sentence for me. I have no podcasters. I have a Facebook page with 200 followers.

The five questions in Move 1 are actually good, and they'd fit a café if I rewrote them: can I get Saturday's trade again next Saturday, how much off-menu stuff crept in, what broke, who shouldn't I have served, can I do it with the hours I've got.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, because the next chapter's about whether people come back, and that's the only question I actually care about.

**6. One thing to change**

Give me one small example that isn't a software subscription — a sandwich shop, a plumber, anybody who serves people in person — even three sentences, so I'm not translating the whole chapter from Australian WordPress into my own trade.
