### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is sharper than most positioning advice because it defines positioning as an operating constraint, not homepage copy. The Plausible story held my attention, although the chapter spends too long extracting lessons from one unusually favorable case. I finished with a useful test, but not yet a method I’d confidently apply to my own side project.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“A business without [a defined buyer] settles them by whoever asked most recently and most loudly.” I’ve watched internal platforms and startups become exactly that: products shaped by support escalation rather than intent. Framing positioning as a way to decide “which bug first” and “which feature next” makes it concrete for an engineer.

“A positioning statement that costs you nothing is not a positioning statement” also worked. Most business books tell me to identify an ideal customer but dodge the uncomfortable part: naming solvent customers I would willingly refuse. The three-customer test is specific enough to expose fake conviction.

The chapter also earns credibility by weakening its own case: “Five things changed in March 2020 and positioning was one,” and “nobody isolated it.” That is the kind of causal humility I rarely see in founder narratives. Recalculating the growth rates and noting that 23% monthly growth can feel stagnant at tiny revenue was especially useful; it separates emotional dashboard-reading from actual trajectory.

### 3. What didn’t

I got bored during the long MRR chronology. Once the chapter established $433, the positioning change, and the subsequent acceleration, every later milestone added drama more than evidence.

I also doubted how transferable “Google Analytics alternative” is. Plausible had a globally known incumbent, an emotionally charged privacy issue, Hacker News distribution, and a marketer joining at exactly the right moment. The author acknowledges this—“that part does not generalise”—but only after building most of the chapter’s persuasive force from it.

The biggest applicability gap is admitted near the end: “Most readers here will narrow on who the customer is… Different manoeuvre, different failure mode, not tested here.” That is probably my situation. If I’m selling a developer tool to mid-market engineering teams, choosing company size, stack, buyer, and procurement tolerance is harder than rejecting an incumbent’s values. The Monday exercise tells me to write the sentence, but not how to decide among several plausible narrowings without prematurely shrinking the market.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I took the chapter as “narrowing caused Plausible’s growth.” On the second, I noticed the author repeatedly disclaims that conclusion: the new cofounder, content, relaunch, site structure, and existing compound growth all confound it. The stronger surviving claim is narrower: positioning made prior product and business-model choices public, coherent, and useful for everyday tradeoffs. I also appreciated more on rereading that adding funnels later is presented as renegotiating a constraint, not proof that the original position was fake.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the chapter correctly leaves distribution unresolved, and that is the part I most want tested.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Add a compact second case where a founder narrows by customer type rather than opposition to a dominant incumbent, including what evidence selected that niche and what happened to revenue afterward.
