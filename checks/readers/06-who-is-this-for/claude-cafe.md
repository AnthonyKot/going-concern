**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I'll be honest, I nearly put it down at "$433 a month" and "MRR." It's about two lads and a bit of software that fights Google, and I'm opening a café in Darlington. But somewhere around "who you are going to disappoint on purpose" it stopped being about them and started being about me, and I finished it slightly cross that it was right.

**2. What landed**

"It is the rule you consult on ordinary days, when two things both look worth doing and there is time for one." Twenty-five years of that. Which supplier to ring back first, whose complaint gets a free coffee and whose gets a polite goodbye. Nobody ever called that positioning; it was just what the good managers did and the bad ones didn't.

"A product shaped like its own support queue." I've run restaurants like that. Menus with forty items because every regular asked for one thing and the owner couldn't say no.

"Name three kinds of customer who should not buy from you." That's the first business-book exercise I've ever seen that I could do on the back of a beer mat. Mine: people who want a full English at 7am, people who want to sit for three hours on one flat white with a laptop, and hen parties. There, done.

"You can be compounding healthily and still read your own dashboard as proof that you are stuck." I don't have a dashboard, but I've watched owners panic in February and blow money on a refit when the numbers were actually fine.

"Refusing advanced reporting at $433 a month is cheap — you have no such customers to lose." That's the bit I'd underline. Saying no is cheapest before you've got anyone to say no to. Which is now.

**3. What didn't**

The whole of Move 2. "Streamlining the communication to send a consistent message everywhere" — that's the TED-talk voice I was warned about, and it's the author quoting someone else's bullet points at me for half a page. The ladder of dollar figures meant nothing to me until the author did the sums later.

"Open source gives up the licensing moat" — I had to guess what a moat is here. Lost me for a paragraph.

The Boundaries section says it straight out: "Most readers here will narrow on who the customer is — one trade, one size of company, one country. Different manoeuvre, different failure mode, not tested here." So the author knows the case doesn't quite fit people like me, and says so, and then moves on. Honest, but a bit like a plumber saying "your boiler's different, anyway here's what I'd do with mine."

"Find your incumbent" — mine is Greggs and the Costa by the station. I can't pick a fight with Greggs on Hacker News, whatever that is.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I thought the story was "they changed their slogan and got rich." Second time I saw the author was arguing the opposite — the sentence didn't invent anything, it just tied up decisions they'd already made so they couldn't wriggle out. That's genuinely useful and I missed it entirely on read one because I was skimming the numbers. I also noticed the author kept undercutting their own case — "five things changed and positioning was one," "the debt stands." First time that felt like waffle; second time it felt like someone I'd trust with a till.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, because the next one's about "where they already are," and if that's about where customers actually walk past, I need it more than this one.

**6. One thing to change**

Move the "three customers you turn away" test up to the top and make me do it before the Plausible story, not after — I'd have read the case looking for my own answer instead of wading through someone else's revenue figures wondering when it'd be my turn.
