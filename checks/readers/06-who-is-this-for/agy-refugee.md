**1. Gut reaction after first read**
I appreciated the total lack of "you can do it!" cheerleading. It reads like a dry manual, which I vastly prefer over empty American hustle optimism, but my stomach dropped when I read about the timeline. I don't have the luxury or savings to survive ten months to make a few hundred euros while trying to keep my family afloat in the Netherlands. 

**2. What landed**
The logic about not taking every customer made complete sense. When I did marketing in Kyiv, the instinct to constantly widen the net to get more clients was always there. The author’s point that widening is "not stupidity, it is arithmetic run backwards" really clicked for me. I also liked the blunt definition of positioning: "It is who you are going to disappoint on purpose." If I start a small accounting service here, I need to know exactly who I will say no to, otherwise I will just be competing on price with locals, which is a race to the bottom I can't win.

**3. What didn't**
I felt completely alienated by the financial reality of the Plausible case study. The author praises the founders for giving away the majority of the market "at $433 a month, by two people drawing no salary." I cannot draw no salary. I am here under temporary protection, paying Dutch rent, with a family relying on my income. This advice implicitly assumes a massive safety net—probably a working spouse or years of accumulated wealth in a stable currency—that I just do not have. When he talks about "giving revenue away" as a strategic move, I felt like the book forgot about people who actually need cash to survive the month.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**
On the first read, I was distracted by the software jargon (first-party cookies, open source, Hacker News) and thought this wasn't for me. On the second read, I realized the math section at the end is the real lesson. The author points out that going from $64 to $403 isn't stagnant; it's a "23% a month, compounding" growth rate. It shifted my perspective from panicking over small raw numbers to looking at the percentage trend, which is a much more rational way to evaluate if a tiny business is working.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**
Yes, because the next chapter promises to explain how to put the message "in front of the right crowd," which is my biggest hurdle right now as an immigrant with a tiny local network.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**
Acknowledge the immense financial privilege required to work for a year on a business that pays zero salary, and include a brief example of how to apply this "positioning by refusal" when you are desperate for immediate cash flow and cannot survive on compounding pennies.
