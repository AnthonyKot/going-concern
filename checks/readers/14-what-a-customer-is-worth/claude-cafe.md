**1. Gut reaction after first read**

Right — a woman spent seven years building software for doctors and it never had a chance, because the thing cost her seventy quid a month and she could only charge under a hundred. That I understood without anyone explaining it to me. The eleven years line stopped me dead; that's a business that's already dead and nobody's told it. But it's all software, and I kept waiting for someone in a café or a shop to turn up and they never did.

**2. What landed**

"Thirty dollars a month against four thousand up front." That's the whole chapter in nine words and I'd have got it stood at a bar.

"A paying customer and a worthwhile customer are not the same thing." I've had customers like that in every restaurant I ran. The party of twelve who book the back room, run you ragged for three hours, drink tap water. You're rushed off your feet and you've lost money. Nobody's ever put a name to that for me before.

"Positive is not the same as sufficient." Yes. And this bit — "A business could run on it if everything else about the business were free, and nothing about a business is free." That's the fear I wake up with.

"There is no average customer." The bloggers and the businesses paying the same $69 and being two completely different customers — that's my lunch trade versus my Saturday brunch trade, and I'd never have thought to count them separately.

And I liked being told what the chapter can't prove. "Cydoc cannot tell you what acquisition costs." An author admitting the limits of his own example is the opposite of a TED talk, and it bought him a lot of trust.

**3. What didn't**

The opening of Move 1 assumes something about me that isn't true: "Somewhere you have already multiplied a monthly payment by a number of months and written the result down." I haven't. I've never done that in my life and I don't have monthly payments — people come in, buy a flat white, leave. So the chapter's opening move is correcting a mistake I haven't made, and for a paragraph I thought this book wasn't for me.

"Two customers who each eventually contribute €2,000" — euros, out of nowhere, in a chapter of dollars. Small thing, but it made me wonder if anyone had read it through.

The Monday list is where I stalled. "Carry the outcome status from chapter eleven across as well — renewed, lapsed, still here." I don't have renewals. Nobody subscribes to a café. I can do the segments — regulars, passing trade, the office order — but the whole apparatus of acquisition spend per customer and a payback date per customer doesn't survive contact with a business where nobody's name is written down. And "your own acquisition time valued at replacement cost" is jargon dressed as plain speech; say "what you'd pay someone else to do it."

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I read the Cydoc numbers as a sad story. Second time I saw it was a *shape*: price ceiling set by the customer, cost per customer set by someone else, big setup cost you can't recover. And that shape does fit me — my price is set by what people will pay for a coffee round here, my costs by the roaster and the energy company, and my fit-out is my four thousand. The chapter never says this. I had to build the bridge myself.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — "Paying Yourself" is the one I'm most frightened of, and this chapter earned enough credit to get me there.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Add one paragraph applying the same three subtractions to a business with no subscriptions and no customer names — a café, a hairdresser, a garage — where the "customer" is a segment and the "setup cost" is the fit-out. Without it, half your readers will decide the arithmetic is for software people.
