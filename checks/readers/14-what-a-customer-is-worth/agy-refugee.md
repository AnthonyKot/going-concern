### 1. Gut reaction after first read
I felt a deep sense of relief reading this. Most Western business advice I've seen is full of aggressive, American-style hustle optimism that expects you to project millions in future sales and ignore immediate risks. This chapter was like a cold, sobering bucket of water—it respects reality, hard math, and survival over blind faith. 

### 2. What landed
The insistence on counting only "contribution to date" rather than projected lifetime value landed perfectly. Back in Ukraine, I worked in accounting, and I know firsthand that you cannot pay rent with a customer's projected future loyalty. The warning that "your labour has a price even when no money moves" also struck a nerve. With my time stretched thin between learning Dutch, navigating a new system, and managing family obligations, treating my own unbilled hours as a strict financial cost is exactly how I need to protect myself. 

### 3. What didn't
I felt entirely excluded by the Cydoc case study. The author quotes: "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000." I am looking to start a small, manageable business here in the Netherlands—perhaps local marketing or bookkeeping—because nobody here values my foreign credentials. I am not building a seven-year medical AI startup. When the text started analyzing an 11-year payback period for software, I almost stopped reading. It felt like a Silicon Valley problem for people with massive financial safety nets, completely detached from the reality of someone who needs a business that can feed a family this year.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I was so irritated by the tech-heavy AI example that I missed the underlying mechanics. On the second read, I forced myself to look past the medical software jargon and realized the "Monday" section at the end is just a pure, ruthless spreadsheet exercise. I saw how the exact same logic—deducting setup time, support, and acquisition costs before calling a customer profitable—maps perfectly to a simple service business. 

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because seeing that the next chapter is "Paying Yourself" tells me the author actually understands the most terrifying and important part of starting a business.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Drop the seven-year AI startup case—or at least balance it—by including an example of a boring, traditional service business so ordinary readers with limited savings don't have to work so hard to translate the lesson to their reality.
