**1. Gut reaction after first read.**
This is the first chapter in a while that felt like it was written about my actual problem — I sell a dress for ₦25,000 and I have genuinely never sat down and asked what's left after the boosted post, the dispatch rider, and the two returns. The Cydoc arithmetic is clean and I followed it without re-reading. But the whole case is a software business in America with $4,000 integrations, and I kept translating in my head the whole time.

**2. What landed.**
"A paying customer and a worthwhile customer are not the same thing" — that sentence is my last four months. I have customers who order constantly and I now suspect I lose money on every one, because they're in Ikorodu and the rider costs more than my margin.

The four subtractions are the most useful list in the book so far, and "Refunds, credits and concessions... they tend to live in nobody's calculation at all" is exactly right. My returns are not a line anywhere. Somebody sends the dress back, I resell it eventually, and I've never counted the two riders it cost me.

"There is no average customer" with the WP Curve bloggers-versus-businesses split — same $69, two different relationships. That's my "just browsing, send price" DMs versus the woman who buys for her whole office. Same price, and I have been treating them as one number.

And "your labour has a price even when no money moves." I spend maybe three hours a day answering DMs from people who never buy. Nobody has ever told me to put a naira figure on that.

**3. What didn't.**
The costs in this chapter don't move. "$70 per doctor" is a stable number you can subtract from a stable price. Mine aren't. My fabric supplier changed prices twice this quarter, fuel moves, and the dollar rate decides what my imported stock costs before I've sold last month's. Contribution to date computed in naira across six months isn't one number — it's six numbers in six different currencies wearing the same name. The chapter never says a word about this, and for anyone reading from a high-inflation economy that's not a footnote, it's the whole calculation.

"The month in which cumulative contribution first exceeded what you spent" also assumes a payback measured in years is even a meaningful frame. Eleven years is a scandal in this chapter. Here, eleven *months* is a long time to have money tied in stock.

The one place I felt talked down to is small: "Somewhere you have already multiplied a monthly payment by a number of months and written the result down." I haven't. I've never done LTV, never heard the term, and the chapter spends its opening telling me my imaginary spreadsheet is wrong.

**4. What changed between read one and read two.**
First time I read the eleven-year payback as the point. Second time the real point was the sentence about positive-not-sufficient — "positive on the monthly line and could not carry the business, and both of those things were true at the same time for years." That's the frightening one, because my margin is positive and I still can't pay myself. I also noticed the boundaries section admits Cydoc can't tell you acquisition cost — which quietly removes the one subtraction I most needed help measuring.

**5. Would I keep reading?**
Yes — "Paying Yourself" is the chapter I've been waiting fourteen chapters for.

**6. One thing I'd change.**
Add one short passage on what to do when your own costs move faster than your customers' payments — restate contribution in the currency or the input that actually moves (units of fabric, dollars of stock), or say plainly that the method needs adjusting. As written, the whole chapter assumes a stable naira, and it never says so.
