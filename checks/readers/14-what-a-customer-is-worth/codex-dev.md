## 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is sharper than most business-book treatments of LTV. I liked the insistence on actual cash, attributable costs, and payback time, but the chapter repeats its core point enough that I started skimming before “Monday.”

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“Future expansion is unknown, not zero — and it is also not evidence” is the line I’d keep. I’ve seen teams make a weak customer look strategic by stuffing expansion and referrals into a spreadsheet; separating observed contribution from hypotheses is genuinely useful.

“A free channel that consumes two days of founder time per customer is not free” also lands. As an engineer considering a side business, my time is probably the largest cost I’m most tempted to pretend is zero.

The instruction to write “not yet” instead of forecasting a payback date is concrete and behavior-changing. So is “aggregate by segment and by channel, never once for the business.” Those are operational rules I could put into a spreadsheet this weekend.

## 3. What didn’t

The claim that lifetime value “is worse than useless” feels overstated. Badly estimated LTV is dangerous, but contribution-to-date has its own severe age bias: a two-month-old customer and a two-year-old customer cannot be compared cleanly without some treatment of observation time. The chapter acknowledges the limitation late, but the opening sells certainty the method has not earned.

More seriously, the featured arithmetic appears dimensionally incomplete. The chapter compares “$70 per doctor” and under “$30 a month per doctor” with “$4,000 to $6,000” per practice, then says $4,000 “comes back in a little over eleven years.” That works only for a one-doctor practice. If an integrated practice has ten doctors, the setup payback is roughly fourteen months before other costs. Maybe Draelos’s eleven-year estimate included the actual practice size or additional expenses, but the chapter as written says, “Her estimate and the arithmetic agree” without supplying the denominator.

I also got bored during the repeated restatements that positive contribution is not sufficient. The point was already established by the Cydoc example.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, the Cydoc numbers felt devastating and authoritative. On the second, I noticed the per-doctor/per-practice mismatch and became less confident in the centerpiece. Conversely, the “Boundaries” section held up better than I initially gave it credit for; admitting that the method systematically misses delayed value is unusually honest.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the teaser about pricing the owner’s labor addresses exactly the omission I care about.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Rewrite the Cydoc calculation with explicit units and doctor count: show the complete equation behind the eleven-year payback, or stop claiming the displayed arithmetic independently verifies it.
