### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is unusually sober pricing advice: it treats price as an experiment constrained by costs, not as a confidence ritual. I trusted the author more because they kept narrowing what the Cosmonaut example could prove. The chapter is useful, though it sometimes feels like a pricing memo padded into a chapter.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“A price has to pass two independent tests: the sale must be worthwhile for the buyer, and fulfilling it must be worthwhile for you.” That is the cleanest framing here. As an engineer, I naturally reach for computable inputs—hours, salary, hosting, contractors—but those establish viability, not willingness to pay.

“Use the cost of replacing the work competently, not the comforting fiction that your own evenings are free.” This hit home. I’ve evaluated side projects as if nights and weekends cost zero, while also claiming I wanted the option to leave a well-paid job. Replacement cost is imperfect, but it forces an honest comparison.

“The uncertainty should change the unit, not make the number disappear” is also strong. A paid diagnostic or bounded first phase maps well to technical consulting, where the unknowns are real and pretending to know the full scope is usually theater.

I also appreciated: “Neither unit is more enlightened.” The distinction between selling a defined outcome and selling access to uncertain effort is more credible than the standard business-book insistence that hourly pricing is always amateurish.

### 3. What didn’t

The Cosmonaut story is interesting but not very transferable to my situation. The author eventually admits that its “first price was chosen by buyers inside a promotional game,” which undercuts how much space it occupies. Kickstarter scarcity, social visibility, an all-or-nothing goal, and a physical consumer product tell me little about pricing a technical diagnostic or B2B service.

“Quote the same offer and price to the next ten comparable buyers” sounds scientific, but “comparable” is doing enormous work. Ten small-business buyers can differ wildly in urgency, authority, trust, budget, and implementation burden. The record would be useful, but ten observations do not isolate price, and the chapter never gives a decision rule for what to do afterward.

The Dutch VAT section lost me completely. I understand why it belongs in a Netherlands-focused book, but as a US reader it interrupts the argument for a jurisdiction-specific compliance aside.

The “Monday” checklist mostly repeats the preceding pages. By then I felt managed rather than informed, especially at “Delete any value claim you cannot connect to one.”

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I thought the chapter’s main claim was “calculate your floor, then test a price.” On the second, I noticed the more valuable claim: choose a unit that assigns uncertainty honestly, and preserve enough consistency to learn from sales conversations. I also noticed how aggressively the author qualifies the evidence. That restraint increased my trust, but it made the central case feel even less capable of supporting the practical method.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the next-ten-customers chapter sounds like where this method either becomes operational or falls apart.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Replace half the Cosmonaut material with one worked B2B technical-service example showing the floor, alternatives, exact quote, ten outcomes, delivery burden, and the specific rule used to revise the price.
