**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I nearly stopped at the Kickstarter. A rubber pen for a tablet, in America, in 2011 — what's that to do with a café in Darlington? But I kept going and by the middle I was nodding. The bit about hours not appearing in the customer's comparison is something I've watched chefs get wrong for twenty-five years and never had the words for.

**2. What landed**

"The buyer is comparing your offer with doing nothing, doing it themselves, hiring somebody else" — yes. Nobody buying a flat white is thinking about my rent. They're thinking about the Costa two doors down and the kettle at home. That's the whole game and he said it plainly.

"Use the cost of replacing the work competently, not the comforting fiction that your own evenings are free." I've seen a dozen owners run themselves into the ground because they never counted their own hours. Blunt, and right.

"A quick yes is not proof that the price was too low." I'd have doubted myself if the first week went well. Good to have that said before it happens.

"A discount is a new offer, not an eraser" — I've done the private-lower-price thing to rescue a table booking. He's right that it teaches you to distrust your own menu.

The Monday list is the most useful page. Short, concrete, no nonsense.

**3. What didn't**

"This distinction matters particularly to you, because you are arriving from salaried technical work." No, I'm not. That line told me straight out the book isn't written for me. I managed restaurants. I'm not turning "competence into a defined change for a defined buyer." I'm selling soup.

The Studio Neat case took up too long for what it gave me. All the pledge arithmetic, then a table of numbers, then a paragraph explaining why the big number doesn't mean anything. If it doesn't mean anything, why am I reading it? The honest bit — "The final total is the glamorous number and the least useful one" — arrives after I've already sat through it.

"The next ten asks test the card, not your nerve." Ten asks? I'll do two hundred on a Saturday. The whole "hold the price for ten comparable buyers" advice is built for someone selling one thing at a time to one person at a time. My problem is a menu of forty items and a room that empties at three o'clock. He does say "quote the same offer" — fine — but nothing here touches that.

The Dutch VAT box: skipped it. Not my country, and it says so itself.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the Cosmonaut story was doing a job I'd missed: selling out and still going under. That is a café. Full room, wrong prices, shut in eighteen months. I've seen it. So the case isn't as foreign as it looked; it's just dressed foreign. I also noticed the "counter-case this chapter does not have" section, which I'd skated past. An author admitting his own example doesn't prove his method — I trusted him more for that, though it's an odd thing to put at the end.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but warily — if the next chapter opens with "since you're a software engineer" again I'll put it down.

**6. One concrete change**

Cut "because you are arriving from salaried technical work" or turn it into "if you are." And give me one line, anywhere, about what "ten comparable asks" means when you sell three hundred small things a day — is it ten days? Ten of one item? Say it.
