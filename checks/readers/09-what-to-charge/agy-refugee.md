### 1. Gut reaction after first read
This chapter felt surprisingly sober, avoiding the loud, empty optimism I usually see in American business books. The instruction to calculate a strict "floor" based on real costs made me feel secure, because with my limited savings, I simply cannot afford to lose money on a bad guess. However, the long story about an American crowdfunding campaign felt completely disconnected from my reality here in the Netherlands.

### 2. What landed — specific passages and why they worked for someone like me
The instruction to include your own labor in the cost floor: "Use the cost of replacing the work competently, not the comforting fiction that your own evenings are free." This was a sharp reminder. In Ukraine, I knew my professional worth, but here, starting over, I am constantly tempted to underprice myself just to get a foothold. The warning that a sale below cost "consumes the business it appears to grow" appeals perfectly to my low tolerance for financial risk. Finally, the "On Dutch ground" box about VAT was a huge relief; it proved the book actually understands the local tax reality I am trying to navigate, rather than just abstract theory.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to
I almost completely tuned out during the Studio Neat example. "They set a $50,000 goal to manufacture it... All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours." This is American lottery thinking. I am not inventing a trendy gadget or raising thousands of dollars online; I am trying to figure out how to charge local clients for administrative services without going bankrupt. I also felt excluded by the assumption that I am "arriving from salaried technical work." My previous salary in Kyiv means nothing in the Dutch market, and my current benchmark is simply covering rent and groceries.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
During the first read, the Kickstarter story irritated me so much that I skimmed the rest. On the second read, I realized the author was actually using that story to prove a point about pricing failure, not to promise overnight wealth. Stripping away my annoyance, I paid more attention to the "Monday" checklist at the very end. It is a highly practical, step-by-step set of instructions that I can actually sit down and use at my kitchen table today to define my first offer. 

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
Yes, because the next chapter is about finding "The First Ten Customers," which is exactly the terrifying next step I need grounded advice for.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Drop the $50,000 Kickstarter story and replace it with an example of an ordinary, boring service business—like an immigrant accountant or a local consultant—trying to price their first small contract without losing their shirt.
