1. Gut reaction after first read

I opened this hoping for a number I can put on a dress tonight, and instead I got two American designers on Kickstarter in 2011. The opening punch — sold out and still short — is sharp, then the chapter spends a long time on a campaign I cannot run from my phone. By the Dutch VAT box I was annoyed. The Monday list at the end is the first part that felt like it was talking to someone who actually has to name a price tomorrow.

2. What landed

“Refusing to name a price until those exist is circular. They are produced by selling, which requires naming a price.” That is my life. I do not have lifetime value. I have WhatsApp.

“Those calculations are necessary. None answers the buyer’s question.” Cost-plus is exactly what I do: fabric, tailor, dispatch, round to a nice number, peek at two other pages. My hours never show up in her comparison. She is comparing me with the girl in Yaba, with sewing it herself, with doing nothing until next month’s salary.

“A discount is a new offer, not an eraser.” In Lagos every chat is “last price.” I have been training people that the caption is a joke. Reduce scope, ask for transfer first, drop the extra wrapping — that I can use this week.

“Cost tells you when to refuse a sale, not what creates the sale.” I have taken orders that looked like growth and then gobbled the profit in POS fees, failed transfers, and a rider who had to go twice because the estate gate was closed.

3. What didn't

The Cosmonaut case is a promotional game with a public goal, scarcity, and press. “A crowdfunding pledge is not an ordinary retail price” — you said it yourselves, then built the whole chapter on it anyway. I sell Ankara two-pieces. Nobody is pledging me to a $50,000 floor.

“This distinction matters particularly to you, because you are arriving from salaried technical work.” I am not. I did not leave a salary to invoice for a report. That sentence made it obvious who this book thinks is holding it.

The Dutch box: “If you sell to consumers in the Netherlands…” I do not. VAT at 21% inside €121 is a different planet from my transfer charges, POS, and a naira that moves while the cloth is still with the tailor. You checked ACM on 2 August 2026 and did not once mention inflation.

“Keep that proposal stable long enough to learn… the next ten comparable buyers.” Ten asks can be two weeks. In two weeks my input costs are not the same number. Holding the price to “learn” can mean selling at last month’s floor.

I also skimmed the Kickstarter table and the “Reading” source notes. They read like a court exhibit, not like help.

4. What changed between read one and read two

First read I almost dismissed the whole thing as Kickstarter tourism. Second read the method is better than the story: one unit, a floor that includes my labour at replacement cost, three real alternatives, one number, record ten. The honest admission at the end — Studio Neat cannot prove this method finds a durable price — made me trust the author more, not less. What still does not hold is the assumption that the floor sits still, and that the reader is a Dutch-adjacent ex-salary consultant.

5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes, because “The First Ten Customers” is the chapter I actually need, but only if it stops assuming Kickstarter, VAT, and a former desk job.

6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change

Cut the salaried-technical-work address and the Dutch VAT box from the main line of the chapter, or replace them with a seller whose costs move weekly and whose customers haggle in chat — and say plainly that “hold the price for ten” has to be shorter when the currency is the thing that will not sit still.
