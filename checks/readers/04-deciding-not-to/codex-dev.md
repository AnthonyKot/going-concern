## 1. Gut reaction after first read

This felt unusually useful for a business book: it gave me a decision mechanism, not motivation dressed as insight. The Cydoc numbers made the danger concrete, although the chapter repeated its thesis enough that I started skimming near the end.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“You are going to be a worse judge of this project later than you are today” landed immediately. I’ve watched multi-year internal projects survive because careers, reputations, and prior commitments became part of the business case. A side business would add my own savings and identity to that mix.

The strongest passage was: “A patent is evidence of novelty, not of demand. A study is evidence of efficacy, not of willingness to pay.” That distinction is obvious once stated, but technically minded founders routinely substitute proof that something works for proof that anyone will buy it. I could easily spend a year building an impressive system because engineering progress feels legible and sales uncertainty does not.

The Cydoc arithmetic was the chapter’s best evidence: roughly $30 monthly contribution against a $4,000–$6,000 integration, producing an eleven-year break-even period per practice. That instantly overruled the emotionally persuasive list of patents, deployments, and clinical results.

I also appreciated “The test is independence, not unfamiliarity.” That is more precise than the simplistic advice to sell only to strangers. A former colleague paying a normal price can be stronger evidence than a random customer receiving a large discount.

Finally, the expanded consequence menu fits my situation. “Return to a salary and continue slowly” recognizes that someone with an established career has options besides heroic commitment or total abandonment.

## 3. What didn’t

The chapter overstates its central claim when it says a future version of me will be “worse-informed about the only question that matters.” Future me will be more biased, but may also know far more about acquisition channels, pricing, and customer behavior. The later “Boundaries” section partly corrects this, but the opening treats bias and information as though they were the same thing.

“Money from a stranger” is memorable but too universal. For enterprise software, regulated products, marketplaces, or developer infrastructure, early evidence may legitimately be paid design partnerships, usage intensity, procurement progress, or technically validated pilots. The text adds qualifications—independence, repeatable price and scope—but the Monday checklist returns to the blunter slogan.

I got bored when the Gymdesk lesson was explained for the third time. The paragraphs beginning “Two claims live in that sentence,” the full Gymdesk section, and “The counter-case this chapter does not have” circle the same qualification. I respect the evidentiary honesty, but the repetition makes the chapter feel defensive.

“Choose someone who will not be kind about it” also struck me as macho business-book phrasing. I want someone dispassionate and willing to challenge my rationalizations, not someone selected for unkindness.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I took the chapter as a strong argument for rigid kill criteria. On the second, I noticed it is actually more cautious: the author admits that a missed threshold may measure an inexperienced operator or an unperformed test rather than absent demand, and explicitly lacks the strongest counter-case. That honesty increased my trust, but it also exposed a gap: the chapter never explains how to set a credible number and date for a market I do not yet understand.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promise of “Leaving Well” directly follows a decision I can realistically imagine facing.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the repeated Gymdesk qualification and use that space for one worked example showing how a salaried technical founder should choose a revenue threshold, deadline, and consequence under uncertainty.
