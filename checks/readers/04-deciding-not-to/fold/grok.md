1. Where each boundary goes, and why that is the first moment it is felt

**Boundary 1 (the rule cannot tell the business from the operator).** It follows the last sentence of the date: “Expect to want to move it. That wanting is data.” That is the first time the chapter puts a clock on the money and admits that the future self will try to move it. A thoughtful reader feels that urge as “I have not yet learned the job” — usually sales and distribution — and wants the extra time for that reason, not because the market has spoken. The boundary has to land here, at full force, because this is the moment the single number is about to be treated as a verdict, and the wanting the paragraph has just named is the confounding the boundary describes.

**Boundary 2 (the rule is only as good as the evidence gathered, not the evidence available).** It follows the last sentence of “Setting the number”: “Neither number is right; both are honest, and both can be failed.” The developer rule just given fails if five companies have not paid by 30 June, including the case in which the developer built until the date and never asked anyone to buy. That is the first moment a reader holding a complete, fail-able rule feels that a miss can be a fact about the calendar rather than about the market. Draelos’s extra year before speaking to a customer belongs here as the real instance of that fail, not earlier as a comment on her story.

Neither number is right; both are honest, and both can be failed. The rule is only as good as the evidence you gathered, not the evidence available. 4. What is lost

What is lost is the halt that presented the two limits as a pair and told the reader to take the rest of the chapter through them; the statements keep their force, but a second-read reader no longer has one place that collects them.
