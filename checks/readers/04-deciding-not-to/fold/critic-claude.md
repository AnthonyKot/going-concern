**1. The rule as written would have stopped Gymdesk — the chapter's own success story.** The Monday template says "if fewer than N customers who could have said no have paid me by [date]." Galperin started with zero customers, revenue arrived months in, and he needed three years. The chapter tries to defuse this by saying his rule was "badly written" and by widening the consequence menu, but that concedes the point: any threshold honest enough to be failed will also be failed by slow-compounding businesses that later work. "Retreat to salary and continue" is presented as a consequence, but in practice it's continuation — so the rule saves face by letting the reader keep going. The chapter has no case of an owner who stopped on a written rule and was right, and admits it has none where stopping was wrong, which means the central instrument is untested in both directions.

**2. The Cydoc diagnosis is hindsight dressed as method.** Draelos's unit economics (~$30/doctor contribution vs $4–6k per integration) only became knowable once hosting/AI costs had risen and EHR integration costs were discovered through doing them. A money-denominated rule in 2019 would have been met by four paying customers and five-figure custom revenue — real money from people who could refuse — and the chapter's own "could plausibly repeat" clause is exactly the sort of judgement call a hopeful founder passes themselves on. The chapter cannot say what N or what date would have stopped her earlier without inventing them; it stops the case at "seven years to ask" and lets the reader assume the rule fixes that.

**3. The "date fixed now, decided while disinterested" premise is doing unearned work.** On day zero the reader knows least about sales cycles, integration costs, and how long anything takes; the chapter admits later-you is better informed on costs but treats today-you as the better judge simply for being uninvested. Enterprise or clinical products routinely have twelve-to-eighteen-month sales cycles; a stranger-proposed date set before learning that is not "honest," it's arbitrary, and the reader who honours it exactly will kill viable businesses or, more likely, learn that their rule was naive and quietly discard the whole discipline.

**Acknowledgement:** Partly, yes — it concedes "That you should decide which one now is a smaller claim, and Gymdesk does not prove it," that "Neither number is right," and records "The counter-case this chapter does not have… No qualifying case has been found" — but it never admits that its own template would have failed Gymdesk, or that Cydoc's numbers were unknowable until late.
