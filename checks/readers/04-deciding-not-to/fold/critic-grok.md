**1. Cydoc does not show that a three-part stopping rule was the missing instrument.** Draelos had four paying customers, deployments, and a known ~$30/month contribution against $4–6k integrations. The failure was unrun unit economics, not a loosely worded “traction by summer” clause. Her own prescription is “ask them to buy it before you build it,” a pre-build test. A rule of the form “if fewer than N independent customers have paid by date D” can be passed by exactly those four sales and still leave the 11-year break-even unasked. The chapter uses seven years of fake-validation to sell a calendar kill-switch that does not force the arithmetic that actually ended the company.

**2. Writing N, a date, and a consequence while “disinterested” is writing them while ignorant, and the “wider menu” then undoes the stop.** Day-zero you do not know cycle time, cost-to-serve, or what a repeatable price is; later-you is compromised on sunk cost but better informed on the business. Gymdesk is the chapter’s own near-miss: a six-month “enough revenue” bound, honoured as written, would have killed a firm that later paid a salary. Once “return to paid work and continue slowly” is a valid honouring of the rule, Cydoc could have “complied” for years. Short-horizon cash tests will also systematically fail long-cycle, regulated, integration-heavy products of the kind the lead case is.

**3. The causal claim the Monday list depends on is stronger than the evidence, and the chapter’s best case is conceded not to prove it.** Both stories are later founder retellings. Gymdesk “does not show that choosing the consequence in advance was necessary.” There is no case of a complete rule, honoured, that should not have been. Absence of that case is not evidence the rule’s false-kill rate is low; it is a selection problem in post-mortems. “Pick one now. Picking now is what makes it possible to honour later” is asserted, then immediately undercut.
