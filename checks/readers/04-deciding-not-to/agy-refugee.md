### 1. Gut reaction after first read
This didn't feel like the typical American startup cheerleading I usually despise. It felt like a survival manual. The idea of planning my exit and setting boundaries before I even start a business gave me a deep sense of relief, because my absolute biggest fear right now is accidentally draining what little savings my family has left.

### 2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me
The distinction between fake progress and real validation hit home: "Money that has cleared, from customers who could comfortably have said no." In my marketing jobs in Kyiv, I saw people get drunk on vanity metrics all the time. Starting over here in the Netherlands, I have zero margin for error and cannot afford to pretend I have a business if I don't. I also appreciated the warning that "A rule with no written consequence does not remove the decision, it defers it to the worst available moment." With my family relying on me, the "worst moment" means not making rent. 

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation
I felt completely alienated by Rachel Draelos’s story when it casually mentioned she "raised $265,000 from family, friends and physicians." My family and friends are trying to survive a war; asking them for a quarter-million dollars is an absurd fantasy. Furthermore, the menu of consequences suggests I could just "return to a salary and continue slowly." This assumes returning to a comfortable corporate job is easy, completely ignoring the reality that foreign credentials like mine are heavily undervalued in the local Dutch market.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I was so distracted by Rachel’s seven-year disaster that I thought the chapter’s only message was a rigid instruction to "quit early." On the second read, I realized I had missed a crucial nuance: "stop is not the only available consequence." Seeing that I could plan to simply "cut the scope to the one part that showed signs of life" made the stopping rule feel less like a death sentence for my ambitions and more like a practical safety net. 

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? 
Yes, because the author treats business failure as a mechanical risk to be managed with simple math rather than a personal character flaw to overcome with blind optimism.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Include at least one example of a business started by someone without a rich network or a lucrative fallback career, to prove how these stopping rules work for people who are starting from zero and literally cannot afford a six-figure mistake.
