**1. Gut reaction after first read**

This one actually spoke to me, which surprised me, because the last thing I want from a business book is a lecture about what I'm worth. The core point — that if I don't put my own wages in the sums, the café can look like it's working when it isn't — landed hard, and I'd never thought of it in those words. But halfway through it stopped being a book for me and turned into a Dutch tax document, and I sat there thinking, I'm in Yorkshire, love.

**2. What landed**

"a badly paid job that also carries the downside of ownership" — that's the sentence. Twenty-five years I've run other people's places and been paid for it. The thing I'm frightened of is doing the same work for less and calling it freedom. He named it in one line.

"The mistake is to ask what you are worth — unanswerable, and slightly demoralising. Ask what the business would have to pay to get the work done." That's the only bit of any business book I've read that made me feel less anxious rather than more. I can price a chef. I can price a cover manager on a Sunday. I know those numbers already.

The three answers are good, and the middle one is right about me: "it looks like the third from the inside and like the first from the outside. Writing the date down is what separates them." I know the feeling of a bad month feeling like the end and looking fine to everyone else.

And "you discover you are doing forty hours of skilled work and twenty hours you could hire for a fraction, and that the second twenty has been crowding out the first" — that's every general manager I've ever known, me included, doing the rota at midnight.

**3. What didn't**

The Dutch box. "Bent u werknemer in een vennootschap of coöperatie waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt?" I don't read Dutch, and there's a page and a half of it, in the language, with article numbers. I skimmed it the first time and skipped it the second. It's the longest section in the chapter and it's the least use to me, and it made me feel like I'd wandered into somebody else's meeting.

"This chapter has no company case… No owner in this book is shown computing their replacement cost and acting on it, and that debt is real." Honest of him, and I respect it, but he's admitted the one thing I wanted. I don't need another rule. I need to watch one woman do it and see the number.

"a management restatement — a copy of last year's profit and loss with one extra line" — I don't have last year's. I haven't opened yet. Nothing in the chapter tells me what to do with a forecast instead of a history, and that's most people buying a first business book.

**4. What changed between one and two**

First time the Dutch section felt like padding. Second time I saw the argument underneath it — one type of owner is forced to price their own labour, the other can pretend it's free, and that's an accident of paperwork rather than sense. That's genuinely interesting. It's just buried under quotations I can't read. I also noticed on the second pass how careful he is that counting the wage isn't drawing it — first time I'd half-read that as "pay yourself properly," which would have been dangerous advice for me in month three.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — and "Enough" is the chapter I actually want, because what the café must cover and what my house needs are two different frights.

**6. One concrete thing to change**

Cut the Dutch quotations to a couple of lines and a link, and use the space for a worked example: one owner, her list of jobs, four real advert rates, the total, the line added, the answer she got. Show me the arithmetic once and I'll do it myself.
