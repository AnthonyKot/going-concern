# Reading as: 24, Lagos, clothes on Instagram and WhatsApp

## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The opening sentence hit me — "a badly paid job that also carries the downside of ownership" is exactly what my aunt says about her shop and exactly what I'm scared of. Then it went to Netherlands, eenmanszaak, gebruikelijkloonregeling, € 58.000, and I was reading someone else's mail for four hundred words. I skipped the Dutch box on read one, and I don't feel bad about it. The Monday list at the end pulled me back.

## 2. What landed

"A business reporting €40,000 of profit before anything is charged for your labour has told you nothing yet." Change the currency and that is my whole life. I count what I made this month as: money in minus what I paid the tailor and the bus and the airtime. Nobody pays me. I thought that was the good part.

"the customer economics of an owner-operated business flatter, and the flattery grows with how hard the owner works" — this is the sentence I'll remember. It says my hustle is hiding the truth, not proving anything.

The three answers, especially the second: "It stops being legitimate when the date is never written down, because then it cannot arrive." I've been in a financed period for two years without knowing it had a name. That reframing alone is worth the chapter.

"you discover you are doing forty hours of skilled work and twenty hours you could hire for a fraction, and that the second twenty has been crowding out the first." I package and I go to the park and I post and I reply DMs at 1am. That twenty is my whole week.

## 3. What didn't

Move 2 is the whole middle of the chapter and it is a foreign tax code. "For an owner who works for an incorporated company in which they hold a substantial interest, the state imposes a statutory method..." I am not incorporated, there is no such rule here, and the sole-trader half — my half — gets three paragraphs whose conclusion is "you're on your own." So the chapter spends its longest section telling me about a discipline I can't have.

"Price each one against several real adverts or quotes. Three or four current rates" — advertised rates for what I do barely exist here, and where they do they're a WhatsApp negotiation, not an advert. And "loaded with what an employer pays on top of the advertised gross" assumes a payroll system I don't touch.

And nothing about inflation. If I price my labour at ₦X today and read the number back in twelve months, the number lied. The chapter treats "last year's profit and loss" as a stable thing to restate. Mine isn't.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

Second read I actually read Move 2's *argument* instead of its facts, and saw the point isn't the Dutch rule — it's "one owner is handed the discipline whether they want it or not, and the other has to supply it themselves." I'm the second owner. That's a real sentence about me. I also caught "Counting it is not paying it," which I'd skimmed as filler and is actually the safety catch: knowing the number doesn't mean draining the account. And the honesty of "This chapter has no company case... that debt is real" made me trust the author more, not less.

## 5. Would I keep reading?

Yes — chapter 16 is called "Enough" and that's the question I actually have.

## 6. One thing to change

Give the sole trader as many words as the bv owner. Move 2's short half needs a real substitute for the missing discipline — how to price your own jobs when there are no adverts, and how to hold the number steady when prices move. Right now the owner with the least help gets the least chapter.
