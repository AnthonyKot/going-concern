## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core argument is strong and uncomfortably relevant: if my hypothetical side business “profits” only because I donate senior-engineer hours, I have built an underpaid job, not necessarily a viable company. But the chapter buries that useful test inside far more Dutch tax detail than I need.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“A business that is profitable only because nobody costed the founder is not profitable” got my attention immediately. I have absolutely evaluated side projects using revenue minus cash expenses while quietly valuing nights and weekends at zero.

“Price the jobs, not the person” is the best practical instruction here. Splitting my work into engineering, sales, support, and bookkeeping avoids the useless question of whether I am personally “worth” my tech-company compensation. The further qualification—costing “the hours a competent replacement would need”—also matters. A bookkeeper would do my bookkeeping faster; another engineer might take longer to understand code I wrote.

The three-answer test is credible because it allows a deliberately subsidized period rather than declaring every early business invalid. “Writing the date down is what separates them” is sharp: a deadline turns founder optimism into a falsifiable plan.

“Counting it is not paying it” also worked. Separating imputed labor cost from household cash withdrawals is exactly the distinction I would want before considering leaving a comfortable salary.

## 3. What didn't

I checked out during the extended Netherlands material. By the time I reached “Article 12a of the Wet op de loonbelasting 1964” and several untranslated statutory quotations, the chapter had shifted from a business argument into jurisdiction-specific documentation. As a US reader, I felt outside the intended audience, and the repeated caveats did not repay the attention required.

The chapter also admits its largest weakness: “No owner in this book is shown computing their replacement cost and acting on it.” That is not a minor “debt”; it means the central method has no demonstrated behavioral outcome. I wanted one worked example showing roles, market rates, loaded costs, adjusted profit, and the resulting decision.

I also doubt that advertised replacement rates fully solve estimator bias. Senior-founder work is unusually bundled and context-heavy. The chapter acknowledges that a successor might “automate, combine or drop” tasks, but the Monday exercise still invites false precision before the operating model has been redesigned.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I reduced the thesis to “pay yourself a market salary,” which sounded simplistic for an early-stage company. The second read corrected me: the author explicitly says to count the cost always but decide separately what to draw. I also appreciated more the warning that replacement means replacing the work system, not cloning the founder—although that caveat deserves to appear earlier and drive the calculation.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the distinction between what the business can cover and what my life requires is exactly the next question I would ask.

## 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change

Replace most of the Dutch statutory box with a one-page, end-to-end case study of a technical founder whose apparent profit changes after pricing each role, including one task automated and one eliminated.
