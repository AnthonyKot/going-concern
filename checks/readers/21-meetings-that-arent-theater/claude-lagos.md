**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I recognised myself in "got a minute?" — my two girls who help me pack and my tailor message me all day and every answer is in my head. But halfway through I realised this whole chapter is one American software company's blog post from 2016, stretched over nine pages. I kept waiting for the part written for a business like mine and it came as one line: "three minutes at opening, standing up." That's the only bit I can actually use, and the author admits he made it up.

**2. What landed**

"A meeting held at the same time regardless is where things get said before they become urgent." That is true and I felt it. Last month my supplier in Aba raised prices and my packer knew four days before I did because nobody had a reason to tell me.

The Friday poker call dying — "We felt guilty doing these calls because we had work we needed to do" — landed hard. I once tried a Sunday voice-note check-in with my two helpers. Nobody said it stopped. It just stopped.

"Whoever is not you and is willing owns the start, the order and the stop." Giving away the clock is a small thing I can actually do without money.

And I respect that the author says outright: "If the interruptions did not fall, the meeting is theatre and this chapter is wrong for your business; drop it and say so." Nobody writes that. Most books never let you conclude they were wrong.

**3. What didn't**

"Put it in the calendar for four weeks before anyone can object to the first one." Whose calendar? My helpers don't have Google Calendar. We run on WhatsApp. The chapter's whole world is a company with a business coach, a retreat, a person named Lesley whose *job* is scheduling meetings, and nine salaried people. That's not a small business, that's a funded startup. Nine people at forty-five minutes is a luxury problem.

"the numbers segment is chapter twelve's weekly cash figure said out loud" — with inflation here my cash figure at Monday opening and Friday closing are different currencies in real terms. A weekly number read aloud daily doesn't mean the same thing when the naira moved 4% that week.

And Move 2 is too long. Four rhythms, four quotes each. I got bored around the Friday poker. Say he tried four, one worked, here's why, move.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read I thought the Groove case was the chapter. Second read I saw the author knows it's thin — "it reports two months and a feeling," "no count of interruptions before and after." So the real chapter is Monday: count, change, count again. That reframing helped a lot. I also noticed the honest line I'd skimmed: "for a business of two, the fixed rhythm may cost more than the interruptions it replaces, and nothing here shows where the line sits." That's me. He told me his own advice might not fit my size and didn't pretend otherwise. Second read I trusted him more and the case study less.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — for the Monday lists and the honesty, not for the case studies.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Cut Move 2 by half and spend the room on the two-or-three-person version. The three-minutes-at-opening line is the only translation to my scale and it's buried mid-paragraph with a hedge on it. Also: don't say "put it in the calendar." Say "pin it in the group chat," or say both — for most of the world the calendar is a chat thread and a phone alarm, and every time a book assumes the calendar I know it wasn't written with me in the room.
