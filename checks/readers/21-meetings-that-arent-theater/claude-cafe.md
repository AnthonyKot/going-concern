**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I came in with my back up — "meetings" is the word I've spent 25 years dodging — and the chapter knew that, which was disarming. The bit where it tells me I already *have* a meeting, it's just called "got a minute?" and I'm the room, that stung in a way I recognised from every Saturday service I've ever run. But it's a long walk through one American software company's blog post to get to three minutes at a counter, and I felt that walk.

**2. What landed**

"A meeting with no start time, no end, no agenda, and always the same attendee." That's my life. I've never had a name for it.

"A meeting held at the same time regardless is where things get said before they become urgent." That's the whole argument in one line and it's true of kitchens — the thing nobody mentions on Tuesday is the thing that ruins Friday.

The Friday poker call dying because "We felt guilty doing these calls because we had work we needed to do" — I've watched exactly that happen with staff socials. The optional nice thing is always the first thing binned. And the fix, giving culture a fixed twenty minutes *inside* a work meeting, is genuinely clever and I'd not have thought of it.

"The point is that you attend rather than run." That one I'll be thinking about. I've never once attended a meeting in my own building.

"Then stop, even if there is time left — especially if there is time left." Good. Blunt. No waffle.

And it admits when it's guessing: "it is a translation, not something the post shows". I trust the author more for that than for anything clever they said.

**3. What didn't**

The whole case study is nine people at screens in different countries. The chapter says so itself — "the post is silent on a team in one room" — which is honest, but honesty doesn't get me a case. I've got seven people, three of them part-time teenagers, arriving at different hours, one of whom will be elbow-deep in a bain-marie at ten past nine. "Same time every working day, at the start of it" — whose start? My chef's start is 7am, my front-of-house's is 11. That's not a small wrinkle, it's the whole problem, and it isn't mentioned once.

"nearly 20 people participated... in less than 7 minutes" — a video, of a company nobody names, shown at a coaching retreat. The author flags this in the Reading note, fair enough, but it's still doing a lot of heavy lifting in the argument.

"chapter seventeen's bottleneck", "chapter twelve's weekly cash figure", "which is where chapter twenty-two starts" — three cross-references in one stretch. I'm reading a chapter, not doing a filing exercise.

Also: "multiply minutes by people and add it up." There it is. The spreadsheet. I knew it was coming.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First time I read Move 2 as a case *for* daily standups. Second time I saw it's actually four failures and one thing that stuck, and the interesting bit isn't the standup — it's that structure beat frequency. More meetings, less time. That's not what I expected and it reframed the lot. Second read I also noticed how much the chapter refuses to claim: "a feeling, not a figure". I respect that, though I did wonder why I'm being sold a rhythm on a feeling.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes — mostly because "Measuring the Right Three Things" sounds like it'll tell me which three, and I'd like someone to finally narrow it down.

**6. One concrete thing I'd change**

Deal with staggered shifts. A café, a workshop, a salon — nobody's people start at the same hour, and "same time every day" quietly assumes they do. Give me the version where the slot sits at a changeover, or where the ones not in get the one line in writing. Without that, item 2 of Monday is undoable for half your readers and they'll put the book down there.
