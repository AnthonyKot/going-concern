## 1. Gut reaction after first read

The core reframing worked: “got a minute?” is already a meeting system, just an expensive and invisible one. I appreciated that the chapter didn’t pretend the evidence was stronger than it was. Still, it spent too long extracting a fairly familiar standup lesson from one founder blog post.

## 2. What landed — specific passages

“Nobody calls this a meeting. It is one: a meeting with no start time, no end, no agenda, and always the same attendee.” That’s sharp and recognizable. I’ve spent years watching senior engineers and managers lose their days to supposedly trivial Slack pings and desk-side questions; the observation scales beyond the tiny owner-operated business being addressed.

“A clock on every segment, not on the meeting” was the most useful operational detail. Plenty of teams schedule a 30-minute meeting and then unconsciously expand every topic to fill it. Time-boxing individual updates is a concrete mechanism, not vague advice to “run efficient meetings.”

I also trusted the author more for saying, “The result he reports is a feeling, not a figure,” and later admitting that the Groove case cannot establish whether this works for a two-person physical business. Business books routinely launder founder anecdotes into laws. Here, at least, the evidence boundary is explicit.

Finally, “count before, count after, and believe the count” gives the reader a reversible experiment with an exit condition. That fits how I’d evaluate a process change at work or in a side business.

## 3. What didn’t

The chapter repeats its thesis too many times. Variations of “the unstructured interruption is already a meeting” appear in the opening, the situation, the case analysis, and the reasoning. By “A meeting held at the same time regardless is where things get said before they become urgent,” I felt the argument had already been closed for several pages.

I also doubted the leap from a nine-person remote software company to “three minutes at opening” for two people at a counter. The author labels it “a translation, not something the post shows,” which is intellectually honest, but honesty does not supply the missing evidence.

“Put it in the calendar for four weeks before anyone can object to the first one” bothered me. That sounds owner-centric and slightly coercive. If the goal is to make the business less dependent on one person, unilaterally imposing a ritual for a month is an odd model of delegated ownership.

The advice also assumes interruptions are batchable. In my world, some blockers genuinely need an answer now because a deploy, incident, or customer escalation is waiting. The chapter needed a rule for distinguishing deferrable coordination from legitimate real-time interruption.

## 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the first read, I mainly saw a long argument for daily standups. On the second, I noticed the more interesting claim: the meeting is an instrumented experiment, with interruption-person-minutes as the metric and removal as the prescribed response if it fails. I also noticed that the author’s caveats do substantial work; without them, the Groove anecdote would not support the small-business prescription.

## 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promised measurement chapter sounds more valuable than another chapter about meeting mechanics.

## 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the chapter by about a third and use that space for a simple triage rule: what waits for the daily slot, what warrants an immediate interruption, and what requires a separate decision meeting.
