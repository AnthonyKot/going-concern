### 1. Gut reaction after first read
At first, it sounded like typical Western corporate nonsense trying to sell a "system," which I usually avoid. But when it described the "got a minute?" interruptions as a hidden meeting where I am the agenda, I felt a sharp sting of recognition. It made me realize that trying to be informal and flexible might actually just create a chaotic environment I cannot afford.

### 2. What landed — specific passages (quote briefly) and why they worked for someone like me
The idea that "A scheduled meeting with no structure is the same theatre with a start time" made perfect sense. At my old marketing job in Kyiv, we had endless, pointless check-ins where everyone just justified their salaries. Because I have very limited savings to risk on starting a business here in the Netherlands, the concept of a strict clock—"sixty seconds per person for done, next, blocked"—appeals to my need for absolute control and efficiency. The instruction to actually tally the interruptions for five days before changing anything is the kind of sober, cautious math I trust over blind optimism.

### 3. What didn't — where I got bored, doubted the author, felt excluded or talked down to, or felt the advice didn't fit my situation
I completely disconnected during the tech startup example about a "Friday call for culture only, a poker tournament over a voice call." I am trying to build a stable income to survive and support my family in a foreign country; I don't have the luxury of time or money for forced workplace fun. Furthermore, the author admits the gap in their own advice: "What this case cannot show is that the rhythm works for a business whose people are not at screens." My business would be a physical service dealing with local Dutch customers, so relying on a remote software team's experience feels entirely alien to my reality.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On the first read, I was focused on learning the mechanics of the proposed meeting structure. On the second read, I noticed how incredibly flimsy the actual evidence is. The author builds this whole rule on a single blog post by a startup founder who reported "a feeling, not a figure" after just two months. As someone who has to calculate every euro carefully right now, realizing this chapter is based on one guy's unmeasured mood made me highly skeptical of the method.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?
I would read the next chapter on "Measuring the Right Three Things" because I am desperate for concrete financial numbers to rely on, provided it isn't based on another tech blogger's feelings.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Stop using remote tech startups as your only examples; give me a case study of a physical shop or a local service business where people are in the same room and dealing with real-world customer interruptions.
