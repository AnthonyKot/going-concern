**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I felt seen and then dropped. The first two pages describe my life exactly — I pay the tailor and the fabric man before any customer pays me — and then the chapter walks off into Instacart and Dutch law and never comes back to a business like mine. By the end I had learned something real, but I had to dig it out from under a lot of pages meant for somebody else.

**2. What landed**

"Growth magnifies the cash cycle, in whichever direction that cycle already runs." That is the whole story of my December. I sold more than ever, made "profit" on every piece, and had no money in January because every naira was sitting in fabric, in the tailor's hands, and in customers who said "I'll pay Friday."

"When customers pay before you incur the costs — deposits… growth can supply cash instead." This is the one I will actually use. I already take deposits on custom orders because I have to; I never understood that this was a *financial strategy* and not just me being desperate. Now I'll take full payment up front on more things, and stop feeling apologetic about it.

"Separate the money that is not yours." I don't collect VAT the way they mean, but I hold money for customers whose orders are not finished yet, and I spend it like it's mine. That sentence stung.

"Can I afford this customer is a different question from is this customer profitable." Yes. The big-order customer who wants credit is the one who nearly finished me.

The "What would prove this chapter wrong" section — an author admitting the advice has a cost, that insisting on deposits can lose you customers. I trust the author more for that.

**3. What didn't**

"The clearest published demonstration is in a document you will never file. Instacart's 2023 registration statement…" — three paragraphs of $242 million and "deferred tax benefit of $373 million." I read it twice and I still could not tell you what a valuation allowance is. Why should I? The author even admits "the case is enormous and the reader is not." So why is it a third of the chapter?

"On Dutch ground" — the whole box. Burgerlijk Wetboek, art. 6:119a lid 5, quoted in Dutch. This is nearly a full page of a chapter I was told was for me. In Lagos the person who owes me money is a girl I know from Instagram. There is no statute. There is no 60-day cap. There is "she blocked me." The author says advice for Americans assumes things that don't exist — well, this assumes things that don't exist even harder.

"Invoice as soon as the contract and the work allow." I don't invoice. Nobody I sell to has seen an invoice. Chapter 13½ is called "The Business That Does Not Invoice" — so the author knows, and still wrote this whole chapter as if I do.

"Hold a buffer sized to the gap you have measured, not to a feeling." In an economy where the naira loses value monthly, holding cash *is* a cost the chapter doesn't mention. My "buffer" is fabric stock, because fabric holds value and cash doesn't.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read, the Instacart section bored me and I skimmed it. Second read I caught the one line that mattered: "a supplier whose receivable is Instacart's 'timing', and who is very likely your size… you are just more often the vendor being timed." That is the point of the whole case and it is buried at the end of it. Second read also showed me the Monday list is genuinely good — the first one (lay ten jobs on one timeline, find the peak overlap) I can actually do with my WhatsApp order history — and the "two bridges" idea is simpler than it looks. But I still had to be told twice.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**

Yes — because the next one is literally titled for me, and I want to see if the author actually meant it.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**

Cut the Instacart numbers to one paragraph ending with "you are just more often the vendor being timed," and spend the saved space on one worked example of a business that takes deposits, holds stock, and gets paid by transfer — no invoices, no statute — so the reader who isn't Dutch and isn't Instacart has something to measure her own gap against.
