**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I'll be honest, I nearly put it down at the Instacart bit. Millions and billions and "valuation allowance on deferred tax assets" — that's not my world and it never will be. But the first paragraph was true, the Tuesday line, and the bit about growth emptying the account is something I have actually watched happen to a man I worked for. So I kept going, grudgingly.

**2. What landed**

"A profitable business can run out of money on a Tuesday." That's the whole thing in one line and I understood it before I'd read another word. I've seen that Tuesday.

"The money that was never yours — VAT collected, payroll taxes withheld — which sits in the account flattering the balance until the day it leaves." Yes. I once watched an owner buy a new coffee machine off a bank balance that was two-thirds the VAT man's. The Monday task about subtracting that money out is the first thing in this chapter I'll actually do.

"Hold a buffer sized to the gap you have measured, not to a feeling." I've been sizing everything to a feeling. Fair cop.

And the last section, where the author says outright "Cash discipline is presented here as free, and it is not." I didn't expect a business book to admit its advice costs something. That earned a bit of trust back.

**3. What didn't**

The Instacart section is a wall for someone like me. "Net income of $428m, less $275m of non-cash items, plus $124m of movements in operating assets and liabilities" — I read that three times and it might as well be in Dutch. Which, later, it actually is. The author says the mechanism "transfers" to a business my size, but I'm being asked to take that on trust after a page of numbers I couldn't follow.

More to the point: this chapter is about invoicing and waiting to be paid. I'm a café. Nobody invoices at a café. They pay at the till, and then I pay the suppliers. The author even says "retail takings" can work the other way — growth supplying cash — in one clause, and then spends the whole rest of the chapter on the invoice-in-March-paid-in-May business. The "Monday" list says "For the last ten jobs" — I don't have jobs. The €36,000 arithmetic, the deposit ladder, "move one invoice earlier this week" — none of it's mine.

The Dutch law box, I skipped entirely. I'm in Yorkshire.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the "two bridges" properly, and the second bridge is mine — "a business can generate operating cash all year while the balance falls because it bought a van." That's the coffee machine again. And I noticed the author's own admission in the boundaries: "no small business in this book is shown measuring its own cash gap." So they know. That made the Instacart stuff feel less like showing off and more like the only evidence they could get their hands on — but it still doesn't help me.

I also noticed the next chapter is called "The Business That Does Not Invoice." So maybe they know that too, and I'm one chapter early.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but only because 13½ has my name on it — if that's another invoicing chapter I'm done.

**6. One thing I'd change**

Put a single café-shaped paragraph in Move 1, right after the March/May example: "If your customers pay at the till, your gap runs the other way — you'll see it in the suppliers' due dates and the VAT quarter, not in receivables." Then I'd have known which half of this chapter was mine before I got to the Instacart wall, instead of finding out from the title of the next one.
