**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I felt like I'd walked into someone else's meeting. Instacart, $373 million, Dutch law in Dutch — none of that is my café. But the bit about VAT sitting in the account "flattering the balance until the day it leaves" made me go cold, because that's exactly what I've seen owners do, twice, and I've watched them get the letter. So I'm irritated and a bit frightened at the same time.

**2. What landed**

"On paper March was a good month. In the bank, March was expensive and May was the relief." That's twenty-five years of my working life in two sentences. I've stood in kitchens where the owner said we'd had a record month and then asked if the wages could go in Friday instead of Thursday.

"Including the money that was never yours — VAT collected, payroll taxes withheld — which sits in the account flattering the balance." I'm going to write that on the wall of the office. The Monday task "Take today's bank balance and subtract the VAT and payroll taxes you are holding for someone else. That smaller figure is what you actually have" — I can do that on the back of an envelope. No spreadsheet.

"Hold a buffer sized to the gap you have measured, not to a feeling." Fair. I've been sizing mine to a feeling.

And I liked "Quoting the Burgerlijk Wetboek at a large customer's accounts department is a way to be paid once and never engaged again." That's someone who's actually dealt with people, not just read about it.

**3. What didn't**

The Instacart section. "net cash provided by operating activities was $99 million, which consisted of net loss of $74 million, adjusted for certain non-cash items of $38 million" — I read that three times and my eyes just slid off. Then the table of six lines. I don't know what a deferred tax benefit or a valuation allowance is, and the author doesn't stop to tell me. I felt like the daft one at the table.

The whole chapter is written for someone who invoices. "For the last ten jobs: the first material cash outflow, every later direct outflow, the invoice date and each receipt." I don't have jobs. People hand me a card, the money's in the bank in two days. The author actually admits this — "The timing half has no reader-scale case" — and then points me to the next chapter, "The Business That Does Not Invoice". So why is that one half a chapter and this one the full one?

The Dutch box. Half a page of Dutch statute I can't read and don't trade under. I skipped it.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I noticed the line I'd skimmed past: "When customers pay before you incur the costs — deposits, prepaid subscriptions, retail takings, a supplier who waits — growth can supply cash instead." Retail takings. That's me. So the danger runs the other way for a café: takings come in and make me feel richer than I am while the brewery, the rent and the VAT are all still owed. The chapter says that but only once, in passing, and I nearly missed it. The Instacart bit also made a little more sense second time — the point is just "the two numbers agreeing is luck" — but it took a lot of reading to get one sentence out.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but only because the next one's title says it's about people like me, and I want to see if the author means it.

**6. One thing to change**

Cut the Instacart quotations down to the one sentence they prove, and put the retail-takings version of the danger — cash arriving before costs, feeling flush while owing the VAT — up front in Move 1 beside the invoicing version, so a shop or a café can see themselves in the first page rather than hunting for one clause in Move 3.
