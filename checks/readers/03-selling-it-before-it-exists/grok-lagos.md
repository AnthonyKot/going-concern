**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I already sell clothes that are not in my hands yet, so the title felt like someone describing my Tuesday and then immediately leaving for Kickstarter. The two-axes thing — they pay, you can actually make it — is the only part that felt like it was written for a person who can go broke. Everything else assumed a platform, a blogger, a factory call, and a lawyer, which is not how I take money on WhatsApp.

**2. What landed**

“Everything people say about wanting your product is free to say.” That is my comment section. People type “I need this in green” and vanish when I send the account number.

The five rungs, especially 1 versus 4: verbal hype versus money they cannot get back. I have been treating “put me down for two” as a sale. It is not.

“A preorder does not return yes or no. It returns a size.” The Glif at 13.7× versus the same people later at 1.24× — that is the first time a book admitted a funded campaign can still be a small answer.

Coolest Cooler: “the best demand evidence anyone in this book obtained and a contract to manufacture sixty thousand complex objects.” I have had a post blow up and then the tailor, the fabric, and the delivery all fail at once. Overshooting as a larger debt, not a win — that is the sentence I would screenshot.

Monday: “If you cannot fill in the date, you do not have a deliverability answer.” And “write down what happens if forty say yes instead of five.” That is the fear I actually have.

**3. What didn't**

“You have spent a career in a job where money was somebody else's department.” I am 24. I am the department. That line is for someone leaving a salary.

Kickstarter as “the instrument.” I do not have that. I have Instagram, WhatsApp, and a transfer that may bounce. Explaining backers and funding goals at that length is a story from another country.

The Dutch box — art. 6:230o, fourteen days, “ask a lawyer.” I cannot afford a lawyer, and my problem is not a cooling-off statute. It is “I have sent it” when they have not, or naira that is worth less by the time the fabric arrives.

“It will be €X.” Even the homework is in euros.

“A working prototype plus a conversation with whoever would have to manufacture it.” Cash-constrained, power cuts, no factory on the other end of an email. That “cheapest form of the question” is not cheap here. Taking money now and delivering later, with inflation, is the second axis on fire, and the chapter never says the word.

The opening (“the instrument that measures demand…”) I had to reread. I got bored in the sources and the “debt stands” ending. It felt like the author proving they are careful, not helping me ask five people for half up front.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

First read I thought this was a Kickstarter chapter with a moral. Second read the Coolest Cooler is the point, and Studio Neat is the setup: same act, two outcomes. I also caught the honest hole — one link from one blog is distribution, not demand — and “a failed presale can be a reach failure,” which is my reach, not my product. The ladder-as-argument, not proven order, made me trust the author more on method and less on whether this sequence fits a clothing page. Deliverability still sits on an American factory timeline. Inflation still is not in the arithmetic.

**5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?**

Yes, because “Deciding Not To” sounds like it might finally talk about a business that should stop, which is more useful to me than another funded campaign.

**6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change**

Replace the Dutch law box and the Kickstarter mechanics with one page that happens on a phone: bank transfer, half up front, a date, what you do when forty people pay and the maker disappears, and what “non-refundable” means when the currency itself is melting.
