### 1. Gut reaction after first read

This is sharper than most “validate your idea” advice because it treats a preorder as both evidence and liability. I trusted the author more for showing the Glif’s distribution advantage and the Coolest Cooler failure, though the chapter felt repetitive and overly universal in places.

### 2. What landed — specific passages

“The instrument that measures demand is the same instrument that creates the obligation” is the chapter’s strongest idea. As an engineer, I recognize this kind of coupled system: the test changes the state of the thing being tested. A successful presale isn’t a green dashboard; it creates customers, deadlines, support load, and reputational risk.

“A preorder does not return yes or no. It returns a size” also worked. Comparing the Glif’s 13.7× result with the same makers’ later 1.24× campaign is much more useful than presenting Kickstarter as a magic validation machine.

I also liked the concrete deliverability checks: “Can it be made at all? At that price? By that date?” That maps well to how I evaluate side-project ideas. I’m rarely worried that I literally cannot build version one; I’m worried that support, integrations, sales effort, and my own time make the promised price irrational.

Finally, “Write down what happens if it works too well” is excellent. Engineers tend to model failure and hand-wave success. The Coolest Cooler example makes oversubscription look like uncontrolled load, which is exactly what it is.

### 3. What didn’t

“Signed intent…is a politeness with letterhead” is catchy but too absolute. In enterprise software, an LOI from the right buyer can require procurement, security, legal, budget ownership, and executive sponsorship. It is weaker than revenue, but sometimes substantially more than thirty seconds of social capital.

“Go to five people…and ask…take the money today” doesn’t fit many mid-market or enterprise products. A willing director often cannot pay today, and forcing an artificial payment mechanism may test corporate purchasing constraints more than demand.

The Dutch consumer-law section lost me. I understand why it exists, but several paragraphs on “art. 6:230o BW” interrupt a broadly framed chapter for a jurisdiction-specific issue irrelevant to my US context. A shorter general warning with jurisdictional notes elsewhere would preserve momentum.

The Coolest Cooler case proves the thesis, but by then the thesis had already been stated repeatedly. I started skimming because the chapter explains the two-axis model, caveats it, operationalizes it, then explains it again through another long case.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two

On the second read, I noticed how often the author limits the claim: the Glif measured exposed demand, the ladder is argued rather than experimentally established, and the missing counter-case is admitted. Those caveats increased my trust. I also became more bothered by the jump from one physical-product case to a general ladder covering enterprise software and consulting.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter?

Yes—the promised case of real recurring revenue with an eleven-year payback is exactly the kind of uncomfortable specificity I want.

### 6. One concrete thing I’d tell the author to change

Cut the Dutch-law box and about half the repeated two-axis explanation, then use that space for one B2B software presale showing how commitment works when a buyer cannot simply “pay today.”
