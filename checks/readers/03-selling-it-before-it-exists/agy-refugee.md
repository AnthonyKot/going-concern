### 1. Gut reaction after first read
Initially, the chapter felt like yet another American business fantasy where people casually raise millions online, which immediately made me defensive. But the core warning about separating "will they pay" from "can you actually deliver it without ruining yourself" hit me hard. It was sobering in a necessary way, because with my family obligations and temporary status here, I absolutely cannot afford to make a financial mistake. 

### 2. What landed 
The stark difference between demand and deliverability felt very grounded. For someone in my position considering a modest consulting business, the idea of testing demand through a "paid discovery — a small fixed-price piece of real work, invoiced first" is brilliant and safe. I am terrified of over-committing, so the warning that "overshooting is not unambiguously good news" because it creates a "larger reading and a larger debt" resonated deeply. It validates my cautious approach to business instead of demanding reckless optimism.

### 3. What didn't 
I felt alienated by the focus on tech accessories and viral marketing. When the author writes, "The Glif was funded inside ninety minutes by one link from one blog," I just sighed. That is internet-lottery luck, completely disconnected from my reality of trying to rebuild an accounting or marketing career from scratch in a new country. Also, the "On Dutch ground" section was intimidating. Reading that "art. 6:230o BW gives that consumer fourteen days to withdraw 'zonder opgave van redenen'" reminded me of the massive bureaucratic and language walls I face daily. It made me feel like one mistake in my terms could legally ruin me.

### 4. What changed between read one and read two
On my first read, I was so distracted by the ridiculous scale of the Coolest Cooler example—thirteen million dollars!—that I almost dismissed the text entirely. On the second read, I realized the scale wasn't the goal; the catastrophe was the point. I saw that the author is actually warning against the exact American hustle culture I despise. The chapter isn't teaching you how to get rich quick; it's teaching you how to avoid destroying your life by selling a promise you can't keep. The "Monday" checklist at the end suddenly looked like a survival tool rather than an academic exercise.

### 5. Would I keep reading the next chapter? 
Yes, because the next chapter is called "Deciding Not To," and a business book that treats walking away as a valid, successful outcome is one I might actually trust.

### 6. One concrete thing I'd tell the author to change
Drop the thirteen-million-dollar Kickstarter cooler and replace it with a normal, boring service business—like a local agency that accidentally sold fifty monthly retainers when the founder only had hours for ten—so readers outside the tech startup bubble can actually see themselves in the failure.
