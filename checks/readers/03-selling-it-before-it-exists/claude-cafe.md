**1. Gut reaction after first read**

I felt like I'd walked into a meeting for the wrong company. Kickstarter, iPhone clamps, sixty-two thousand strangers buying a cooler with a blender in it — I'm going to sell flat whites and bacon butties to people who work within five minutes of the door. But the bit about the man who took $13 million and ended up owing 20,000 people made my stomach turn, because I understood it instantly: he took the bookings and couldn't do the service.

**2. What landed**

"Everything people say about wanting your product is free to say. Evidence begins where saying it starts to cost them." That's twenty-five years of regulars saying "you should open your own place, love" in one line. None of them has put a deposit down.

"Asking feels like a violation — you have spent a career in a job where money was somebody else's department." That's me exactly. I did the rotas, the staff, the front of house. Head office did the money. The author didn't say it to flatter me; he said it to tell me to get over it.

The Coolest Cooler ending: "only one of them felt like winning." I've watched a chef take a 200-cover Christmas party because the number looked good and lose the regulars for a month. The bit about a hundred units on a kitchen table but sixty thousand can't be made "anywhere you have access to" — that's over-booking, and I know it in my bones.

"However long you think the project will take to complete, you may do well to simply double that estimate." The builder quoted me six weeks. I'm writing twelve on the wall.

**3. What didn't**

The "five rungs" and the "second axis" — I lost the thread. "Two questions on two separate axes, and you can pass one while failing the other catastrophically." I had to read that three times, and even then I thought: just say "can you sell it, and can you actually make it." The ladder-versus-axis argument is the author arguing with himself.

"On Dutch ground" — I skipped the whole box. Articles of Dutch law with the Dutch left in. I'm in Lancashire. If the book's written for the Netherlands, tell me on the cover so I stop reading feeling like I've sat in someone else's lesson.

The Monday list: "It will be €X, it will be ready by [date], and I need half up front to start." Who do I say that to? Nobody puts a deposit on a coffee. "Paid discovery… suits most consultancies" — I'm not a consultancy. The Monday page is for someone building gadgets or selling software, and the chapter never once turns and looks at a shop.

**4. What changed between read one and read two**

Second time I saw the honesty I'd missed. "This chapter would be lying if it offered $137,417 as a clean measurement of demand" — he admits one blog link did the work. And "the counter-case this chapter does not have… the debt stands." I've never seen anyone in a business book say "I haven't proved this." That bought him a lot of trust. I also noticed the line "a non-refundable payment from someone who is not your customer is worth less than a refundable deposit from someone who is" — which quietly saves the whole thing for me. My mate's £50 means nothing; the office manager across the road ordering twenty sandwiches on a Tuesday means everything.

Also, second time, the Cydoc line — four customers paying full price and an eleven-year payback — is the scariest thing in the chapter and it's thrown away in one sentence.

**5. Would I keep reading?**

Yes, but only because chapter four is called "Deciding Not To," and I want to know if he's got the nerve to tell me that.

**6. One thing I'd tell the author**

Put a shop, a café, a plumber — anyone who sells a thing you can't preorder on the internet — into the Monday list. Tell me what rung four looks like when the product is a table and a kettle. Otherwise every reader like me finishes the chapter agreeing with you and with nothing to do on Monday morning.
