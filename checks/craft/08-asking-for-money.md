# Craft review — Chapter 08, "Asking for Money"

Reviewed 2026-08-26. Craft only; all figures assumed correct. Findings ranked by impact.

The short version: this is a chapter with real conviction in it. The lede ("Every closed tab
is a no with no reason attached") is the best in Part II; "You are not too sophisticated for
it; you are avoiding it" is exactly the register the book promises; the limit block — "the
part that did not scale was him" — is the finest use of that device so far, and its closing
concession ("one fortnight, one company, self-attributed") shows the discipline the
constitution asks for. The failures are two families. First, the known ones: the opening
flinches to third person on its sharpest line, and the narrator surfaces twice in the first
person, which the voice rules flatly forbid. Second, and specific to this chapter: the
Monday list ignores the chapter's own precondition, and Move 2 trusts Barry's sentences more
than its own — fourteen quotations stitched with splices, so the load-bearing story reads as
an annotated source rather than a thing being told.

---

## 1. Monday tells you to send emails without the list the chapter just called mandatory

**Quote:**
> **Send twenty emails this week.** One question: what is frustrating you about [the thing
> they use now]. No pitch, no price, no link. Send them before Friday.

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** Move 3 says, in its own words, "An instruction to send emails is worthless
without a list," and spends a whole subhead on building one — narrow past comfort, enumerable
by hand, "a definition narrow enough to be a *list* is a definition you can act on Monday
morning." Then Monday opens with the exact instruction the chapter called worthless: send
emails, no list-building step anywhere in the five items. The chapter contradicts its own
method at the precise point where the reader acts on it. This is the same class of defect as
the pilot's best finding, and it needs no new facts — the missing action is sitting in Move 3.

**Rewrite** (replaces item 1; the merged send instruction absorbs "Send them before Friday"
so no item count changes):

> **Write the list before the first email.** A hundred names, by hand, from chapter six's
> definition — if you cannot finish it, the definition is still too wide. Then send twenty
> of them one question before Friday: what is frustrating you about [the thing they use
> now]. No pitch, no price, no link.

---

## 2. The opening line accuses the reader in the third person

**Quote:**
> This is the chapter the reader of this book will skip, and the skipping has a respectable
> vocabulary.

**Quote (same flinch, mid-move):**
> The reader's first objection is not one of Barry's three.

**Lens:** The reader in the chair.

**What's wrong:** Known failure mode 3, on the most load-bearing sentence in the chapter.
The line is an accusation, and it is a good one — but it is aimed over the reader's shoulder
at "the reader of this book," a species the person in the chair can decline to belong to.
The template says Move 1 opens on the reader's actual position, second person. Said to the
face, the same sentence lands as being seen; said about a third party, it lands as a lecture
about other people's weaknesses. The second instance repeats the dodge at the moment the
chapter anticipates the reader's own thought.

**Rewrite:**

> This is the chapter you were going to skip, and the skipping has a respectable vocabulary.

> Your first objection is not one of Barry's three.

---

## 3. The narrator surfaces in the first person, twice

**Quote:**
> Barry puts it better than I can:

**Quote:**
> — the growth that direct selling produced did not continue in his absence, on the one
> occasion the record lets us look.

**Lens:** Warmth without slack.

**What's wrong:** The invisible-narrator rule is settled, and both of these break it — the
first with an outright "I," inside Move 1's central argument paragraph, the second with an
"us" on the last line of the chapter's best block. Both are reaching for warmth
(self-deprecation, companionship) through exactly the channel the book has closed. The warmth
they want is available through word choice alone.

**Rewrite:**

> Barry's version is blunter:

> — the growth that direct selling produced did not continue in his absence, on the one
> occasion the record allows a look.

---

## 4. The shut-down-or-double-down moment gets one clause

**Quote:**
> The decision he then faced was chapter four's, arriving on schedule: *"Either give
> ConvertKit the attention it deserved or shut it down"*. He doubled down, and did two
> things at once.

**Lens:** Story craft.

**What's wrong:** Known failure mode 1, localized to the hinge of the case. This is the
moment the whole chapter turns on — eighteen months of decline, $1,207, a man deciding —
and the deciding takes four words. The reader never stands inside the version where he shuts
it down, even though the pull toward that version is already on the page: one paragraph
earlier, the books "were earning well." A fact placed at the decision instead of before it
buys the moment of not-knowing. Zero new facts; the cost is one redundant clause elsewhere
("and that was the high-water mark" duplicates the very next quote, "$2,480 was the peak
month" — cut it).

**Rewrite:**

> The decision he then faced was chapter four's, arriving on schedule: *"Either give
> ConvertKit the attention it deserved or shut it down"*. Shutting it down was the
> defensible choice — the books were paying and the tool was not, and had not been for
> eighteen months. He doubled down anyway, and did two things at once.

## 5. The drafting history surfaces at the climax of Move 2

**Quote:**
> And the number this book was told did not exist in public anywhere:

**Lens:** Story craft.

**What's wrong:** Known failure mode 2. "This book was told" is the research process — some
exchange between drafts and reviewers — leaking into the case at its single most important
beat, the hundred-emails yield. The reader has no idea who told the book what, and the frame
tries to borrow drama from the book's own sourcing effort instead of from the number. The
number needs no help.

**Rewrite:**

> And the yield:

## 6. "The ninety-three" — false precision that contradicts the chapter's own premise

**Quote:**
> None of that is available to someone reading a funnel report. It is available to someone
> who asked a hundred people and listened to what the ninety-three said.

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** This is the closing line of the chapter's best paragraph — the ask → reason
→ removal → retention sequence — which is why a small false note here outranks larger ones
elsewhere. "Ninety-three" manufactures a figure the source doesn't carry (the yield was
"5–7," so the nos are 93–95, and picking one end is decoration), and it quietly overclaims
against the chapter's own thesis: of a hundred *emails*, the ones who never replied said
nothing at all. A silent inbox is a closed tab. The chapter's distinction is between silence
and a sentence, and the fix is to land on that distinction.

**Rewrite:**

> None of that is available to someone reading a funnel report. It is available to someone
> who asked a hundred people and listened to the ones who said no.

---

## 7. "The mastermind decisions" — a detail pasted in with its context left behind

**Quote:**
> So they moved again — the mastermind decisions were to *"Rebrand ConvertKit to be"* email
> marketing for professional bloggers, and to *"Focus even more on direct sales."*

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** Known failure mode 5. "The mastermind" arrives with a definite article and
no introduction — the reader has met no mastermind. It is a term carried over from Barry's
article with the surrounding furniture left behind, and it reads as a summarizer's shortcut.
Unpacking the word ("his mastermind group") adds no new fact; it glosses one already on the
page.

**Rewrite** (same length; the splice around "Rebrand" is repaired in the same pass):

> So they moved again — his mastermind group's decisions: rebrand as email marketing for
> professional bloggers, and *"Focus even more on direct sales."*

## 8. Quote-splices and a filler quotation in Move 2

**Quote:**
> The first was positioning, and he is blunt about the diagnosis: *"if your market
> definition has the word"* *anyone* in it, you will probably fail.

**Quote:**
> then *"we continued to execute on the same strategy and grew 27% up to $2,100."*

**Lens:** Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong:** The first quotation ends mid-clause, one word before its own point, so the
sentence stutters exactly where it should snap. The second quotes eight words of corporate
filler — "we continued to execute on the same strategy" — that a writer telling this story
would never keep; only the number is load-bearing, and the number is already in the figures
strip. Move 2 carries fourteen quotations; these two are where the stitching shows worst.

**Rewrite:**

> The first was positioning, and he is blunt about the diagnosis: a market definition with
> the word *anyone* in it will probably fail. He chose email marketing for authors. *"By the
> end of October MRR had grown 23% to $1,646"*, then another 27% in November, to $2,100.

## 9. Move 1's thesis lands on a referent the reader has to reconstruct

**Quote:**
> Against which: *"The basic rules of social interactions require them to give you a
> reason"* as to why they will not buy. That asymmetry is the whole argument.

**Lens:** Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong:** "Against which" points back past two paragraphs of word-of-mouth argument
to the closed tab from three paragraphs earlier, and "that asymmetry" names a comparison
whose two poles were never put in the same sentence. This is the paragraph the whole move
exists to deliver, and it asks the reader to assemble it. Both halves are already on the
page; put them side by side.

**Rewrite** (same length):

> Set the closed tab against a person you have asked: *"The basic rules of social
> interactions require them to give you a reason"* why they will not buy. That asymmetry is
> the whole argument.

---

## 10. The editorial aside deflates the $50,000 moment

**Quote:**
> *"on January 1st, 2014 I invested $50,000 into ConvertKit"*, a date which is his slip for
> 2015 and is left as he wrote it — and hired a developer.

**Lens:** Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong:** The boldest single act in the recovery — real money down after eighteen
months of decline — is interrupted mid-sentence by fourteen words of textual criticism. The
sourcing standard requires the flag, and the Reading list already carries it in full, with
the reasoning. The prose needs only the smallest visible mark.

**Rewrite:**

> *"on January 1st, 2014 I invested $50,000 into ConvertKit"* (his slip for 2015; see the
> reading list) — and hired a developer.

---

## Overall note

The single change that would most improve this chapter: **retell Move 2 in the book's own
voice, and keep quotation only where the exact wording is load-bearing.** The move currently
runs on fourteen quotations — splice-quotes, filler-quotes, quotes of things any narrator
could say better — and the effect is a writer standing behind Barry's article pointing at
passages, rather than a person telling a story they know cold. The quotes that must survive
verbatim earn their marks precisely because the rest are released: the goal, "$2,480 was the
peak month," the shut-down ultimatum, "been trying to get you to do that for over a year,"
and the hundred-emails yield. Everything else — the strategy filler, the spliced diagnosis,
the mastermind fragment — should be told, not exhibited. Findings 4, 5, 7 and 8 are all
local symptoms of this one condition; fixing it as one pass also opens the room for the
decision beat in finding 4, which is the moment the chapter most needs the reader to live
through.
