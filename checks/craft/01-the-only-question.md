# Craft review — Chapter 01, "The Only Question That Matters"

Reviewed against CONTEXT.md §3 (template) and §4 (voice). Facts assumed correct; this is craft only.
The chapter is strong — the case is genuinely load-bearing, the Monday list is concrete enough to
fail at, and several lines ("a hobby with an accountant", "the advice does not soften; it hardens",
"Then the Webstore opened... and the orders were not enough") are the book at its best. Seven
findings, ranked.

---

## 1. The case runs backwards through its own hinge

**Quote:** "In July 1999 it signed an agreement with the engineering firm Bechtel to build up to **26 more** of them over three years, a programme the company estimated at approximately $1.0 billion, with each centre costing $30–35 million." / "Then the Webstore opened, on 2 June 1999, and the orders were not enough."

**Lens:** Story craft

**What's wrong:** "Then" is doing false work — June comes before July, so the reader who is tracking dates (this book trains them to) stumbles exactly at the chapter's best sentence. And the inversion buries the most vertiginous beat in the case: the company signed for twenty-six more centres *weeks after* taking its first order, on almost no data. Told in lived order, the signing becomes a decision made under uncertainty; told in this order, it's an exhibit filed before the story starts.

**Rewrite (swap the two paragraphs, adjust the joins):**
> The Webstore opened on 2 June 1999. The following month, with the first orders barely counted, Webvan signed an agreement with the engineering firm Bechtel to build up to **26 more** of them over three years, a programme the company estimated at approximately $1.0 billion, with each centre costing $30–35 million.
>
> The orders were not enough.

Cuts "In July 1999" and "Then... on 2 June 1999, and"; adds "The following month, with the first orders barely counted" — net length the same, and "The orders were not enough" now stands alone, which it earns.

---

## 2. The drafting room door left open

**Quote:** "*Usually*, and not *strictly superior*, which is what an earlier draft said. The stronger phrase would claim variable wins in every state of the world, and the debt this chapter carries is precisely a state where it does not... That is a reason to keep looking, not a licence to promote the claim while nobody is checking. Worth the premium is a statement about the odds, which is all the evidence below supports."

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear

**What's wrong:** "which is what an earlier draft said" and "while nobody is checking" are the book's revision history surfacing in the prose — a narrator admission with the pronoun filed off. A practitioner states the boundary and what supports it; only an author discusses their drafts. The epistemic honesty must stay (and the `.owed` block already carries the debt visibly); the archaeology should go.

**Rewrite:**
> *Usually*, not *always*. The stronger claim would be that variable wins in every state of the world, and there is a state where it does not: a business that commits early to fixed capacity and takes a market a variable competitor could not have served. No checkable case of it has been found — a reason to keep looking, not a licence to strengthen the claim. *Worth the premium* is a statement about the odds, and the odds are all the evidence supports.

Shorter than the original.

---

## 3. Nobody decides anything in the case

**Quote:** "The bet was on automation. Rather than picking orders off shop shelves, Webvan built purpose-designed distribution centres."

**Lens:** Story craft

**What's wrong:** Borders appears once, as a credential, and never acts again; from here the case is a company and its filings. The fatal choice the chapter itself names — sizing a building for 8,000 orders a day before a single customer had placed one — happens with nobody on stage. One possessive fixes it without heroizing anyone: the bet gets an owner, and "no hero founders" survives because he is only ever shown deciding, not being brilliant.

**Rewrite:**
> Borders' answer was automation. Rather than picking orders off shop shelves, Webvan built purpose-designed distribution centres.

---

## 4. "Most owners" lets the reader out of the chair

**Quote:** "Most owners could not state that number for their largest fixed cost. It is worth ten minutes to find out, because it converts a vague anxiety into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic problems can be solved."

**Lens:** The reader in the chair

**What's wrong:** At the exact moment the principle should land on this reader's lease, the prose turns away to survey a population. "Most owners" is the lecture register; the reader gets to nod along about other people. The sentence right before this one was in second person and should stay there.

**Rewrite:**
> Most owners could not state that number for their largest fixed cost. Ten minutes with the one in front of you converts a vague anxiety into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic problems can be solved.

Shorter; cuts "It is worth ten minutes to find out, because it".

---

## 5. "We" walks on stage

**Quote:** "This is the least informative sentence in this book, and we will come back to it in chapter two."

**Lens:** Warmth without slack

**What's wrong:** The only first-person in the chapter. "We will come back" is a tour guide's voice — a narrator surfacing, however mildly. The book's forward references elsewhere ("Which is chapter three.") already show the invisible form.

**Rewrite:**
> This is the least informative sentence in this book; chapter two is about why.

---

## 6. The scaffolding announces itself

**Quote:** "Three consequences follow, and they are the working content of this chapter."

**Lens:** Rhythm and music

**What's wrong:** "the working content of this chapter" is the template describing itself — and it's the third "this chapter" self-reference in five paragraphs ("the subject of this chapter", "keeps this chapter honest" follows soon after). The headed subsections make the structure visible on their own; saying so is a seam.

**Rewrite:**
> Three consequences follow.

---

## 7. The core trade-off arrives in a knot

**Quote:** "A worse per-unit economics that you can switch off is, in the pre-evidence phase, usually worth the premium over a better one you cannot."

**Lens:** Rhythm and music

**What's wrong:** This is the sentence the whole subsection exists to deliver, and it's the hardest one in the chapter to parse: a singular article on "economics", a qualifier parked mid-clause, and "a better one you cannot" forcing a re-read to recover the elided verb. "The pre-evidence phase" is also coined jargon where plain time-order would do.

**Rewrite:**
> Before the evidence is in, worse unit economics you can switch off are usually worth the premium over better ones you cannot.

---

## Overall note

Retell Move 2 strictly in the order the people inside lived it, with the decider on stage. The
facts are already almost in sequence — premise, bet, capacity, launch, the numbers arriving, the
collapse — but the one inversion (finding 1) and the vanishing founder (finding 3) mean the reader
receives the case pre-sorted into exhibits rather than passing through June 1999 believing, as the
company did, that the building would fill. The chapter's stated ambition is that this failure "is
difficult to recognise from inside"; the case will prove that sentence, instead of merely preceding
it, once the reader is put inside for the length of one summer.
