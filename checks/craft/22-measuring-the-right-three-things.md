## Does it read like a person or a machine? A person, with a good ear for the aphorism, who has not yet checked what the rest of the book already put in the reader's thirty seconds.

Move 1 is exact: "You know the number … the person you hired hears the same figure and knows only
that it is a number" is the reader's square, and "what happens to a number once the people reading
it are also the people being judged by it" plants the second half of the chapter without announcing
it. The rule passes §6d in one sentence — *three figures under the total, defined in writing before
anyone is judged by them, read weekly by someone the numbers do not pay* — and Move 3's boundary
paragraph gives its limit in the same breath ("worth nothing for the fourth thing you did not
measure"). The §6i compression is handled at the point it is made: "The compression that follows is
this book's" sits directly after the seventh figure, and the owed block is the most honest in the
part — it says what the post cannot supply and hands the counter-case to Monday 5. Monday 5 is the
chapter, and it is failable.

Two problems carry the weight, and neither is about Barry. First, the chapter forgets what the
thirty seconds already contain. Ch. 12 built a weekly dashboard of *three* figures — available cash,
committed demand, an operating quantity that leads revenue — and ch. 21 put ch. 12's cash figure into
the meeting, read aloud by *you*. Ch. 22 opens by attributing "someone who is not you" to ch. 21,
proposes a different three, keeps cash as a fourth ("not left out at all") and then in Monday 4 tells
the reader that a fourth number means taking one away — while pointing the cash figure at "chapter
thirteen", which is the chapter that says the *previous* one asked for it. A reader who has done
ch. 12 now has six weekly numbers and no sentence reconciling them (finding 1). Second, the
"measure becomes target" argument, which is the chapter's own and is good, is built on two glosses
the post does not bear: the annual-plan push is counted as two independent movements when it is one
event seen through three windows, and it is called a change "without the business changing" when
customers paying a year up front is exactly the business changing (finding 3); and Barry's own
"push that down to 0%" is turned into a target "a definition, a discount or a window can hit for
you" — but a discount *raises* contraction and so raises net revenue churn; it cannot hit 0% for
anyone. The one person in the post who fixed his definitions in the open, on a dashboard "all
calculated exactly the same way", is presented as the example of the danger. That is the chapter
using his transparency against him, and the arithmetic does not even support it (finding 2).

Sweep of the known modes. (1) verdict-first: "It is also, unusually, a list of the questions a
mature owner asks" precedes the list; "A number nobody had to falsify" closes Move 2 as the book's
verdict on an S-1 the reader has not seen (findings 2, 8). (2) apparatus: "this chapter reads it as
that"; "This is the book's selection, not Barry's" — the §6i sentence is said in Move 2, Move 3, the
owed block and Reading, four times for a rule that asks for once (finding 7). (3) flinch: "Three is
also a claim about the reader's size"; "the figure most owners can state and least like to" (finding
9). (4) counting and self-announcing: "Then three more", "Seven things, then, not three", "And the
post shows something else, in passing, that the chapter's title is really about" (finding 8). (5)
summariser's seams: "A total rising smoothly", "unusually", "nearly dead", "on a sales calendar",
"without the business changing" (findings 3, 8, 10). (6) source accounting inside the argument:
Move 2's first paragraph carries Reading's evidence-class sentence (finding 7). (7) not applicable.

Word count: 2,412 rendered (2,227 in the prose body) against a 3,000 ceiling. Findings 1 and 6 add
about 60 words; findings 3, 7, 8 and 9 remove about 90.

## 1. The chapter does not know what ch. 12 and ch. 21 already put in the thirty seconds, and points the cash figure at the wrong chapter

**Quote** — Lede: *"Thirty seconds of numbers, every morning, read aloud by someone who is not you —
that was chapter twenty-one's instruction, and it leaves open which numbers."* — Move 3: *"the cash
figure is not left out at all, it is the one you already read weekly from chapter thirteen and it
measures survival, not the business."* — Monday 4: *"If you find yourself adding a fourth number to
the morning three, take one away."* — Decision: *"Which three figures the business reads every week
without you in the room"*.

**Lens** — Continuity (ch. 12, 13, 21) / Monday concrete enough to fail at.

**What's wrong** — Ch. 21's Monday 1: "Then thirty seconds: this week's cash figure from chapter
twelve, read aloud *by you*." The "someone who is not you" is this chapter's proposal (Monday 3),
not ch. 21's instruction; the lede attributes it backwards. Ch. 13 opens "the previous chapter asked
for a weekly cash figure separately from profit" — the figure is ch. 12's, and there is no link on
"chapter thirteen" in the HTML (line 153). More seriously, ch. 12 asked for three weekly figures —
"one liquidity measure, one committed-demand measure, and one operating quantity that leads
revenue" — and this chapter proposes three different ones without a sentence saying how they
relate, then says cash stays as well, then forbids a fourth. And the cadence wavers: "every morning"
(lede), "every week" (Decision), "read weekly" (Move 3), "the morning three" (Monday 4). Ch. 21's
meeting is daily; ch. 12's figures are weekly. The reconciliation the reader needs is one sentence:
the three are weekly figures, said at the daily meeting, and they sit *under* ch. 12's three, which
are the same three from the other side — ch. 12's committed demand is the movement figure before it
arrives, ch. 12's operating quantity is it arriving. That is a zero-fact fix; the material is all in
ch. 12.

**Rewrite** — Lede: *"Thirty seconds of numbers, every morning — that was chapter twenty-one's
instruction, and it leaves open which numbers, and who reads them."* — Move 3, "What is left out"
paragraph: *"the cash figure is not left out at all: it is the one chapter twelve already has you
reading weekly, it measures survival rather than the business, and it stays where chapter twenty-one
put it. Chapter twelve's other two — committed demand and the quantity that leads revenue — are the
movement figure before it lands; if you have kept them, the first of this chapter's three is already
half filled in."* — Monday 4: *"If you find yourself adding a number to the three and the cash
figure, take one away."* — Decision, first clause: *"Which three weekly figures the business reads
at the morning meeting without you in the room"*. Add the ch. 12 link at line 153.

## 2. "Push that down to 0%" is turned into a gameable target, and the discount the sentence names would move the figure the other way

**Quote** — Move 3, "The moment a measure becomes a target": *"The S-1 company did not lie; it drew
the definition of churn where it wanted the number to come out, and Barry's own stated goal for the
year — push net revenue churn "down to 0%" — is exactly the kind of target that a definition, a
discount or a window can hit for you while the customers go on leaving."*

**Lens** — Is the "measure becomes target" argument built from the source or asserted over it /
financial-literacy precision (§6i) / fairness to the case.

**What's wrong** — Three things. (i) The arithmetic: the post says the annual-plan discount is a
*contraction*, and "revenue churn also includes contraction, so it spikes when you do a big push on
annual plans". Net revenue churn nets expansion against cancellations and downgrades; a discount adds
to the downgrades. A discount therefore pushes net revenue churn *up*, away from 0%, not towards it.
Of the three levers named, only a definition and a window could flatter the figure; the discount is
the one Barry shows *hurting* it. (ii) The fairness: Barry is the one who explained the window, the
discount and the S-1 definition, and he tells the reader to compare only figures "calculated exactly
the same way". His 0% goal is stated on a public dashboard whose definitions he does not control —
Baremetrics computes them — which is precisely the defence the next paragraph prescribes ("fix the
definition before anyone is judged by the figure, in writing"). Using his goal as the specimen of the
danger, one sentence after "Barry knows this, which is why he can read his own numbers", is the
chapter contradicting itself to land a line. (iii) The motive: "drew the definition of churn where it
wanted the number to come out" is an intention the post does not assert — Barry says "That's
convenient", which is a raised eyebrow, not a finding. The argument does not need Barry as its
example; it needs the reader as its example, and the S-1 as Barry gave it.

**Rewrite** — *"The S-1 company did not lie; it had a definition of churn under which a customer
leaving in the first ninety days was not a loss, and Barry's word for that is "convenient". The
target that goal-sets against such a figure — his own for the year is net revenue churn "down to
0%", on a dashboard whose definitions he does not set — is safe only for as long as the definition is
not the goal-setter's to move. Yours will be."* (Drops the discount from the list of levers; keeps the
window and the definition, which the post does support.)

## 3. One event is counted as two, and customers paying a year up front is described as the business not changing

**Quote** — Move 2: *"Twice in it a number moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the
business getting better or worse."* — Move 2: *"Same business, same customers, three figures swinging
on a sales calendar."* — Move 3: *"Every figure on that dashboard is honest, and two of them moved
without the business changing: an annual-plan push made net revenue soar and MRR fall in the same
month, and a thirty-day window made churn spike and then recover on a calendar."*

**Lens** — Story craft (inference inside the source) / "measure becomes target" built from the source.

**What's wrong** — The post describes one event: the November annual-plan push. It appears in net
revenue (up), MRR (down, because the annual price is discounted), and revenue churn (up, because the
discount is contraction; then down when "the contraction from annual plans is now outside the rolling
30 day window"). The window did not "make churn spike" — the discount did; the window only sets when
it stops showing. So "twice" and "two of them" count one cause as two, and Move 3's list is the same
event split in half. And the event is not the business staying still: several thousand customers
committing a year's money in advance at a discount is a change in the business — in its cash, its
commitment and its churn exposure — which is why Barry tracks net revenue and MRR separately in the
first place. What the post shows is stronger and simpler than the chapter's version: *one real event,
three figures, three different directions* — which is the whole case for reading the components and
not the total, and the whole case for fixing definitions. "Sales calendar" is a fair gloss of "Each
November … over Cyber Monday" and can stay.

**Rewrite** — Move 2: *"Once in it, one event moves three numbers three different ways. Each November
ConvertKit pushes annual plans, sold at a discount: net revenue that month climbs "as high as $3
million", while MRR drops, because the discount is a contraction — and revenue churn spikes with it,
then falls again when "the contraction from annual plans is now outside the rolling 30 day window."
Same business, same customers, one decision, three figures swinging on a sales calendar."* — Move 3:
*"Every figure on that dashboard is honest, and one November decision moved three of them in three
directions at once — net revenue up, MRR down, churn up and then down again on a thirty-day window —
while the customers themselves were the same people, now paying yearly."*

## 4. "He reads it as a health check in both directions" attaches an ARPU sentence to the concentration figure

**Quote** — Move 3: *"Barry's is a third of the customers for two thirds of the revenue, and he reads
it as a health check in both directions — he wants the average to rise, but not so far "that we are
beholden to a few customers.""*

**Lens** — Story craft: every gloss of a figure checked against the figure.

**What's wrong** — The quotation is from the ARPU section ("Personally I'd like to see our ARPU climb
to closer to $75–100 … but not see it so high that we are beholden to a few customers"). It is about
average revenue per user, which the chapter has just demoted to the monthly page as "arithmetic on
the first two figures". The 80/20 section that follows it contains no evaluation at all — Barry gives
the split and explains it ("customers paying everything from $29 up to $5,000") and stops. "He reads
it as a health check in both directions" is therefore the chapter's reading of a figure Barry does not
read, supported by a quotation about a different figure. The "both directions" point is a good one
and the quotation does support it — for the average. Say so, and let the concentration figure be what
the post makes it: a split he reports without a verdict.

**Rewrite** — *"Barry's is a third of the customers for two thirds of the revenue, and he gives the
split without a verdict. The verdict he does give is on the average — he wants it to rise, "but not
see it so high that we are beholden to a few customers" — and that is the same worry from the other
end: the average can rise because everyone pays more, or because a few pay for everyone."*

## 5. "Monthly is as granular as you need to go" is used twice, both times outside its sentence, and the chapter's weekly movement figure contradicts it without saying so

**Quote** — Move 2: *"On how often to look: "Monthly is as granular as you need to go.""* — Move 3:
*"Those go in the monthly look — Barry's own "as granular as you need to go" —"*.

**Lens** — Inference inside the source / lens (d), the translation marked as the book's.

**What's wrong** — Barry's sentence is about MRR movement, and its context is evaluating a company
from outside: "I also often look at the daily breakout … but that's more in the weeds and I'd never
look at that to evaluate another company. Monthly is as granular as you need to go." He does not say
ratios or averages belong on a monthly page; and for the movement figure — the chapter's *first*
weekly number — he says monthly suffices. So the chapter quotes him for the cadence of the figures it
demotes, and against the cadence of the figure it promotes, and never addresses the second. The
chapter's weekly cadence is defensible on its own ground (ch. 12's cycle-length rule: "a cash-tight
retailer may need to look daily"), and it should stand on that ground, marked as the book's, rather
than lean on a sentence that points the other way.

**Rewrite** — Move 2: cut *"On how often to look: "Monthly is as granular as you need to go.""* and
carry the quotation to Move 3's boundary paragraph: *"Three is also a claim about your size, and so
is weekly. Barry looks at his breakout monthly — "Monthly is as granular as you need to go" — and a
business of ConvertKit's shape has the staff to read seven and the churn to need them; at two or five
people, on chapter twelve's rule, the cadence follows the operating cycle, and for most readers of
this book that is the week."* — Move 3, "What is left out": *"Those go in the monthly look, and they
stay out of the thirty seconds because a person reading seven numbers aloud is reading none of
them."*

## 6. Monday 1 leaves "the top" open, Monday 2 fixes it at ten, and Move 3 says the figure is customers named, not a percentage — then Monday 2 asks for a share

**Quote** — Move 3: *"the concentration figure is read as customers named, not as a percentage."* —
Monday 1: *"and which customers make up "the top"."* — Monday 2: *"the share of the week's money from
the top ten customers."* — Move 3: *"A lost customer is lost on the day they do not return, not
ninety days later"*.

**Lens** — Monday concrete enough to fail at (lens e) / lens (d), the non-subscription translation.

**What's wrong** — Three instructions about one figure do not agree. Monday 1 makes the reader
define the top; Monday 2 has already defined it (ten); Move 3 says the figure is a list of names, and
Monday 2 asks for a share, which is a percentage. Pick one: the definition is the reader's (Monday 1),
Monday 2 uses "as you defined it", and the figure read aloud is both — the share, with the names
behind it, which is what Move 3's sentence actually means. Separately, "lost on the day they do not
return" is not a day a shop or a studio can find; a subscription cancels on a date, a non-returning
customer just does not come, and the only way to know is a window — the very device the paragraph
has just called a lever. For the book's reader the honest instruction is the one Monday 1 already
gives: a lost customer is lost on a day *you fixed in advance*, and the number is read against that
day, not moved to a kinder one.

**Rewrite** — Move 3: *"A lost customer is lost on the day you wrote down in advance — thirty days
without a return, sixty, a cancelled contract — and not on a later day chosen once the number is in;
a discount that lowers the monthly figure is written down as a discount; the concentration figure is
read as a share with the customers named behind it, not as a percentage alone."* — Monday 2: *"the
share of the week's money from the top customers as you defined them in item one, with their names."*

## 7. The §6i sentence is said four times, and Move 2's first paragraph carries Reading's Note

**Quote** — Move 2: *"The post is a founder's own reading of his own numbers, and this chapter reads
it as that. It is also, unusually, a list of the questions a mature owner asks of the figures, in

the reader's behalf"* — Move 3: *"This is the book's selection, not Barry's, and the ground for it is

is an argument"* — Reading: *"The "three things" of the title are this book's selection"*.

**Lens** — Apparatus (mode 2) / source accounting inside the argument (mode 6) / §6d.

**What's wrong** — §6i requires that the compression be marked as the book's at the point it is
made, and the Move 2 sentence does that well. Move 3 then says it again in the first paragraph of
the reasoning, the owed block says it a third time (where it is doing different work — naming the
risk — and can stay), and Reading a fourth (where it belongs). The Move 3 repetition is the one to
cut: the reader has just been told, and "the ground for it" is the sentence that matters. "This
chapter reads it as that" is the narrator on stage, and "a founder's own reading of his own numbers"
is the evidence-class line Reading already carries. "Unusually" is a verdict with nothing behind it.

**Rewrite** — Move 2, first paragraph: *"…brought "people reading the metrics only on the surface
level." What he wrote is a list of the questions he asks of the figures, in order, with the figures
attached."* — Move 3, first paragraph: *"Three figures, read weekly by someone other than you: what
came in and what went out underneath the total; what you lost, counted as customers and as money
separately; and how much of the money rests on how few of the customers. The ground for choosing
these three is what each lets a person who is not you see."*

## 8. Counting openers, a self-announcing turn, and "a total rising smoothly"

**Quote** — Move 2: *"A total rising smoothly hid a year that was anything but."* — Move 2: *"Then
three more."* — Move 2: *"Seven things, then, not three, and Barry nowhere says fewer would do."* —
Move 2: *"And the post shows something else, in passing, that the chapter's title is really about."*
— Move 2: *"A number nobody had to falsify. They only had to define it."*

**Lens** — Counting and self-announcing (mode 4) / seams (mode 5).

**What's wrong** — The post does not say the total rose smoothly; it says the total is the less
interesting graph and the breakout is "all over the map". The year did rise (it crossed $1m), but

has folded two). "Seven things, then, not three" is the §6i count with a "then" — it is right, and it

then explaining the title. "A number nobody had to falsify. They only had to define it" is a good

**Rewrite** — cut *"A total rising smoothly hid a year that was anything but."* — *"Then three more."*

## 9. Two flinches into the third person

**Quote** — Move 3: *"Three is also a claim about the reader's size, and it should be held that
way."* — Move 3: *"Concentration is the figure most owners can state and least like to"*.

**Lens** — Second-person flinch (mode 3).

**What's wrong** — "The reader's size" is the chapter describing its reader to someone else; the
reader is in the room. "Most owners" is a generalisation the book has no figure for and the sentence
does not need — the reader is the owner who can state it and does not like to.

**Rewrite** — *"Three is also a claim about your size, and it should be held that way."* (folded into
finding 5's rewrite of the paragraph) — *"Concentration is the figure you can state and least like

## 10. Small pointers: "the record chapter eleven asked you to keep", "nearly dead"

**Quote** — Move 3: *"from the record chapter eleven asked you to keep."* — Move 2: *"at $1,207 a month

**Lens** — Continuity (ch. 08, 10, 11).

**What's wrong** — Ch. 11's Monday says "Add five columns to the record from chapter ten — or start

## Overall note

The chapter's rule is right, the compression is honestly marked where §6i wants it, and the owed
block is a model. What needs doing is in two places. First, put the chapter back inside the book it
is in: the thirty seconds already hold ch. 12's cash figure, ch. 12 already asked for three weekly
numbers, and ch. 21 has the owner reading them — one reconciling sentence in Move 3 and a corrected
lede fix it, and the "chapter thirteen" pointer becomes ch. 12 (finding 1). Second, take the chapter's
hands off the annual-plan push and off Barry's 0% goal: the push is one event in three figures, not
two movements without change, and a discount cannot flatter net revenue churn — it is the thing
Barry shows worsening it (findings 2 and 3). With those out, the "measure becomes target" section
stands on what the post really gives it — the S-1 definition, the thirty-day window, Barry's own
"compare only the same number" — and on the reader's business, where the window and the definition
are theirs to fix (finding 6). The rest is the usual pass: the ARPU sentence back on the ARPU
(finding 4), the "monthly" quotation back in its own sentence (finding 5), the §6i sentence said once
in the argument (finding 7), and the counting openers gone (finding 8). Nothing here needs a new fact.
