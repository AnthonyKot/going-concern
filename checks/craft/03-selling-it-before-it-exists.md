# Craft review — Chapter 03, "Selling It Before It Exists"

Reviewed 2026-08-26. Craft only; all figures assumed correct. Findings ranked by impact.

The short version: this chapter is close. The lede is exact, Move 1 sees the reader clearly
("money was somebody else's department"), the ladder is genuinely useful, and the Coolest Cooler
limit block ends on the best sentence in the chapter ("only one of them felt like winning").
The weaknesses are concentrated in one place — the Studio Neat case is presented as a records
citation with the drama filed under "details" — plus a handful of seams where the apparatus
shows through the prose.

---

## 1. The case is told verdict-first; the one moment of uncertainty is filed as a "detail"

**Quote:**
> The campaign ran from 3 October to 2 November 2010. The archived page records the close:
> *"5,273 Backers · $137,417 pledged of $10,000 goal."* Just under fourteen times the target,
> at about twenty-six dollars a head.

…and four paragraphs later:

> Two details from their account matter more than the totals.
>
> The first is how the Glif got funded. On the day they launched, they emailed the writer of a
> widely-read technology blog and offered him a prototype.

**Lens:** Story craft.

**What's wrong:** The close arrives before the launch. The only moment in the whole case where
two people are deciding under uncertainty — launch morning, nothing pledged, emailing a stranger
to offer a prototype — is delivered as retrospective evidence ("detail the first") after the
reader already knows it worked. There is no *before*; the case reads as a table with commentary,
and the template's evidence-then-analysis rhythm is doing the ordering instead of the story.

**Rewrite** (reorder, not expand — the "Two details" scaffold sentence is cut, which pays for it):

> The campaign opened on 3 October 2010 with nothing pledged. That day they emailed the writer
> of a widely-read technology blog and offered him a prototype. He linked to them the next
> morning, in a post of 167 characters. In their words: *"1 hour and 25 minutes after the post
> went live, we surpassed our funding goal of $10,000, and finished the day with over $25,000."*
> By the close on 2 November the archived page records *"5,273 Backers · $137,417 pledged of
> $10,000 goal"* — just under fourteen times the target, at about twenty-six dollars a head.

The shipping paragraph, the four-more-times paragraph, and the prototype-rule paragraph follow
as now; the "honest hole" paragraph lands harder because the email story is fresh rather than
four paragraphs back.

---

## 2. Cydoc arrives as a name from the index, not a case the reader can picture

**Quote:**
> <a>Cydoc</a> had four paying customers at full price with recurring revenue — rung five — and
> an eleven-year payback per practice. The demand evidence was real; the business was not
> deliverable.

**Lens:** The reader in the chair.

**What's wrong:** The reader has not met Cydoc and has no idea what a "practice" is doing in
this sentence — nothing establishes who Cydoc sold to, so the load-bearing figure dangles from
a missing antecedent. This is the cross-reference apparatus talking past the reader.

**Rewrite** (cut "per practice", which pays for the added signpost):

> Chapter four's case, Cydoc, had four customers paying full price on recurring revenue — rung
> five — and an eleven-year payback on each. The demand evidence was real; the business was not
> deliverable.

---

## 3. "A widely-read technology blog" — the book names everything else

**Quote:**
> they emailed the writer of a widely-read technology blog and offered him a prototype.

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** A book whose voice is built on named companies and exact figures suddenly goes
coy about the one actor whose favour, by the chapter's own admission, determined the whole
reading — and the reading list names Daring Fireball anyway. The vagueness reads as caution,
not craft, and it blunts the "honest hole" paragraph, which depends on the link being a real,
specific, unrepeatable event.

**Rewrite** (shorter):

> they emailed John Gruber of Daring Fireball and offered him a prototype.

Then in the honest-hole paragraph, "one link from one blog" keeps its bite because the reader
now knows which one.

---

## 4. "Notice… Notice also…" — the podium surfaces

**Quote:**
> Notice what the second axis cost them before any money existed. […]
>
> Notice also the honest hole, which they point at themselves.

**Lens:** Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong:** Two consecutive paragraph openings issue the same classroom imperative. It is
the one place in the chapter where the reader is told to look rather than shown, and the repeat
makes it a tic. The content of both paragraphs is excellent; only the stage direction is wrong.

**Rewrite** (same length):

> The second axis had already cost them something before any money existed. A working prototype
> and a conversation with a factory is not a weekend. […]
>
> There is an honest hole here, and they point at it themselves.

---

## 5. "Three of the five will be the same" — a promised ratio nobody can promise

**Quote:**
> Record the reason for each no; three of the five will be the same, and that sentence is the
> thing to fix.

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** This is confidence borrowed rather than earned — a predicted distribution
stated as fact, in a book that otherwise only asserts numbers it can source. A practitioner
would promise the *repetition*, not the ratio; the fake precision is exactly the LinkedIn move
the constitution warns about.

**Rewrite** (shorter, imperative intact):

> Record each no word for word; the reason that repeats is the thing to fix.

---

## 6. "That year's tournament" — a detail pasted in with its context left behind

**Quote:**
> closed at *"$12,442 pledged · 1,211 backers"* — delivered in time for that year's tournament.

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong:** The reader has never been told the app had anything to do with a tournament;
the phrase arrives trailing source-context the prose didn't carry over — the summarizer's seam.
It is a shame, because a hard external deadline is a deliverability point this chapter would
love, if it were set up.

**Rewrite:** Either earn it or cut it. If the source names the app and the event, one clause
does it: "an app for following that spring's basketball tournament — delivered before tip-off."
If not:

> closed at *"$12,442 pledged · 1,211 backers"* — and delivered on time.

---

## 7. The limit block says "in this book" twice in three paragraphs

**Quote:**
> On the first axis that is an unambiguous pass, the most emphatic in this book.

…then:

> The pledge total was simultaneously the best demand evidence anyone in this book obtained and
> a contract to manufacture sixty thousand complex objects…

**Lens:** Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong:** The same self-referential superlative twice in one block — the book pointing
at itself where it should be pointing at the sixty-two thousand strangers. The second instance
sits in the block's best sentence and has earned its place; the first is a warm-up for it.

**Rewrite** (pure cut):

> On the first axis that is an unambiguous pass. Sixty-two thousand strangers made
> non-refundable payments, at $185, for an object that did not exist.

---

## 8. The Dutch box opens on a riddle

**Quote:**
> Rung four may be less final than it looks, and the scope of the rule is the whole of it.

**Lens:** The reader in the chair.

**What's wrong:** "The scope of the rule is the whole of it" refers to a point the reader can
only reconstruct after finishing the box — an abstract gesture where the box's actual content
(who is selling to whom, and how) is concrete and interesting.

**Rewrite** (same length):

> Rung four may be less final than it looks — and whether it is depends entirely on who is
> selling to whom, and how.

---

## Overall note

Rebuild the Studio Neat case in time order (findings 1, 3, and 4 together): open on 3 October
with nothing pledged, the email to Gruber, the eighty-five minutes, *then* the close, the
shipping, the four repeats, the prototype rule — with the honest-hole paragraph immediately
after, while the link is still warm. Every fact is already on the page; only the sequence is
wrong. Right now the chapter's load-bearing case reads like the archive it cites — verdict
first, drama in the appendix — and it is the single stretch where a reader could believe a
machine assembled the record. Ten minutes on that same morning, told in the order the two of
them lived it, is what makes the rest of the chapter's machinery feel like it was built by
someone who was watching.
