# Craft review — Chapter 09, "What to Charge"

Scope: craft only; every figure assumed correct. Judged against CONTEXT.md §3 (template), §4
(voice), and §6d (caveat placement — respected throughout; nothing below moves a caveat where §6d
forbids). The baseline is high: Move 1 is among the book's best ("as if value were a number waiting
behind the sofa, or to raise prices until people complain, as if complaints paid invoices"), the
collective-action reading of pay-what-you-want is a genuine practitioner's insight, and "Studio Neat
had found demand and a pricing failure in the same result" is a sentence the whole chapter earns.
The findings are ranked against that standard.

**Failure-mode sweep (ch. 1–5 patterns):** (1) verdict-first — partly present: the lede spoils by
design, which is the template, but the case's one human decision is also delivered verdict-first
(finding 2). (2) apparatus surfacing — present three times, once at the worst possible spot
(findings 1, 5, 8). (3) second-person flinch — present once, at the chapter's most reader-specific
paragraph (finding 3). (4) openings that count their contents — absent; every section opens clean.
(5) summarizer's seams — mild: one frame pasted in with its context half-attached (finding 7).

Nine findings.

---

## 1. The climax pauses to audit itself

**Quote:** "All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours. The campaign was still about
$5,000 short. If the roughly $45,000 pledged at that point all belonged to those slots, dividing it
by 3,000 gives an average near $15. That is also this book's arithmetic, and the source does not let
us verify the assumption behind it. The argument does not need the average: the supply was exhausted
while the aggregate remained below the project's stated floor."

**Lens:** Story craft

**What's wrong:** This is the paragraph the entire chapter stands on — sold out and still short —
and between the reveal and its meaning sit three sentences of sourcing mechanics, including a
conditional number the prose itself says the argument does not need, and a first-person "us" (the
only narrator surfacing in the chapter). §6d already licenses the fix: the principal caveat ("That
division is this book's, not a number Studio Neat published") stands one paragraph up, and the
Reading list's "Arithmetic" entry already carries the $15 illustration in full, condition and all.
The prose is paying a debt twice and paying it at the climax.

**Rewrite (cut the two middle sentences; nothing is lost — Reading keeps the $15):**
> All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours. The campaign was still about $5,000
> short. The supply was exhausted while the total remained below the project's stated floor. Studio
> Neat had found demand and a pricing failure in the same result.

---

## 2. The one decision in the case arrives as a verdict

**Quote:** "They did not preserve the experiment at the expense of the product. They opened
additional, unlimited reward tiers."

**Lens:** Story craft

**What's wrong:** This is the only moment in the case where two people choose under uncertainty, and
the reader is told the outcome of the choice before feeling that there was one. The bind is already
on the page, two paragraphs up — all-or-nothing, sold out, $5,000 short, nobody charged and nobody
served if the goal fails — but it is never assembled into the moment where it could have gone either
way. Reorder facts already present; no new ones needed.

**Rewrite:**
> Sold out, still short, and all-or-nothing: they could hold the experiment pure and let the product
> stand or fall with it, or change the offer. They opened additional, unlimited reward tiers.

Same length as the original two sentences plus the generosity of "They did not preserve the
experiment at the expense of the product," which this version implies rather than states — if that
line must survive, it can replace "or change the offer."

---

## 3. The reader described in the third person at the most personal moment

**Quote:** "This distinction matters particularly to people leaving salaried technical work. Your
salary tells you the minimum economics of selling your time."

**Lens:** The reader in the chair

**What's wrong:** "People leaving salaried technical work" is this book's reader, exactly, named as
a demographic in the one paragraph written specifically for them — and the very next sentence proves
the flinch was unnecessary by saying "Your salary." Failure mode 3, textbook instance: third person
at precisely the sharpest moment.

**Rewrite:**
> This distinction matters particularly to you, because you are arriving from salaried technical
> work. Your salary tells you the minimum economics of selling your time.

---

## 4. "The aggregate reached it" — the mechanism sentence loses its antecedent

**Quote:** "They let the buyers fill in the number that would determine whether the aggregate
reached it."

**Lens:** Rhythm and music

**What's wrong:** This sentence carries the whole mechanism of the case — individual free choices
summing against a fixed floor — and "it" points backwards at the nearest number, which is $16.67,
when it means $50,000. "The aggregate reached it" is also the abstract register the surrounding
paragraphs avoid. A reader who parses it wrong has the case's engine upside down.

**Rewrite:**
> They let the buyers fill in the number, and staked the goal on what three thousand free choices
> would add up to.

Same facts, same length; "three thousand free choices" is already established by the preceding
paragraphs.

---

## 5. Monday hands the reader a piece of the book's machinery

**Quote:** "Put a review date on it. That card is the chapter's artifact."

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear

**What's wrong:** "The chapter's artifact" is the book's internal template vocabulary (the operating
record is "one artefact" in the book's own planning language, spelled differently at that). In a
Monday list — the highest-traffic prose in the chapter, the part the reader acts on — the sentence
tells them what the card is *to the book*, not what it is to them.

**Rewrite (same length):**
> Put a review date on it. The next ten asks test the card, not your nerve.

Or simply cut the sentence and end the item at "Put a review date on it."

---

## 6. The chapter's promise sentence trips on its own shorthand

**Quote:** "So this chapter will not find the correct price. There is no such number available
before the asks. It will build a price you can defend for the next ten of them, and a record that
tells you what to change afterwards."

**Lens:** The reader in the chair

**What's wrong:** This is Move 1's closing promise, and its middle sentence goes bureaucratic
("available") and cryptic ("the asks", then "them" reaching back across a sentence). Chapter 8
earned the word "ask", but here it is compressed past the reader's comfort at exactly the sentence
that sets the chapter's contract.

**Rewrite:**
> So this chapter will not find the correct price. No such number exists before you ask. It will
> build a price you can defend for the next ten asks, and a record that tells you what to change
> afterwards.

---

## 7. "They advised" — the source's genre pasted in with the case

**Quote:** "In Studio Neat's later account of its five campaigns, Kickstarter took 5% and payment
processing about 3%; they advised allowing for about 5% of backers whose payments fail."

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear

**What's wrong:** Mid-sentence the chapter slips from narrating the Cosmonaut to summarizing a
how-to post — "they advised" has no addressee inside the case; it is advice to the blog's readers,
imported with its frame half-attached. Failure mode 5 in miniature: a detail pasted in with its
context left behind.

**Rewrite:**
> In Studio Neat's later account of its five campaigns, Kickstarter took 5% and payment processing
> about 3%, and they had learned to allow for about 5% of backers whose payments fail.

("Had learned to allow for" glosses advice explicitly drawn from a five-campaign retrospective; no
new fact, but if the fact-check pipeline reads it as one, the fallback is "and about 5% of backers'
payments could be expected to fail.")

---

## 8. The counter-case block counts a method the chapter never numbered

**Quote:** "It reveals the separation between demand and sufficiency; it does not establish that
this chapter's three-part method finds a durable price."

**Lens:** The practitioner's ear

**What's wrong:** "This chapter's three-part method" is the apparatus labelling itself — and
miscounting: Move 3 has four subheads, and the reader was never told they were reading a numbered
method. Naming the parts instead of counting them makes the sentence do the work the label only
gestures at.

**Rewrite:**
> It reveals the separation between demand and sufficiency; it does not establish that a floor, a
> named alternative and one held proposal find a durable price.

---

## 9. Six thousand people, filed under logistics

**Quote:** "Manufacturing still had to be paid for, and the Cosmonaut's delays had to be managed for
more than six thousand people."

**Lens:** Warmth without slack

**What's wrong:** The paragraph's human cost arrives in double passive — delays "had to be managed"
— at the one place the chapter could let the backers exist as people who were promised June and got
December. The facts are all on the page.

**Rewrite (same length):**
> Manufacturing still had to be paid for, and more than six thousand people were now waiting
> through the Cosmonaut's delays.

---

## Overall note

The single change that would most improve this chapter is to give Move 2 back its middle: the
forty-eight hours between sell-out and the decision to open new tiers. Findings 1 and 2 are one
structural problem seen twice — at the exact moment the reader should be standing where Gerhardt and
Provost stood (sold out, $5,000 short, an all-or-nothing clock running), the prose first steps out
to audit its own arithmetic and then reports the founders' choice as a settled verdict. Cut the
duplicate caveat (the Reading list already holds it, as §6d intends) and stage the bind before its
resolution, and the case stops being a well-argued exhibit and becomes the thing this book does
best: a decision the reader passes through without knowing how it ends. Everything needed is
already on the page.
