# Craft review — ch. 14, What a Customer Is Worth

Scope: prose only; facts assumed correct. Constraints honoured: no narrator surfacing, no hedging in
Monday, numbers stay, counter-case discipline stays, §6d caveat placement respected.

**Sweep of the known failure modes.** Present: verdict-first case (1), apparatus in prose (2, 3, 5),
second-person flinch (6), source accounting in the argument's path (3, 4, 7). Absent: no section
opening counts its contents in so many words, though the Move 1 list comes close. The specific risk
named for this chapter — the retelling repeating ch. 04 — is real and is the overall note.

---

## 1. The case is told with the verdict already announced, and the one fact that lets the reader feel the walls closing is used as a static figure

**Quote**
> They are these. Her customers "wanted to pay fewer than $100 per month" per doctor. Against that, "the hosting and AI costs for our platform had gone up to about $70 per doctor". And to connect the product to a practice's medical records system, "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000". Put those together and her own conclusion follows: "it would take 11 years per practice just to break even".

**Lens** — Story craft.

**What's wrong** — By the time the reader reaches this paragraph the epigraph has said "not survivable", Move 1 has said the instrument is worse than useless, and the paragraph before has said "the economics underneath them". There is no moment of not-knowing. Yet the page already holds the fact that makes one: the cost "*had gone up to*" $70 — a moving number under a fixed ceiling. The current telling presents three figures as a table read aloud; the story is a ceiling that the doctors set and a floor that rose to meet it while she watched. Ch. 04 already did the flat version ("Thirty dollars a month against a four-thousand-dollar integration") — this chapter has to show the movement or it is repeating.

**Rewrite** (same facts, replaces the quoted paragraph and the "Put those together" sentence)

> The doctors who used it got eleven minutes back on every visit, and they "wanted to pay fewer than $100 per month" for them. That was the ceiling, and it was set by the customer. Underneath it, a number she did not set was moving: "the hosting and AI costs for our platform had gone up to about $70 per doctor". What had once been a margin was now something under thirty dollars a month per doctor, and every practice still had to be connected to its records system first — "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000". Thirty dollars a month against four thousand up front. Her own arithmetic: "it would take 11 years per practice just to break even".

## 3. Source accounting in the argument's path, twice in Move 2, when the Reading note already carries it

**Quote**
> That figure is a subtraction of two of her published numbers rather than one she states, and it is arithmetic anyone can check. Set against an integration at the cheaper end, $4,000, a monthly contribution of that size takes something over eleven years to come back. Her estimate and the arithmetic agree, which is the reassurance you want before building an argument on a founder's own figure.

and, earlier:
> She has since published a detailed post-mortem of why she shut it down. The account is her own and unaudited, which matters for what can be drawn from it, and it is unusually specific about the numbers that matter here.

**Lens** — The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong** — The Reading list already says: "The under-$30 monthly contribution in Move 2 is a subtraction of two figures Draelos publishes, not a figure she states," and "A founder's own post-mortem, which is weaker evidence than a filing and is attributed as such throughout." Under §6d that is where these belong. In the prose they are an auditor clearing his throat between the subtraction and its meaning. "Which matters for what can be drawn from it" is the debt register's voice. One clause of the reconciliation earns its place — the reader should know the eleven years is hers, not the book's — and it is already delivered by "Her own arithmetic" in finding 1.

**Rewrite** — cut both. Replace the first quoted passage with:

> Under $100 a month against about $70 of cost specific to that doctor leaves something under $30 — before support, before her own time, before anything else. Against the cheaper integration, $4,000, that comes back in a little over eleven years. Her estimate and the arithmetic agree.

Replace the second with:

> Her post-mortem, published after the shutdown, is unusually exact about the numbers that matter here.

## 4. The channel paragraph is a digression that Boundaries then repeats, and it contains a seam ("three letters") from a term the chapter never uses

**Quote**
> And note what perfect records would have bought her: a cost for each of four individual acquisitions, which is four anecdotes rather than a cost per channel. Three different things are being asked for by the same three letters. What one customer cost to acquire is measurable once. What a channel costs requires several comparable acquisitions through the same mechanism. Whether that channel cost is affordable needs this chapter's contribution and payback figures on top. Cydoc could have had the first and had no process from which the second could be estimated.

**Lens** — Story craft / practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong** — Move 2 is the story; this is a taxonomy of acquisition cost delivered in the middle of it, and the reader cannot know which "three letters" are meant because *CAC* appears nowhere in the chapter — a detail carried in from the drafting notes with its context left behind. Boundaries then says the same thing again ("four customers arriving through four routes give nothing from which a channel cost could be estimated … Cydoc had no acquisition process to measure"). Keep the fact and the one-line consequence in Move 2; let Boundaries keep the analysis it already has.

**Rewrite** (replaces from "Four customers, four channels" to the end of the paragraph)

> Four customers, four channels, no channel used twice. Chapter seven asked whether a channel repeats; here none was tried a second time — which shows no repeatable mechanism, not that none of them could have repeated. It also means that even perfect records would have given her four prices for four acquisitions, and no price for any channel.

Cut from Boundaries nothing; the sentence "The narrower true statement is that Cydoc had no acquisition process to measure, not that its acquisitions had no cost" now does its work once.

## 5. Drafting history surfacing as a hand-off note

**Quote**
> Chapter four used exactly this material to make a different point — that milestones like these can keep satisfying a badly written stopping rule for years. The economics underneath them were left for here.

**Lens** — The reader in the chair.

**What's wrong** — "Were left for here" is the plan talking, not the book. The reader does not need to be told where the material was parked; they need the turn from *milestones* to *arithmetic*, which is one sentence.

**Rewrite**
> Chapter four asked what those milestones did to her stopping rule. This chapter asks what was underneath them.

It is a contrast and a warning, not a calculation.

## 8. Move 1 opens by pointing at the previous chapter instead of at the reader's spreadsheet

**Quote**
> The previous chapter asked whether you can afford to finance the gap between doing the work and being paid for it. This one asks the question that should have come first: whether what eventually arrives is worth financing at all.

**Lens** — Rhythm and music.

**What's wrong** — The template says open on the reader's actual position, not on history; this opens on the book's own table of contents. The link to ch. 13 is good and can stay — one sentence later, once the reader is already in the chair.

**Rewrite**
> Somewhere you have already multiplied a monthly payment by a number of months and written the result down as what a customer is worth. The previous chapter asked whether you can afford to finance the gap between the work and the payment; this one asks whether what eventually arrives was worth financing at all.

Then cut the first sentence of the following paragraph ("The standard instrument for this is lifetime value, and the standard version of it is worse than useless in a business this age.") down to: "That is lifetime value, and in a business this age it is worse than useless." Net length: shorter.

## 9. Monday 4 asks for a statistic the record it just built does not contain

**Quote**
> If two segments have similar averages and different spreads, you have two businesses.

**Lens** — The practitioner's ear.

**What's wrong** — The record described in Monday 1–3 has one row per customer; a reader with a two-person studio has perhaps six customers in a segment and no "spread" worth the word. The chapter's own method is *look at the rows*, and the sentence should say that.

**Rewrite**
> If two segments average the same and one of them contains both your best customer and your worst, you have two businesses, not one.

## Overall note

The chapter's structural risk was named in advance — retelling ch. 04 — and it has happened at the
level of the paragraph, not the chapter. Ch. 04 already delivered "Thirty dollars a month against a
four-thousand-dollar integration"; Move 2 here delivers the same subtraction with more sourcing
around it, and then spends its final paragraph on a taxonomy of acquisition cost that Boundaries
repeats. What ch. 04 could not show, and this chapter can, is *movement*: a price ceiling the
customers set, a cost that "had gone up to" $70 underneath it, and a payback that is a date rather
than a quantity. Rebuild Move 2 around that (finding 1), strip the source accounting to the Reading
note where it already lives (findings 3, 7), and move the CAC taxonomy out of the story (finding 4).
The reasoning and Monday are already the strongest in Part III; the case just needs to be a story
the earlier chapter had no reason to tell.
