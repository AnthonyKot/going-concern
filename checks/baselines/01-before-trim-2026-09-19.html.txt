  <p class="kicker">Part I · Is there anything here? · Chapter 1</p>
  <h1>The Only Question That Matters</h1>
  <p class="lede">Every business book eventually says that you cannot create demand, only find it.
  This is true, and stated that way it is nearly useless — because a business can die of this
  while holding the very demand it bet on. Webvan did.</p>

  <div class="decision">
    <span class="tag">The decision</span>
    Whether to commit money to building the thing, before you know that enough people want it at a
    price that covers what it costs you to make.
  </div>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Demand is three numbers, not one</h2>

    <p>You have an idea you cannot stop thinking about, and the thinking has reached the stage where
    it costs money to continue. Not much, at first. A deposit on a unit. A machine. Three months of
    your own time, which you have stopped counting because counting it is discouraging.</p>

    <p>Everyone you have described it to thinks it is a good idea. This is the least informative
    sentence in this book; <a href="02-talking-to-people.html">chapter two</a> is about why.</p>

    <p>The advice you will receive at this point, from every direction, is that you should validate
    demand before you build. It is correct advice. It is also, in the crude form in which it is
    almost always given, a piece of advice you can follow scrupulously and still lose everything —
    because it treats demand as a fact about the world, a yes or a no, when it is actually a
    relationship between three quantities that move independently of one another:</p>

    <ul>
      <li>how many people want it,</li>
      <li>what they will pay,</li>
      <li>and what it costs you to serve them at that volume.</li>
    </ul>

    <p>Move any one of those and the answer changes. A market that is real at ten thousand customers
    is imaginary at four hundred. A grocery order people will genuinely place at $81 does not exist
    at $103, and a building priced on the second number is a building priced on a fiction. The
    question is never <em>is there demand</em>. The question is whether there is demand <em>in the
    shape your cost structure requires</em>, and the second question is much harder to ask, because
    it requires you to know your cost structure before you have built anything.</p>

    <p>What follows is the most expensive illustration available of the difference between the two
    questions.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Webvan: the demand was real</h2>

    <div class="case">
      <p class="case-head"><span class="who">Webvan</span> — Foster City, California, 1996–2001</p>

      <p>Louis H. Borders, who founded Borders Books in 1971, founded Webvan in December 1996 on a
      premise that was not stupid and has since been proved correct: people would rather not go to
      the supermarket.</p>

      <p>Borders' answer was automation. Rather than picking orders off shop shelves, Webvan built
      purpose-designed distribution centres. Each was sized, in the company's own words, "to process
      product volumes equivalent to approximately 18 supermarkets" — a designed capacity of
      <strong>8,000 orders per day</strong>. At that volume, with an average order of $103, Webvan
      told investors a centre would run at a 12% operating margin and generate $300 million a year.
      One building, doing the work of eighteen shops.</p>

      <p>The Webstore opened on 2 June 1999. The following month, with the first orders barely
      counted, Webvan signed an agreement with the engineering firm Bechtel to build up to
      <strong>26 more</strong> distribution centres over three years — a programme the company
      estimated at approximately $1.0 billion, each centre costing $30–35 million. One building was
      taking orders; twenty-six more were on paper.</p>

      <p>The orders were not enough.</p>

      <p>As of 31 December 1999 the Oakland centre — the flagship, the proof of the model — was
      running at <strong>less than 25% of the capacity for which it was designed</strong>. Nine
      months later, as of 30 September 2000, it had reached <strong>less than 40%</strong>. Both
      figures are Webvan's own disclosures to the SEC.</p>

      <p>The order size problem the company could fix, and did. The average order in the fourth
      quarter of 1999 was <strong>$81.31</strong>, more than $20 short of the $103 the model
      required; by the fourth quarter of 2000 it had reached $112. What Webvan could not fix was
      volume. In the third quarter of 2000, after five full quarters of operation, the Oakland
      prototype still did not break even on a cash-flow basis, and the company estimated that order
      volume would have to rise <strong>"by a minimum of forty percent"</strong> from September's
      level to get there. It also disclosed, flatly, that "to date no Webvan facility has become
      profitable on a cash-flow basis."</p>

      <p>The accumulated deficit ran $159.4 million at the end of 1999, $440 million by September
      2000, and <strong>$612.7 million</strong> by December 2000 — a net loss of $453.3 million in
      that year alone. Webvan filed for Chapter 11 in July 2001.</p>

      </div>
    </div>

    <p>Read those numbers again, because almost every retelling of this story gets the moral
    wrong.</p>

    <p>Webvan was not off by an order of magnitude. It needed forty per cent more orders. Under 40%
    of eight thousand is still upwards of three thousand grocery deliveries a day, every day, in one
    metropolitan area, in the year 2000 — thousands of households doing precisely the thing the
    company had been founded on the belief that they would do. The demand was <em>there</em>. It was
    not there in the quantity required by a building sized for eighteen supermarkets, which had been
    designed before anyone knew what the real number would be.</p>

    <p>And here the popular version of the story needs correcting, because the Bechtel agreement is
    usually presented as the fatal act — a billion dollars signed away on warehouses nobody wanted.
    The filings say otherwise. Webvan disclosed that it had <em>"no obligation under the Bechtel
    agreement to build any distribution centers and, consequently, no capital commitment under the
    agreement until Webvan determines to proceed"</em> with a particular site. By late 2000 it had
    no plans to build any more, and said so. The option was real and the company exercised it.</p>

    <p>It died anyway — because optionality on the centres you have not built yet does nothing about
    the ones already standing. Webvan's error was not the contract for twenty-six warehouses. It was
    the first one: committing to a facility whose economics required eight thousand orders a day
    before a single customer had placed one. Everything after that was an attempt to grow demand up
    to a cost structure that had been fixed in advance — a race run on the cost structure's terms,
    against a deadline set by the bank balance.</p>

    <p>Put the model the other way round and it is a forecast. A centre that pays only at 8,000
    orders a day, at $103 an order, at a 12% margin, is a prediction that 8,000 households in one
    city will order groceries every day — written in concrete before it was written down. That is
    the arithmetic this chapter ends by asking you to do on Monday, at your own scale. Webvan did
    it after the building was up.</p>

    <div class="aftermath">
    <p class="case-head"><span class="who">Aftermath: Instacart</span> — the same idea, a decade later</p>

    <p>The obvious question about Webvan is whether it was simply wrong about people wanting
    groceries delivered. It was not.</p>

    <p>Instacart, founded in 2012, went after the same appetite and built no warehouses at all. It
    used the shops that already existed and paid people to walk around them. In 2022 it fulfilled
    <strong>262.6 million orders</strong> across the United States — up from 223.4 million the year
    before — on gross transaction value of <strong>$28.8 billion</strong>, and reported its first
    annual net profit: $428 million, of which $358 million was a tax benefit from releasing a
    valuation allowance rather than anything that happened in the business.</p>

    <p>Two cautions, because it is easy to take too much from that. Those are national figures set
    against one Oakland facility, so any ratio between them is arithmetic without meaning. And
    Instacart's decade was not Webvan's: smartphones, retailer integrations and a labour market that
    made same-hour delivery affordable all arrived in between. This is not the same business built
    better. It is a different business serving a demand that had, by then, become servable.</p>

    <p>What the comparison establishes is narrow and sufficient. The appetite Webvan bet on was real,
    and it was still real long after Webvan was gone. Whatever killed the company, it was not that
    nobody wanted the product — which leaves the cost structure holding the bill.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>The rule, in its useful form</h2>

    <p>The principle underneath all of this is usually called the iron law of the market, and it
    says that no quality of execution will save a business selling something nobody wants. That is
    true, and it is much narrower than the version you will hear, because demand does not sit in the
    world as a fixed quantity waiting to be discovered. Repositioning an identical product for a
    different buyer can turn an indifferent market into an eager one; removing friction — a form
    that takes two minutes instead of twenty, delivery on Thursday instead of "within three weeks" —
    routinely converts people who wanted the thing in principle into people who buy it; naming a
    category can assemble a demand that was real but had no word for itself, and therefore no way
    to search for you. All of that is execution, and all of it changes the number. What execution
    cannot do is make people value something they fundamentally do not want. Keep that. It disposes
    of a large category of doomed ideas at no cost — provided you keep sharp the line between
    creating awareness of a product people would like if they knew about it, which is an ordinary,
    winnable job, and manufacturing a preference that is not there, which is not; most businesses
    that believe themselves to be doing the first are doing the second, and the failure mode looks
    identical from inside for about eighteen months. If you take nothing else from this book, take
    the habit of asking the question before rather than after.</p>

    <p>But state it in its useful form, which has a second clause:</p>

    <blockquote>Demand must exist — and it must exist at a volume and price that the cost structure
    you are about to commit to can survive.</blockquote>

    <p>The first clause protects you from building something nobody wants. The second protects you
    from a different failure, and a harder one to see coming: building the right thing on a cost base
    that requires a level of demand you have not yet demonstrated. That failure is the subject of
    this chapter. It is difficult to recognise from inside because everything except the arithmetic
    looks like a business that is working — and the second clause is stated in terms of your cost
    structure rather than the market because demand can be real and simply <em>early</em>. Webvan's
    market existed in 1999. It did not exist at 1999's cost of labour, smartphone penetration, or
    consumer habit. Being ten years early is indistinguishable, from inside the business, from being
    wrong: the spreadsheet looks the same, the bank balance looks the same, and the market research
    says people want it, which they do. You cannot control when a market arrives. You can control
    whether you are still solvent when it does.</p>

    <p>Two consequences follow.</p>

    <h3>Your cost structure is a demand forecast in disguise</h3>

    <p>When you sign a five-year lease, hire your second employee, or buy a machine that only pays
    for itself at a certain throughput, you are not making an operational decision. You are making a
    quantitative prediction about demand and putting money behind it, usually without noticing that
    you have done so. Every fixed cost you take on has a number hidden inside it: the volume at
    which it stops being a mistake.</p>

    <p>Most owners could not state that number for their largest fixed cost, and the principle does
    not scale even though the number does: a business whose entire footprint is one room on a
    twelve-month lease has a breakeven exactly as a $35 million building does, and an owner who
    cannot state it is in Borders' position with fewer zeros — and with less margin for being early.
    Ten minutes with the one in front of you converts a vague anxiety into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic problems
    can be solved.</p>

    <h3>Variable beats fixed until you know the number</h3>

    <p>Before you have real demand data, the correct instinct is to convert as much as possible of
    your cost base from fixed to variable, even when the variable option is more expensive per
    unit. Renting is worse than owning at scale and better than owning at uncertainty. Contractors
    cost more per hour than employees and can be stopped. Where nothing converts — you cannot build
    a quarter of a steel mill or license half a pharmacy, and there are real markets where the
    minimum viable physical footprint is large — this advice does not soften; it hardens: there is
    no small version to build, so the response is to demand far more evidence before committing,
    and to secure the demand contractually before the capital goes in rather than after, which is
    <a href="02-talking-to-people.html#presale">chapter two</a>. Before the evidence is in,
    worse unit economics you can switch off are usually worth the premium over better ones you
    cannot.</p>

    <p><em>Usually</em>, not <em>always</em>. <em>Worth the premium</em> is a statement about the
    odds; the counter-case it owes is in the note at the end of this chapter.</p>

    <p>This inverts once you know the number. After demand is demonstrated and stable, the fixed-cost
    version is often cheaper per unit, and an owner who stays variable long after the volume is
    proven may be leaving margin for a competitor to take. The trap is not fixed costs. The trap is
    taking them on in the wrong order.</p>

    <h3>The same arithmetic at the size of one person</h3>

    <p><strong>This is an invented example; Lea returns in chapter ten.</strong> Lea is an
    employed developer. She wants to sell small consultancies an automated weekly report: €900 a
    job, about €50 of tools per job and, by her estimate, eight hours of delivery and two of
    selling. Nobody has paid her yet. Several former colleagues have told her it is a great idea.</p>

    <p>Her warehouse is not a building. It is a day of her salary. She could ask her employer for
    a four-day week, which would take €800 a month off what reaches her household, and the
    business would cost about €100 a month in software and bookkeeping whatever she sold. So the
    four-day week is a forecast in disguise. It says: jobs will replace €900 a month. Her pay is
    inside that number, because a breakeven that pays you nothing is not a breakeven; it is a
    hobby with an accountant.</p>

    <p>Each job leaves €850 after its tools. One job a month leaves her €50 short; two clear the
    line with €800 to spare. <strong>Two jobs a month is her Oakland number</strong>: the volume
    at which the commitment stops being a mistake. Her time is counted once, as the twenty hours
    two jobs would take, well inside the day she would free. Her pay is counted once, as the €800
    the jobs must replace. She does not also charge each job for her own hours at somebody else's
    rate; that would bill her twice for the same day. Chapter ten prices those hours to answer a
    different question: whether the work itself is worth doing.</p>

    <p>Now the evidence. Paid customers: zero. Against a forecast of two a month, zero is the
    whole answer. Asking for the four-day week today would be Webvan's first building: a fixed
    cost set before a single order.</p>

    <p>The variable version exists. Lea has about thirty-two hours a month outside her job and
    household — on her own estimate, room for three jobs. It is slower and more tiring, and it
    commits her to nothing she cannot stop. She also asks her employer one question: if she moves
    to four days, can she move back, and on what notice? The answer tells her whether this
    warehouse has a door.</p>

    <p>So she writes the rule while nothing is sunk: <em>if three customers have paid by the end
    of March, and the jobs took roughly the ten hours each I estimated, I ask for the four-day
    week; if not, I don't, and I find out why.</em> The second half matters as much as the first:
    the money works on ten hours a job, and at twice that, two jobs would eat the freed day and
    more. The rule is not a verdict on
    the idea. It is the idea's first test, sized so that failing it costs evenings, not a salary.
    <a href="10-the-first-ten-customers.html">Chapter ten</a> picks her up after three paid jobs
    and shows what her ten hours turned into.</p>

    <p>Notice what her rule refuses to count: the colleagues who said it was a great idea.
    Everyone hears that sentence. <a href="02-talking-to-people.html">Chapter two</a> is about why
    it tells you almost nothing, and what to ask instead.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="monday">
    <h2>Monday · one commitment, one number</h2>
    <p><strong>Do this one thing.</strong> Name your first warehouse: the biggest commitment you
    are about to make that you cannot cheaply undo — a lease, a hire, a machine, the salary you
    stop drawing. Write what it adds to each month, including any pay it takes away from your
    household. Divide that by what one sale leaves after its own cash costs — fees, materials,
    hosting, not your hours — and round up. That is how many sales a month the commitment is
    betting on. Beside it, write how many people have actually paid you or signed something
    binding. Compliments, sign-ups and “I would definitely use that” count as zero. You now hold
    the commitment's demand forecast and your evidence against it. If the second number is zero,
    that is not a failure; it is the accurate starting position.</p>
    <p><strong>Missing a number?</strong> If you have no price yet, or do not know what a sale
    costs you, write each missing input and where you will get it —
    <a href="09-what-to-charge.html">chapter nine</a> for the price, <a href="02-talking-to-people.html">chapter two</a>
    for paid evidence. That list is Monday done, and it is also an answer: a commitment whose
    number you cannot yet write down can wait until you can.</p>
    <p>If you have another hour:</p>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Check the hours.</strong> Multiply the sales you need by the hours each takes,
      selling included, and hold that against the hours you actually have. The money can clear
      while the week does not.</li>
      <li><strong>Write the stopping rule, with a date and a number.</strong> Something you cannot
      argue with later: <em>if fewer than eight customers have paid by 30 September, I do not sign
      the lease.</em> Write it now, while you are calm and nothing is sunk.</li>
      <li><strong>Price the cancellable version.</strong> Get the actual quote and the actual notice
      period for the smaller, stoppable form of the same commitment this week, and see what the
      option to be wrong costs. <a href="04-deciding-not-to.html">Chapter four</a> is entirely
      about this.</li>
    </ol>
  </section>
