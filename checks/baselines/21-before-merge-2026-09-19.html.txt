  <p class="kicker">Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 21</p>
  <h1>Coordinating Work and Acting on Signals</h1>
  <p class="lede">A task can be clear and still stop between two people. Somebody needs an answer,
  somebody else can give it, and the customer has a deadline. The useful routine gets that decision
  made in time and makes its consequences visible.</p>

  <div class="decision">
    <span class="tag">The decision</span>
    Which information and response arrangement will help the team keep one commitment,
    who acts on it, and how you will check whether the routine earns the time it takes.
  </div>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Start with the decision holding up the work</h2>
    <p><a href="20-managing-people.html">Chapter twenty</a> produced an agreed assignment and a
    shared review. It also left an unanswered question: would Maya and Sam's escalation route
    work when Maya was busy? They remain an explicitly invented team throughout this chapter.
    The routine below is this book's proposal; the company accounts later provide narrower
    evidence about meetings and measures.</p>
    <p>On the following Tuesday, Sam finds another unfamiliar calculation at 09:20 and sends
    the agreed direct message. Maya has booked a customer call across the 09:30 check.
    Sam receives no answer by 10:00, flags the draft as at risk and continues the other sections.
    Maya sees the message at 11:15. The 11:00 internal draft deadline has passed. This particular
    customer expects a checked report at noon.</p>
    <p>Sam followed the new route. Maya did not keep her response commitment. More frequent
    status reports from Sam would not give him the authority Maya retained. The immediate
    decision is whether Maya can approve the calculation and finish the check before noon,
    or must contact the customer to agree a later delivery.</p>
    <p>She determines that the remaining check will not fit. She contacts the customer, who
    agrees to 13:00. Maya supplies the calculation rule and checks the completed report before it
    goes out at 12:40. Record both dates: delivery met the revised agreement but missed the
    original promise. Quietly replacing noon with 13:00 would hide the cost of the block.</p>
    <p>For the next report, Maya protects the 09:30 check and the response time through 10:00
    before accepting calls. Sam keeps the blocked item visible until the decision is received.
    If Maya cannot provide that availability, she must arrange a capable authorised backup or
    change the work and customer promise in advance. A meeting invitation alone provides no cover.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="move" id="measures">
    <h2>Choose signals that can change a decision</h2>
    <p>Start with the question you must answer, then choose the observation. Maya needs to know
    which reports risk missing a promise, which decision would release each one and whether
    faster delivery is creating correction work. She does not need three universal business
    numbers read aloud each morning.</p>
    <p>A current blocked report can require action before any weekly percentage changes. Sam
    records the customer, original deadline, missing decision, request time, decision owner and
    latest useful response time in the existing task record. For the report above, the
    unresolved calculation needed Maya's response by 10:00. Reaching that time without an answer
    triggers the agreed at-risk message and a decision about the promise.</p>

    <h3>Define the measure before judging the result</h3>
    <p>For a short trial of the routine, Maya and Sam use the definitions below. These are
    illustrative choices for their report work, not benchmarks for another business. A small
    count stays visible beside any percentage.</p>
    <p><strong>Open decisions.</strong> From each active report's task record, read the request
    time, decision owner and latest useful response time. At that deadline, seek the decision
    or change the plan. Show an unknown response time explicitly; a missing decision owner
    is a coordination gap to resolve, not a zero-length wait.</p>
    <p><strong>Delivery.</strong> For the next five reports due, count checked reports delivered
    by the original customer deadline over all reports due, including unfinished ones. Use
    the task record and sent report as evidence; show unknown delivery times separately.
    Inspect each miss and unknown. A revised deadline does not erase the original. Decide
    whether the response route, workload or promise needs changing.</p>
    <p><strong>Quality.</strong> For the same reports, use correction notes and customer follow-up
    to count those needing a correction within seven days of delivery. Show reports whose
    seven days are incomplete and any missing follow-up. Read the corrections before
    celebrating speed. No reported error is narrower evidence than confirmation that the
    report was useful.</p>
    <p>They set a review two weeks from the start. If all five reports and their seven-day
    follow-ups finish sooner, they can review then. If work or follow-up is incomplete at
    the review, show the partial observations and set the next check. The live response
    deadline still applies to each task; the review window is no reason to leave today's
    blocked customer waiting.</p>
    <p>For each signal, name the person who maintains it and where the underlying record can
    be inspected. Date a definition change and explain whether earlier observations can still
    be compared. Keep a missing timestamp as unknown while you check the sent record; do not
    replace it with an assumed success. If records cannot support a proposed measure, use a
    smaller direct check while you collect the missing information.</p>

    <h3>Choose differently when the work asks a different question</h3>
    <p>A stock seller may need quantities on hand and confirmed arrival dates before promising
    collection. A consultancy considering another booking may need usable hours by skill and
    existing commitments. A customer-concentration review needs a defined period and revenue
    basis, with the named accounts behind the share. Select information for the decision at hand.</p>
    <p>Use <a href="12-reading-your-own-business.html">chapter twelve's period review</a> for
    the economics and <a href="13-profit-is-not-cash.html">chapter thirteen's dated forecast</a>
    for upcoming payments and low balances. A team facing a purchase decision can read the
    relevant amount and date from those records; it need not reconstruct the accounts in a meeting.</p>
    <p>If buyers are anonymous, a till total cannot tell you which regulars stopped returning.
    You can inspect recorded sales, item quantities, returns and direct customer feedback.
    Leave individual retention unknown unless you have records that support it. Where later
    purchases can be linked to a customer, keep them separate from the
    <a href="10-the-first-ten-customers.html#after-sale">evidence of a useful result</a>.
    A repeat purchase does not establish whether the earlier promise was fulfilled.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Read different measures of the same event</h2>
    <p>In January 2019, ConvertKit founder Nathan Barry explained his company's public metrics.
    He distinguished monthly recurring revenue, a view of subscription value, from the dashboard's
    “net revenue,” which he described as money brought in during the month.</p>
    <p>His annual-plan example makes the distinction concrete. ConvertKit promoted discounted
    annual plans each November. He reported net revenue reaching about $3 million during the
    push, while the discount reduced MRR. He also explained that revenue churn included
    contraction, so discounts could raise that figure without the same change in the number
    of customers leaving. The contraction subsequently moved outside its rolling thirty-day window.</p>
    <p>One event therefore required several interpretations: money arriving sooner, lower
    monthly subscription value and a churn measure affected by the discount. Treating the
    last figure as a count of lost customers would lead to the wrong diagnosis. These are
    Barry's account and dashboard definitions, not a fresh audit of ConvertKit's accounts.</p>
    <p>He also reported that 6,994 customers, about 33% of the total, produced $811,117 in MRR,
    about 68% of the total. This was his split above the base plan, not a test proving
    dependence on a few named customers. It illustrates why a revenue share needs a defined
    group behind it. His account does not establish which signals your team should use.</p>

    <h3>Keep the work visible when a measure becomes a target</h3>
    <p>A target creates a reason to ask how a number could improve without the work improving.
    Sam could mark a draft complete before reconciling it. Maya could rewrite the deadline
    after a delay. A rising completion score would then conceal unfinished work or a broken
    promise. That is a possible failure to check, not an inevitable effect of having targets.</p>
    <p>Keep the original deadline, delivery evidence and corrections beside the count. Ask the
    person doing the work to explain an unexpected movement; inspect a sample together.
    If the measure rewards the wrong action, change the target or definition openly and
    retain enough history to explain the break. Do not use a small, incomplete count as a
    verdict about an individual's performance.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Choose a response arrangement that fits the urgency</h2>
    <p>For a straightforward decision, a written task update can be enough if the decision
    owner will see it and respond before the deadline. If several people must resolve a
    trade-off together, use a short conversation with that question stated in advance.
    If delay would cause immediate harm, use the agreed urgent route. The format follows
    the time available for a response.</p>
    <p>Name a coordinator and a decision owner; they may be the same person. Sam maintains
    the report record and flags missing answers. Maya decides calculation changes and customer
    commitments. Sam can resolve formatting issues himself. A coordinator who notices a block
    does not automatically have authority to remove it.</p>
    <p>Maya and Sam choose a brief written update before each report session and the protected
    response window, with a call only when the written question cannot be resolved in time.
    They do not add a daily meeting on days without shared work. Where a recurring meeting is
    useful, give it a purpose, the necessary participants and a time budget. Make small decisions
    there when the right people and information are present. Assign larger ones an owner and
    deadline instead of letting them become unowned follow-up.</p>

    <h3>What Groove changed</h3>
    <p>In February 2016, Alex Turnbull described several meeting arrangements Groove had tried.
    He reported that relying on unscheduled calls left the remote team poorly coordinated
    and interrupted. A weekly Monday call had social and coordination value; a separate
    Friday social call did not last. Daily standups also needed refinement.</p>
    <p>The structure he described assigned scheduling and moderation to Lesley, with a
    thirty-minute Monday recap, ten-minute Tuesday–Thursday standups and a twenty-minute
    Friday update including customer feedback. After a couple of months he judged the team
    more efficient. The account supplies formats and his assessment, but no before-and-after
    delivery results or total coordination cost.</p>
    <p>Those times belong to Groove's remote team. They do not establish that Maya and Sam
    need the same cadence, that unscheduled conversations are always wasteful, or that a
    meeting should exclude decisions. The transferable question is who needs to know or
    decide what, by when, and whether the chosen arrangement actually gets that done.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="move">
    <h2>Check the action and what it cost</h2>
    <p>The record for Maya and Sam's blocked report now has a decision as well as a status:
    Maya to contact the customer and supply the rule; Sam to finish the draft; Maya to check
    before release. It records the agreed 13:00 delivery, actual 12:40 delivery and missed
    original noon deadline. Their next action is to protect the response window and test it.</p>
    <p>They review the next five reports using the definitions above. The following figures
    are invented trial results. They also reconstruct five earlier reports from the available
    records, retaining the one delivery timestamp they cannot establish.</p>

    <p>For this comparison, all ten reports have completed the seven-day quality follow-up;
    correction records are available for each. Time includes writing and reading updates,
    calls and the coordination review, excluding report production and substantive checking.
    An eight-minute call attended by both people counts as sixteen minutes, not eight.</p>
    <p>The new window costs twenty more minutes of combined time. It has four confirmed
    on-time deliveries, but the earlier window might also have had four: one timestamp is
    unknown. Corrections are unchanged. These small windows do not establish that the new
    routine caused better delivery. They justify inspecting the late report and asking
    whether timely decisions are now arriving, rather than declaring a percentage victory.</p>
    <p>Suppose the remaining delay was missing customer input, with Maya's internal response
    on time. They retain the response window for another five reports and agree an earlier
    input check with that customer. If a recurring meeting had added time without helping
    a decision, they could shorten, replace or remove it while preserving the response route.
    Fewer interruptions is useful only alongside work that still gets done.</p>
    <p>No set of signals promises to predict every surprise. Review a missed event by asking
    whether information was available in time, what it would have changed and what collecting
    it would cost. Sometimes the issue is a decision nobody took; sometimes the signal
    genuinely did not exist. <a href="23-slack.html">Chapter twenty-three</a> takes up the
    capacity needed when work is disrupted.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="monday">
    <h2>Monday</h2>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Choose one shared commitment.</strong> Use the assignment from chapter twenty.
      Name the next dependency, the customer or internal promise, and the latest useful decision
      time. A solo operator can use a supplier or customer handoff.</li>
      <li><strong>Define the information needed.</strong> Name each source, period, unit or
      denominator, missing observations and the action it could trigger. Start with the task
      record; add a signal only if it answers a decision you need to make.</li>
      <li><strong>Agree the response route.</strong> Name who keeps the record, who decides, when
      they respond and how an unanswered or urgent item escalates. Choose a written update or
      conversation that fits the available time.</li>
      <li><strong>Run it on real work.</strong> Record the decision, action owner, deadline and
      result. Preserve original promises alongside revisions. Resolve an available small decision
      now; give an unresolved one a responsible person and response date.</li>
      <li><strong>Review after a stated window.</strong> Inspect delivery, quality, unresolved
      blocks and time spent coordinating. Include unknowns and incomplete follow-up. Continue,
      revise or remove parts of the routine on that evidence, then set the next check.</li>
    </ol>
    <p>Your output is one coordination record linking a commitment to information, a decision
    and follow-up. A task list or notebook is sufficient. If no shared work is underway,
    prepare the first dependency and leave the results unfilled.</p>
  </section>

  <div class="owed">
    <p class="case-head"><span class="who">What would challenge this advice</span></p>
    <p>A counter-case is still owed: a small team that chose relevant signals, named decision
    owners and response times, and reviewed the cost honestly, yet delivered worse because
    maintaining the routine consumed the attention the work needed or obscured an important
    unmeasured change. Neither founder account measures that trade-off. The invented trial
    shows how to inspect it; it cannot establish that the proposed routine is worthwhile.</p>
  </div>
