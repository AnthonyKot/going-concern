The First Ten Customers

They are not a small sample of the next thousand. They are a test of the process that produced them — the first point at which promised work can be compared with what selling and delivering actually required.

The decision Whether the way you acquired and served the first customers is something you can repeat next month within the time and capacity you actually have — and who delivering to them has told you to stop selling to.

Move 1 The situation

Ten people have paid. The temptation is to treat them as a market and start extrapolating, and the standard warning against it is that early customers are unrepresentative — friendlier, more forgiving, more likely to have come through a personal connection. True, and not very useful, because it tells you to distrust the only data you have without telling you what to do instead.

The more useful move is to stop reading them as a sample at all. What the first customers can expose is not the market but the machinery: whether the thing you did to get customer one is a thing you could do again next month, and whether delivering what you sold is survivable at the price you sold it for. Ten is a review point, not an evidentiary threshold. You may find a pattern, or the honest result may be that there is not enough evidence yet. Either is more useful than extrapolating a market from ten sales.

Which turns the chapter into five questions, and they should be answered in writing before you decide anything about growth:

  1. Can customer ten be acquired by something resembling what got you customer one?
  2. How much unpriced custom work appeared once you started delivering?
  3. What broke — in the work, the tools, the hours, the promises?
  4. Did delivering reveal that you had sold to the wrong person, or at the wrong price?
  5. Could you repeat the same sale and delivery next month within your available hours and capacity?

For a business that serves people across a counter, the same five read: can Saturday's trade be had again next Saturday, how much off-menu work crept in, what broke, who should not have been served, and can it be done with the hours there are.

Note what is not on the list. Whether they stay, renew or come back is the next chapter's subject and genuinely a different question; a business can repeat the sale reliably and still lose everyone it sells to. This chapter stops at the point where the sale and its delivery can be done again.

And repeatable does not mean delegable. This chapter asks whether this owner can do it again under ordinary conditions next month. Whether staff can do it without the owner is Part IV, beginning with chapter seventeen.

Move 2 What actually happened

In mid-2013 Dan Norris had spent nearly a year on an analytics product called Informly and was, by his own account, "sitting at $476 recurring monthly income." Then, in his words: "on 26 June I launched WP Curve and within a week I had doubled the $476 to a total of $952."

WP Curve sold unlimited small WordPress jobs on a monthly subscription — humans, not software, at $69 a month, answering at all hours from several countries. It is a service business of the least glamorous kind: the questions above are only fully legible when somebody has to actually do the work.

One division makes that week concrete. If Informly held steady, the new $476 at $69 a month is about seven customers in seven days — this book's division, not a figure Norris published. The week straddles a month's end: the ladder below closes June at $907, and the seventh day of trading lands a few days later at $952.

Then the part nobody puts in the launch story. In July they grew "70% which means we are now covering costs", and promptly stopped publishing revenue at all — a decision they later explained and apologised for, having been advised that "being too transparent might hurt us." They returned voluntarily and said why, so this is not an accusation; but the months that resumed were good months, a self-interrupted series is weaker evidence than an unbroken one, and the gap sits exactly where the hardest stretch would be. When the numbers resumed in November, the shape of the grind was visible — and shape is all the ladder can give, because every figure on it is company-wide. Informly, and later two more products, ran alongside WP Curve; the reports never separate its revenue, and the customer counts below are described as totals too:

Read the customer counts rather than the money. "At the start of October we had 64 monthly recurring customers in total"; "During October, we signed up 59 more bringing our total number of recurring customers to 123." And the line that gives those figures their scale: "Our previous best month of customer signup was 13." By New Year, "We started December with 155 customers and welcomed the new year with 178 customers", at "a final MRR of $9,812" — "we fell $188 short" of ten thousand. They put that at "an annual revenue run rate of $117,744."

On how those customers arrived, Norris is explicit and unromantic — and still the seller describing his own month, which is worth holding onto: these four posts are the source because they were written at the time rather than recalled for a later interview, not because their author was disinterested. "no virality involved. We have to work for every single customer." He offers his own benchmark for what that is worth: "in my last business I hit 120 customers after 6.5 years and 40 of them came via an acquisition."

Move 3 The reasoning

They found out twice that they were targeting the wrong people

The same discovery arrived twice, four months apart, through two instruments that cannot substitute for each other — and neither arrived at ten customers. The first followed roughly fifty conversations; the second appeared after the company reported more than 150 customers, and the launch week itself is this book's estimate of about seven, with no published delivery detail attached. What the case shows is where to inspect for segment evidence, not that ten observations are enough to act on.

The first came from selling. They had decided web agencies were the buyer — agencies have WordPress work, so let them resell it. Then, in October: "from the 50-odd web agencies Dan and I have spoken to", even after reshaping the offering to suit them, nothing. So they stopped: "completely remove our agency offering and only offer our services to business owners." They stopped in the middle of their best month — the same October that signed 59 customers of the other kind. Nothing in the totals said anything was wrong; the fifty conversations did. The lesson they drew is the right one, and harder than it sounds — "When you compromise on your core offering, something is not right."

The second came from delivering, and could not have come any other way. By December some customers were signing up and leaving inside the same month, and the pattern was not random: "The common thread is that all of these customers have been running blogs, rather than businesses." For a blog, "$69 feels like a big cost." The response was not to cut the price. It was to change who they went after — "we will spend more time marketing to business owners instead."

Notice the difference between the two instruments. Roughly fifty conversations produced no buying evidence from the agencies they approached — which is not the same as proving no agency would ever buy. That is chapter eight's apparatus doing its job, and it works before anyone pays. But nothing anyone said in a sales call could have told them that a blogger's $69 feels different from a business's $69. That only became visible once real people were being served and leaving. Asking finds the buyers who say no. Delivering finds the buyers who said yes and should not have.

Which is why the fourth question — did delivery reveal the wrong customer? — cannot be answered in advance. You ask it deliberately, once real delivery records exist, looking for a pattern in who is unhappy rather than counting how many.

The same evidence answers two questions

The December cancellations are retention evidence — customers leaving inside a month is exactly what failing to hold people looks like. The same cancellations are also acquisition evidence, and neither reading cancels the other.

What the pattern adds is where the losses clustered. They were bloggers, for whom $69 carried a different meaning than it did for an operating business — so the same fact that measures retention also says something about whom the company had been approaching. This chapter uses the second reading to revise targeting; chapter eleven owns the first, and owns the question of what makes anyone stay.

Your test is wider. When early customers leave, ask whether the pattern points to the segment, the promise, the price or the delivery. When the losses cluster in one segment, acquisition is often the earliest place you can intervene — not because it is always the cause, but because changing who you approach is something you can do on Monday. A business that does not choose who walks in still chooses: by what is on the menu, what it costs, and when the door is open.

What "no virality" is worth as a finding

The most quoted thing a founder can say about early growth is that it came from word of mouth, which chapter eight argued is usually the name of an output being offered as a cause. Norris says the opposite, and says it while the numbers are still small: every customer was worked for, one at a time.

Treat that as a measurement rather than a lament — but only half a measurement. Knowing that customers did not arrive virally is useful once the work that produced them is written down; effort does not become a process merely by being effortful, and it can just as easily be reputation, a chance introduction, or something different every time. Here some of it was reputation: the reports name mentors and audiences — established bloggers and podcasters who linked to them, and one whose email to his list arrived within a day of a phone call. That asset predates the business, and anyone starting without one should expect the same process to run slower rather than to fail. Sixty-four to a hundred and twenty-three in a month is an observed rate that can be compared with the next month, not yet one you can plan against. A business whose early growth is genuinely unattributable has nothing even to compare. His own comparison makes the point sharper than any argument: the previous business took six and a half years to reach 120 customers, and a third of those arrived in an acquisition rather than through anything he did twice.

Refusing the work that does not repeat

One more decision sits inside the first-customer period and is easy to miss because it looks like turning down money. Norris, writing in November: "We have turned down tens of thousands of dollars worth of one off work."

At $8,371 in monthly recurring revenue, tens of thousands of dollars in declined project work was material against the recurring base — how material cannot be said more precisely, because tens of thousands is indefinite and an MRR snapshot is not an annual total. A WordPress support company gets asked constantly to build a site, and each such job pays more than a subscription. The objection is not that bespoke work teaches nothing — sometimes it reveals a repeatable need, or becomes a product. It is that mixing it into the subscription made the process they were trying to standardise harder to see.

This is the sharpest form of the chapter's question. Repeatability is not something you discover about the first ten customers; it is something you protect by declining the eleventh when it is shaped differently. And the cost is paid immediately, in cash, while the benefit is a process you can still describe in a year — which is why almost nobody does it, and why the ones who do tend to write the number down afterwards. If the one-off is this month's rent, take it — and write it in the record as a one-off, so that it never counts as the process.

They also kept a target to measure against: "We have a 10% monthly growth target." Having one is what makes December's "17% increase" and the $188 shortfall legible as anything other than mood.

Monday

Do not start a fifth document. Extend the record built by the previous four chapters — or, if you are starting here, start it here with the columns you can fill. Its completed form is the first-customer operating record.

  1. Build one row per customer. Carry in the segment and refusals from chapter six, the acquisition mechanism from seven, the ask and objection from eight, the price and promised scope from nine. Add why they bought now — the trigger, not the reason they liked it — any discount or concession granted to close, and what delivery actually required: actual hours, work outside scope, rework, failures and promises missed, and one plain flag: was delivery completed. Blank cells are missing instrumentation, not zeroes.
  2. Start the next chapter's columns now, and do not use them yet. Whether they used it, cancelled, renewed, reordered or referred anyone: capture each the moment it becomes observable, because evidence you did not record is gone. What you must not do is close this chapter's decision with them — chapter eleven owns their interpretation. Mark anything that cannot have happened yet as not yet observable, never as blank or zero.
  3. Recalculate the sale at actual delivery cost. Value the hours at what you would pay someone competent to do them — chapter nine's replacement cost, which is neither your old salary nor nothing — add the other direct costs, and compare the result with the price. Unbilled hours are an input to margin, not the margin itself.
  4. Mark whether this owner could repeat each row next month. Use the hours and capacity actually available. Where the answer is no, name the constraint: acquisition, custom work, delivery, recovery or price.
  5. Write the segment decision the record supports. Name the segment you will stop selling to, or write “not enough evidence yet” and the next observation required. Do not manufacture a refusal to complete the exercise.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What would falsify it: an owner who kept the operating record, found an apparently coherent pattern, dropped the segment it pointed at and rebuilt acquisition around the remainder — and was wrong, because the dropped segment was the real market and the early signal was noise. This chapter permits “not enough evidence yet,” but it still recommends acting when a pattern is visible, and visible patterns can be false. Somewhere there is a business that did this and destroyed itself; its absence from the record is the usual absence.

Closing Part II · Will someone pay?

Five chapters, and like Part I they were a ladder — but a ladder of a different kind. Part I's rungs cost the buyer more at each step. These cost you more, and each one is only legible once the one before it has an answer:

a defined buyer → a way to reach them → the act of asking → a price that survives contact → a sale you can make again

The dependency is strict, and it runs further than this part goes: acquisition → repeatable delivery → a customer relationship that persists → economics that work. Part II owns the first two and stops. It has to, because the third and fourth need evidence that does not exist yet at ten customers. The order is the sequence in which evidence becomes legible, not a one-way workflow — delivery will send you back to the segment, the channel, the ask or the price, and should.

Before going on to Does the money work?, you should be able to point at all six of these. Not intend them. Point at them.

  1. A buyer definition that names who you turn away, and what refusing them is supposed to buy. Chapter six.
  2. Two channels you are practising, each sorted into what accumulates and what is only contemporaneous, with the review date already written down. Chapter seven.
  3. A record of asks in your own hand — who you approached, what they said, and the reason behind each no. Chapter eight.
  4. One price, above a floor you have actually calculated, held across enough comparable asks to mean anything, with VAT kept separate from what remains. Chapter nine.
  5. A record of what delivery actually required against what you sold: hours, work outside scope, rework, exceptions, promises missed. This chapter.
  6. The segment decision the record supports — who you will stop selling to, or not enough evidence yet written down with the next observation named. Also this chapter.

Those six are not six documents. They are one, accumulating: the segment and refusals from six, the acquisition mechanism from seven, the ask and the objection from eight, the price and promised scope from nine, and what delivery cost from this chapter. Kept as one row per customer, it is the only artefact Part II produces, and Part III extends the same rows rather than starting again.

Now the limitation, which is again larger than the achievement. You have evidence that you can identify, reach, sell to and deliver for a small number of customers under conditions you can describe. You do not know whether those customers stay, return, expand or refer — or whether the revenue becomes enough cash, soon enough, to keep the business alive. Nothing above measures any of that, and a business can pass every item on this list and still be dying quietly. That is Part III, and it is where most of the businesses that clear Part II are lost.
