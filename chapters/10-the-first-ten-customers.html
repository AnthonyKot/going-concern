The First Ten Customers

The first three customers paid the same price. One job took eight hours, another eleven, and the third eighteen. Before accepting the fourth, you need to know what changed.

The decision What to keep, change or investigate before selling the next few jobs, using the time, money and promises recorded in the ones you have already delivered.

What did you actually sell?

You have persuaded someone to pay and done the work. Perhaps you have done it three times; perhaps ten. You can now compare the business you described in the proposal with the work that arrived in your calendar. That comparison is the task here. Ten is a convenient review point, not a number that makes a conclusion reliable. Review sooner if a job exposes a problem you cannot afford to repeat.

If you have no paying customers yet, use the example to prepare a record for your first job. Leave actual hours and outcomes empty until they exist. Estimates belong beside actuals, with their names attached. If you already keep a sales record from the preceding chapters, extend it; if you are starting here, your last proposal, calendar and invoice are enough to begin.

A small consultancy, after three jobs

This is a hypothetical worked example. Lea is an employed developer testing a service for small consultancies. She offers to automate one weekly spreadsheet report for €900: one input file, one output, a handover and one round of corrections. Changes to the source system and ongoing maintenance are excluded. She expects eight hours of delivery. All example prices and costs exclude VAT; the figures are chosen to make the reasoning visible, not to suggest a market price.

Lea has completed three jobs. Each customer has paid €900. Her record separates time spent winning the job from time spent delivering it. Delivery includes calls, corrections and handover, rather than just programming. The direct cash costs are tools or services bought for that job.

A provided the agreed input file. B needed three extra hours because Lea's instructions for preparing it were unclear. The invoice still said €900.

Before doing any arithmetic, choose what you would investigate before the next quote. Is the problem the price, the kind of customer, or the work included? There may be more than one answer. Write yours down, then compare it with the record below.

B suggests an instruction problem worth fixing. C contains two different issues: work Lea owes to correct her own mistake, and an addition she agreed to supply. Neither establishes that C is a bad customer. A new scope sentence alone will not prevent another coding bug; a testing checklist alone will not prevent another unpriced integration. Recording only “overran by ten hours” would conceal the distinction that makes a useful response possible.

What WP Curve learned after people paid

WP Curve's monthly reports provide a real example of revising an offer and its audience while serving customers. The founders sold small WordPress support jobs on subscription. Their reports describe their own experience; they do not establish how Lea's consultancy will perform.

In October 2013, the founders reported that conversations with roughly fifty web agencies had produced no traction for an agency offering, despite attempts to adapt it. They decided to stop offering that version and concentrate on business owners. Some agencies were becoming affiliates instead. The distinction mattered: an organisation could help bring customers without being the customer for the service they wanted to sell.

In December, they described a few WordPress support customers who had signed up and cancelled within the same month. Those customers ran blogs rather than businesses. The founders believed the monthly price felt expensive to that group and decided to spend more effort reaching business owners. That was their interpretation of the cancellations. The report does not give enough detail to separate price, customer circumstances, expectations and service experience as possible causes.

The November report describes another choice: the founders had declined substantial one-off work while building a recurring service. A custom project might bring welcome money, but it would require different work from the subscription they were trying to establish. Their choice illustrates a trade-off. It does not mean that a new consultancy should refuse projects; for Lea, projects are the intended business.

These observations came at different stages, well beyond a literal first ten customers. They support looking separately at sales conversations, delivery and cancellations. They do not validate a ten-customer test, prove that a segment was permanently wrong, or show that the procedure below improves survival. The procedure is this book's proposed way to inspect your own work before committing to more of it.

For Lea, the useful question is whether the next buyer wants the defined report service or a broader integration project. Both could be viable offers. At present she has a price and a time allowance for only one. She needs to recognise the difference before promising delivery.

Work out what you can repeat

Put a cost on the hours

Lea uses €50 an hour as an illustrative allowance for competent labour. It is an economic test of the work, not a payment she has made and not her after-tax income. In your own record, use a rate you can explain, and distinguish an estimate from an actual replacement quote. Revisit the conclusion if a plausible change in that rate reverses it.

First examine delivery: subtract direct cash costs and the delivery-time allowance from the price. For A that leaves €900 − €50 − (8 × €50) = €450. For B it leaves €300. For C it leaves −€50. C has used more delivery resources than the price covers on Lea's assumptions, even before finding the customer is counted.

Then include the sales hours. A leaves €350, B €150, and C −€300. Across the three jobs, €2,700 of sales less €150 of direct cash costs and €2,350 for 47 hours leaves €200. That remainder still has to cover shared business expenses and work omitted from these rows. It is not net profit or take-home pay.

The bank looks more encouraging: the customers paid €2,700 and those job-specific purchases cost €150. The difference is €2,550 before other cash movements. Much of that money compensates Lea for work the calculation has costed but she has not paid someone else to do. Do not subtract her actual drawings and the same imputed labour again when judging these jobs. The later financial chapters will separate the accounts, cash and household requirements.

Include the attempts that did not sell

A customer row makes unsuccessful selling easy to overlook. Suppose Lea also spent six hours on approaches that produced no sale in this period. At the same allowance, that is another €300, turning the €200 remainder into −€100. Record those six hours once in a separate line for the period; do not attach all six to every customer. Work on prospects still considering an offer should also remain visible, with its outcome marked pending.

This does not establish that the service cannot work. Early setup and selling may become easier. But that improvement is something to observe. Lea cannot fund the present shortfall with hours she hopes to save later. If she chooses to spend time learning, she should be able to say how much time she can afford and what she expects to learn from it.

Check the next month against your calendar

Lea has 32 hours available next month, after her job and household commitments. She reserves eight for general selling, administration and uncertainty, leaving 24 for the sales and delivery work attached to accepted jobs. Two jobs like A would use 20 hours. A and B would use 24. One like C would use 23, leaving almost no room for another customer. These are scenarios, not bookings or forecasts.

She should also place the work on actual dates. Twenty-four hours spread over a month cannot satisfy two customers who both need twelve hours of attention this Friday. Access delays and review calls may constrain a job even when the total hours fit. The next proposal needs a start date and a delivery arrangement she can honour.

A arrived through an existing relationship and B through A. Those are useful routes, but Lea does not yet know whether either will produce another buyer next month. C took more sales time through a direct approach. Three rows cannot tell her whether that channel will improve. She can reserve time for another small batch of approaches and compare what happens, while avoiding promises that depend on customers she has not found.

Make one change you can examine

Lea chooses to keep the €900 single-file offer for the next two suitable jobs. Before quoting, she will inspect a sample input and send clearer preparation instructions. If another system needs connecting, she will assess and quote that work separately before agreeing to it. She will also add a check for the bug found in C's job. These actions address recorded problems; she has no evidence yet for excluding C's entire industry.

Her next review asks whether the clearer input requirement reduced preparation work and whether delivery stayed near eight hours without concealing unpaid corrections. If it did not, she will inspect what consumed the time before choosing between changing the offer, allowing more time, repricing or stopping this test. Two jobs are a manageable next commitment, not a statistical threshold or a promise that the uncertainty will be resolved.

For your business, the next change may be different. If the same work consistently costs more than the price covers, revisit the price or method. If customers keep asking for the same addition, investigate a separate offer. If a job is incomplete, record the hours already spent and estimate the remaining work separately: money collected does not make an unfinished job look profitable. Where records are missing, reconstruct what you can and mark the uncertainty rather than inventing precise hours.

A one-off job can still be a sensible choice. Record its actual scope and costs so it does not silently become evidence that the standard offer works. If you need its cash, include its delivery time in the calendar before accepting another standard job alongside it.

Monday

Set aside one session with your proposals, calendar and payments. Start with the jobs you have, even if there are fewer than ten. You are finished when you can explain one next decision from the record and name what remains unknown.

  1. Reconstruct the work. For each customer, record how they arrived, the price and payment status, promised scope, sales hours, delivery hours, direct costs and whether delivery is complete. Add a short explanation of overruns. Keep unsuccessful and pending sales effort in a separate period total. Mark estimates and gaps. Download a blank CSV record if you do not already have one; it opens in a spreadsheet and contains headings, not automatic calculations.
  2. Compare the money and the calendar. Follow Lea's calculation with your figures. Include your time once, using an explicit allowance, and keep cash received separate from the price. Then fit the next proposed jobs into the hours and dates you actually have. If one missing figure could reverse your decision, obtaining it is the next task.
  3. Write the next offer and review. State what you will keep or change, which observation led to it, and when you will examine the next completed work. “I do not yet know why this overran; I will record preparation and corrections separately on the next job” is a usable decision. You do not need to reject a customer group to complete the exercise.

What this review cannot establish

A few early jobs may mislead you. An unusual request can look like a whole market, and a difficult first attempt can become easier with practice. The WP Curve reports do not test this worksheet, and this chapter has no documented counter-case of an owner following this procedure and dropping a segment that would have worked. Keep the next commitment affordable and revisit the interpretation as evidence arrives. A tidy record does not remove uncertainty.

Into chapter eleven: did it help?

You now have something more useful than a count of customers: a record of what you promised, what it took to sell and deliver, and what you intend to change. You can begin here and fill in earlier questions as they become relevant; the preceding chapters are there to help with the buyer, the route to them, the ask and the price.

Lea has marked three jobs delivered. She has not yet checked whether the reports are being used or saving the customers time. Add the promised result and a follow-up date to each row now. For her next job, she will ask how the weekly report is produced today and agree when to check the new process after handover. Record a customer's answer when it arrives; do not postpone useful evidence to preserve a chapter order.

Chapter eleven takes up that distinction between delivering the work and the customer getting the result. The same record continues, with unknown outcomes left visible.
