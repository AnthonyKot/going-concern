Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 10

From the First Sale to a Useful Result

The first three customers paid the same price. One job took eight hours, another eleven, and the third eighteen. Before accepting the fourth, you need to know what changed.

The decision What to keep, change or investigate before selling the next few jobs, using the time, money and customer results recorded in the jobs you have already delivered.

What did you actually sell?

You have persuaded someone to pay and done the work, perhaps three times, perhaps ten. You can now compare the business you described in the proposal with the work that arrived in your calendar, and follow it through to actual use: work can be delivered and paid for while the person meant to use it still cannot. Review as soon as a job exposes a problem you cannot afford to repeat.

If you have no paying customers yet, use the example to set up a record for your first job: the promised result and a follow-up date beside the scope, estimates beside actuals. Your last proposal, calendar and invoice are enough to begin.

A small consultancy, after three jobs

This is a hypothetical worked example, continuing chapter one's Lea. She kept her job, sold in her evenings and, by the end of March, has been paid for three jobs: the first half of her rule. The second half — did each job take about the ten hours she estimated? — is what this chapter reads. She offers to automate one weekly spreadsheet report for €900: one input file, one output, a handover and one round of corrections. Changes to the source system and ongoing maintenance are excluded. She expects eight hours of delivery. All example prices and costs exclude VAT; the figures are chosen to make the reasoning visible, not to suggest a market price.

Lea has completed three jobs. Each customer has paid €900. Her record separates time spent winning the job from time spent delivering it. Delivery includes calls, corrections and handover, rather than just programming. The direct cash costs are tools or services bought for that job.

Before doing any arithmetic, choose what you would investigate before the next quote. Is the problem the price, the kind of customer, or the work included? There may be more than one answer. Write yours down, then compare it with the record below.

B suggests an instruction problem worth fixing. C contains two different issues: work Lea owes to correct her own mistake, and an addition she agreed to supply. Neither establishes that C is a bad customer. A new scope sentence alone will not prevent another coding bug; a testing checklist alone will not prevent another unpriced integration. Recording only “overran by ten hours” would conceal the distinction that makes a useful response possible.

Find out what happened after delivery

Paid and delivered is not the same as used. Lea goes back to each customer to find out.

Recover the promise before judging the result

Lea promised an automated report, a handover and a round of corrections. The customers wanted less time spent assembling their weekly figures. Those are related, but the proposal did not establish a measured starting point or promise a particular number of minutes saved. She cannot now invent a target and mark the old jobs against it as though everyone had agreed.

She can ask what happened. For the next job she will make the intended result clearer before quoting: the person responsible should be able to produce the agreed report from the agreed input, check it and send it without Lea operating the tool. She will ask how long that takes today and what improvement would be worthwhile. A target should reflect the buyer's work and what she can reasonably supply, rather than a number chosen to make the proposal impressive.

Keep the delivery commitment beside the intended benefit. Lea can test whether her tool processes the agreed file. Whether it saves time also depends on the customer's preparation, checks and working arrangements. Discussing those dependencies helps both people recognise a problem; it does not erase Lea's responsibility for what she agreed to deliver.

Ask about one actual occasion

Lea follows up after each customer has had two weekly reporting opportunities. That interval is an assumption for this example: it gives this particular workflow a chance to be used. A repair needed today, a seasonal purchase and a long implementation need different timing.

Her message is short: “Were you able to use the new process for last week's report? What still needed doing by hand, and about how long did the whole report take, including preparation and checks?” She invites an estimate and records it as one. If the customer would prefer to show her the workflow, they can arrange that. She does not need copies of their underlying business data to record the answer.

Respond, then update the cost of the job

Each answer needs a response, and any work the response takes belongs to that job.

Describe the result at the strength of the evidence

A careful note might read: “A reports using the process for two weekly reports; estimates total preparation and checking at 30 minutes each, against roughly 90 previously. No annual saving verified. Ask whether any required figures still need manual correction.” The reported difference is about an hour per use; the note preserves who supplied the estimate and how little of the future it establishes.

A faster report that omits a required figure may still fail the promise. Ask what remains awkward and whether the output is usable; speed should not displace the customer's reason for buying.

Repair the point where the work stopped

B has answered. The outcome is not unknown: the new process has not been used because the intended operator cannot access it. Lea needs to establish whether the issue is an omitted permission, a handover to the wrong person or a requirement outside the agreed setup. Her first step is to arrange a short session with the operator and the person who can authorise access.

Suppose, in this example, Lea discovers that she handed over only to the buyer and missed an access step required by the agreed setup. She spends one hour resolving it. That hour belongs to the job. B's delivery time rises from eleven to twelve hours. That extra hour must travel into the cost review, even though the invoice has already been paid.

This is why follow-up belongs beside the delivery record: it finds work the completion tick missed. It should not quietly become unlimited free support, so Lea explains each next action and its scope before taking it on. Her future handovers will include the actual operator and an access check.

What B's next report showed

Two weeks after the access session, B's operator runs the weekly report herself for the first time. Lea asks the same question as before. The answer: about 45 minutes including checks, against roughly two hours before, with one column still filled in by hand because B's accounting export now names it differently.

That last detail is the test of the scope sentence. A renamed column in the customer's export is a change to the source system, which the €900 excluded. Lea does not fix it quietly one evening. She explains why it sits outside the job, offers to update the mapping as a separate €100 piece of work, and leaves the decision with B. Her hours on B stay at twelve; the offer is a possible new sale, not a hidden correction.

B now has a status Lea can state at its real strength: benefit reported by the person who does the work, one manual step left, a priced fix offered. It is the job that went wrong, was repaired, and then got used. The repair cost one hour. Staying silent would have cost the customer.

Give an unanswered message an honest status

C might be busy, absent, satisfied or disappointed. Lea does not know. She sends one further short message, then stops chasing, records both attempts and leaves the outcome unknown. Unknown is different from not yet due: a customer whose next reporting cycle has not happened has not failed to answer. And if someone declines follow-up, record that and respect it.

Read the whole group before making a claim

At the first check Lea had three paid jobs: one reported benefit, one barrier to use and one unknown outcome. At the second, two report a benefit and one is still unknown. Keep all three in the record, with dated updates. Do not turn the two replies into a result for all three customers, or turn three jobs into a reliable future success rate.

Optional: a published report that keeps unknown outcomes visible

Turing School's historical outcomes reports offer a useful example of making missing information visible. Its H1 2019 Back End Engineering report, using the Council on Integrity in Results Reporting format, lists 67 graduates. At 180 days after completion it reports 80.6% employed in-field, 9.0% still seeking a job in-field and 7.5% under “Could Not Contact”. Those are not the only categories; the employment table also includes people not seeking in-field employment. The percentages should be read with the report's definitions and rounding.

The useful feature is that the unreachable graduates remain visible. A report about only the people who answered would answer a different question. It would not tell the reader what was known about the whole reported group.

The report also separates graduation from employment. Its employment figures concern the graduates included in that report, not everyone who ever enrolled or paid tuition. That boundary matters: you cannot turn a graduate employment rate into a success rate for all customers by changing the label. Nor does an employment result establish how much the school caused it. The report provides categories for observations, not a controlled comparison of causes.

Decide what to repeat, with the correction included

With B's extra hour added, Lea can now price what each job really took.

Put a cost on the hours

Lea uses €50 an hour as an illustrative allowance for competent labour. It is an economic test of the work, not a payment she has made and not her after-tax income. In your own record, use a rate you can explain, and distinguish an estimate from an actual replacement quote. Revisit the conclusion if a plausible change in that rate reverses it.

First examine delivery: subtract direct cash costs and the delivery-time allowance from the price. For A that leaves €900 − €50 − (8 × €50) = €450. For B, now at twelve delivery hours, it leaves €250. For C it leaves −€50. C has used more delivery resources than the price covers on Lea's assumptions, even before finding the customer is counted.

Then include the sales hours. A leaves €350, B €100, and C −€300. Across the three jobs, €2,700 of sales less €150 of direct cash costs and €2,400 for 48 hours leaves €150. That remainder still has to cover shared business expenses and work omitted from these rows. It is not net profit or take-home pay.

The bank looks more encouraging: the customers paid €2,700 and those job-specific purchases cost €150. The difference is €2,550 before other cash movements. Much of that money compensates Lea for work the calculation has costed but she has not paid someone else to do. Do not subtract her actual drawings and the same imputed labour again when judging these jobs. The later financial chapters will separate the accounts, cash and household requirements.

Include the attempts that did not sell

A customer row makes unsuccessful selling easy to overlook. Suppose Lea also spent six hours on approaches that produced no sale in this period. At the same allowance, that is another €300, turning the €150 remainder into −€150. Record those six hours once in a separate line for the period; do not attach all six to every customer. Work on prospects still considering an offer should also remain visible, with its outcome marked pending.

This does not establish that the service cannot work. Early setup and selling may become easier. But that improvement is something to observe. Lea cannot fund the present shortfall with hours she hopes to save later. If she chooses to spend time learning, she should be able to say how much time she can afford and what she expects to learn from it.

Check the next month against your calendar

Lea has 32 hours available next month, after her job and household commitments. She reserves eight for general selling, administration and uncertainty, leaving 24 for the sales and delivery work attached to accepted jobs. Two jobs like A would use 20 hours. A and B, including B’s correction, would use 25 and exceed the 24-hour allowance. One like C would use 23, leaving almost no room for another customer.

She should also place the work on actual dates. Twenty-four hours spread over a month cannot satisfy two customers who both need twelve hours of attention this Friday. Access delays and review calls may constrain a job even when the total hours fit. The next proposal needs a start date and a delivery arrangement she can honour.

A arrived through an existing relationship and B through A. Those are useful routes, but Lea does not yet know whether either will produce another buyer next month. C took more sales time through a direct approach. Three rows cannot tell her whether that channel will improve. She can reserve time for another small batch of approaches and compare what happens, while avoiding promises that depend on customers she has not found.

A real service changed its offer while serving customers

WP Curve's founders reported that roughly fifty web-agency conversations had produced no traction for an agency offering in October 2013. They stopped that version and focused on business owners; some agencies became affiliates instead. In November they described declining one-off projects while building a recurring service. In December they reported early cancellations among bloggers and attributed them partly to how that group perceived the price.

Lea's version of the same move is smaller: a broader integration project might be worth selling, but as its own offer with its own scope and estimate, not silently supplied inside the single-file price.

Choose changes you can examine

Lea chooses to keep the €900 single-file offer for the next suitable jobs that fit the calendar. Before quoting, she will inspect a sample input and send clearer preparation instructions. If another system needs connecting, she will assess and quote that work separately before agreeing to it. She will also check access with the actual report operator and add a check for the bug found in C's job. These actions address recorded problems; she has no evidence yet for excluding C's entire industry.

The four-day week from chapter one waits. The jobs took ten, fifteen and twenty-three hours against the ten she estimated, so the second half of her rule has not been met. She does not argue with it. The next jobs will show whether clearer scope brings them back near ten.

Her next review asks whether the clearer input requirement reduced preparation work and whether delivery stayed near eight hours without concealing unpaid corrections. If it did not, she will inspect what consumed the time before choosing between changing the offer, allowing more time, repricing or stopping this test. A small next commitment is a test, not a statistical threshold or a promise that the uncertainty will be resolved.

For your business, the next change may be different. If the same work consistently costs more than the price covers, revisit the price or method. If customers keep asking for the same addition, investigate a separate offer. If a job is incomplete, record the hours already spent and estimate the remaining work separately: money collected does not make an unfinished job look profitable. Where records are missing, reconstruct what you can and mark the uncertainty rather than inventing precise hours.

Monday · one job, from promise to result

Do this one thing. Take your most recent finished job. On one page write what you promised, what you charged, your hours winning it and your hours delivering it (mark estimates as estimates), what you spent on it, and what you know about whether the customer is actually using the result: benefit reported, blocked, unknown or not yet due. If the answer is unknown, send the customer Lea's two questions today. You now hold one honest job record and, if needed, one question out.

No finished job yet? Set up the same page for your first one, with the promised result and a follow-up date filled in and the rest blank. No record of your hours? Reconstruct them from your calendar, mark them estimated, and start counting on the next job.

If you have another hour:

  1. Do the same for every job so far, and add once, at the bottom, the hours spent on approaches that sold nothing. This book comes with a blank customer-record spreadsheet (a CSV file, no formulas) with these columns; chapter fourteen uses its later ones.
  2. When a customer answers, respond and re-cost. Name the action, who does it and the next check, and add any correction time to that job once.
  3. Choose the next offer. Say what will change, which observation it comes from, and check next month's jobs against the hours you actually have.
What this review cannot establish

A few early jobs may mislead you. An unusual request can look like a whole market, and a difficult first attempt can become easier with practice. The WP Curve reports do not test this worksheet, and this chapter has no documented counter-case of an owner following this procedure and dropping a segment that would have worked. Keep the next commitment affordable and revisit the interpretation as evidence arrives. A tidy record does not remove uncertainty.

WP Curve's monthly reports are contemporary and first-party. They show separate observations about selling, scope and cancellations; they do not isolate the causes of cancellation or establish that any group of customers was permanently wrong for the service. Lea, her jobs and her customers' answers are invented.

From customer jobs to the whole business

The record now follows a promise into delivery, use and any correction. The next question is what those jobs leave after the business's shared costs and all your work. Reading Your Own Business and Paying Yourself brings the same records into one financial period.
