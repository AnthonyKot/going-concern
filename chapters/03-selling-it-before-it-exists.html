Selling It Before It Exists

The instrument that measures demand is the same instrument that creates the obligation, and it does both in one movement. There is no way to take the reading without also incurring the debt.

The decision What you are going to ask people to give up in order to prove they want this — and whether you could actually deliver what they would then have bought.

Move 1 The situation

You have had the conversations. Somebody described a real workaround, gave you a date, named a number they already spend. That was chapter two, and it got you as far as a conversation can go — which is not far, because everything in it was free for the person you were talking to.

The next question costs them something, and its answer is the first you can bank: will you pay for this now, before it exists?

Two things make this hard, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is that asking feels like a violation — worst if money has always been somebody else's department, but nobody is immune — because the request converts a pleasant conversation into a transaction. It does. That is the point. The discomfort is the price of the information and it is cheap.

The second is larger. The moment somebody pays you for a thing that does not exist you have sold a promise you may not be able to keep. Not might not want to — may not be able to, because you have never made this thing, do not know what it costs, and have just agreed a price and a date on the basis of a guess.

So: two questions on two separate axes, and you can pass one while failing the other catastrophically. What will they give up? And could you deliver what they just bought?

Move 2 What actually happened

Studio Neat — Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost, 2010–

Two people. Their first product was the Glif, a small moulded lump that clamps an iPhone to a tripod. In October 2010 they put it on Kickstarter with a working prototype and a funding goal of $10,000.

Kickstarter's mechanics are the argument. Backers pledge; nobody is charged unless the goal is reached; when it is, everyone is charged at once and the money is the maker's. There is no product yet and there may not be a factory. The buyer hands over a non-refundable payment for a thing that does not exist; the maker receives cash and a list of people they now owe.

The campaign opened on 3 October 2010 with nothing pledged. That day they emailed John Gruber of Daring Fireball and offered him a prototype. He linked to them the next morning, in a post of 167 characters. In their words: "1 hour and 25 minutes after the post went live, we surpassed our funding goal of $10,000, and finished the day with over $25,000." By the close on 2 November the archived page recorded "5,273 Backers · $137,417 pledged of $10,000 goal" — just under fourteen times the target, at about twenty-six dollars a head.

They started shipping "in early December, 2010" — a little over a month after the campaign closed.

They then did it four more times: a stylus, an ice-making kit, a syrup kit, and one iPhone app. Across all five they raised, by their own account, "nearly half a million dollars in total." All five were delivered.

One detail from their account matters more than the totals — the rule they give for when a campaign is allowed to launch at all: "a napkin sketch is not enough. If you are making a physical piece of hardware, a working prototype is a must." And beyond the prototype, it is "important to start conversations with manufacturers beforehand to make sure your design is not impossible (or extremely difficult) to manufacture." Their diagnosis of the alternative: "projects that are successfully funded but fail to deliver (in timely manner, or at all) are a result of not doing the necessary legwork up front."

The second axis had already cost them something before any money existed. A working prototype and a conversation with a factory is not a weekend. It is what most people skip, and skipping it is invisible until it is the only thing that matters.

There is an honest hole here, and they point at it themselves. The Glif was funded inside ninety minutes by one link from one blog. That is a fact about distribution, not about photography accessories, and this chapter would be lying if it offered $137,417 as a clean measurement of demand. It measures demand among the people who saw it, and a favour from a stranger determined who saw it.

One more number from their table, because nobody ever quotes it. Their February 2013 campaign, an iPhone app, had a goal of "$10,000" and closed at "$12,442 pledged · 1,211 backers" — and delivered on time. Against the Glif's 13.7 times, that is 1.24: same makers, same instrument, an answer an order of magnitude quieter. A preorder does not return yes or no. It returns a size, and the size is the finding.

Move 3 The reasoning

Everything people say about wanting your product is free to say. Evidence begins where saying it starts to cost them. So rank what you might ask for by what the buyer gives up — not by what it signals, not by how encouraged you feel afterwards.

The five rungs

1. Verbal enthusiasm. Costs nothing, tells you nothing, for all of chapter two's reasons. Not worthless — it says the pitch is intelligible — but not evidence about purchase.

2. Signed intent. A letter of intent, a memorandum, a "yes, put us down for ten." Costs a signature and no money. What it measures is whether someone will spend thirty seconds of social capital to be helpful, which is worth knowing and is not demand. In a company it is worth more, and how much more depends on what the signature has already cost: one that has been through procurement, legal and a budget holder is a different object from one a sympathetic director signed alone. Ask which it is. Until money moves, the second kind is a politeness with letterhead.

3. A refundable deposit. The first rung with money on it, and a genuine jump: the buyer opens a wallet, chooses an amount, and explains the charge to whoever else sees the statement. But the refund is an escape hatch, and it does more work than it appears to. A refundable deposit is a bet the buyer can unwind — it measures interest at a moment rather than commitment over time.

4. Non-refundable payment. The buyer parts with money they cannot get back for a thing that does not exist. The first rung genuinely hard to fake, where the Glif sat, and the rung at which your obligation becomes real.

5. A full-price repeat purchase. They bought, received, came back and bought again without a discount. The strongest evidence available anywhere in Part I, and the only rung that tests the product rather than the promise. Also the slowest, which is why it sits at the end of a sequence rather than the start.

Two rules. Climb until it hurts to ask, then ask — the rung you are avoiding is the one carrying the information. And read the size, not the verdict: the difference between fourteen times your target and one-and-a-quarter is the whole message, and both are technically a pass.

The other axis, which is not a rung

There is a sixth thing, and putting it on the same ladder is the mistake this chapter exists to prevent — because it does not measure the buyer at all. It measures you.

Can you deliver what they just bought, at that price, in that time?

You can be at the top of the buyer ladder and off the cliff on this one. Chapter four's case, Cydoc, had four customers paying full price on recurring revenue — rung five — and an eleven-year payback on each. The demand evidence was real; the business was not deliverable. Climbing does not get you here. Ask this separately, and answer it before you sell.

Three checks, none optional:

  • Can it be made at all? Studio Neat's version — a working prototype plus a conversation with whoever would have to manufacture it — is the cheapest form of the question, and it happens before the launch button.
  • At that price? Not the price you would like: the price you just took money at, including the thing, the shipping, the payment fees, the returns and the hours — and in the money it will actually be paid in, on the day you have to buy the materials, which where prices move is not the day you were paid.
  • By that date? Their advice is one sentence and you should take it literally: "However long you think the project will take to complete, you may do well to simply double that estimate."

And one structural point, which the aftermath below makes at length: success on the first axis makes the second axis harder — not proportionally, but in kind. Studio Neat's warning against stretch goals names it: "a slippery slope that can lead to biting off more than you can chew."

On Dutch ground

Whether "non-refundable" holds depends on who is selling to whom, and how — and for most readers of this book the answer is reassuring. If you sell to a business, none of what follows applies; that is a different regime. If you make things to order — goods "vervaardigde zaken, die niet geprefabriceerd zijn", made to the consumer's specification, not prefabricated — art. 6:230p BW puts you outside the rule too. Bespoke work, custom commissions, a service designed for the client: rung four is what it looks like.

The rule itself catches one situation: a trader selling a stock product to a consumer at a distance or away from business premises. There, art. 6:230o BW gives the consumer fourteen days to withdraw "zonder opgave van redenen" — without giving reasons. For goods the clock starts the day the consumer receives them, not the day they pay; for services, the day the contract is concluded. So in an ordinary online consumer preorder, rung four moves back toward rung three, and the wording of what you sell matters.

Whether a crowdfunding pledge is a contract of this kind depends on whether you are acting as a trader, the backer as a consumer, and the arrangement as a distance sale — a question for someone qualified, not for a book. If you are selling stock goods to consumers online, ask a lawyer who does consumer contracts before you write the terms, not after somebody asks for their money back.

Boundaries

A failed presale can be a reach failure rather than a demand failure. The Glif cleared its goal in eighty-five minutes because of one link. Show a preorder page to two hundred people and sell nothing, and you have learned something about two hundred people. This is chapter two's sampling problem in different clothes: read a presale alongside how many people saw it, never on its own.

The ladder ranks cost to the buyer, not relevance to your business. A non-refundable payment from someone who is not your customer is worth less than a refundable deposit from someone who is. Independence and fit come first; the rung is a tiebreak.

This case cannot tell you the ladder is correctly ordered. Studio Neat ran one instrument, five times, successfully. Nothing here compares a deposit against a payment against a repeat purchase for the same product, because nobody runs that experiment. The ordering is an argument from what each rung costs the person paying. The case supports one rung of it — that a non-refundable payment before existence is obtainable, informative and survivable — not the sequence.

Monday

  1. Write the sentence you are avoiding. "It will be X, it will be ready by [date], and I need half up front to start." Say it out loud once. If you cannot fill in the date, you do not have a deliverability answer, and that is Monday's real finding.
  2. Do the deliverability arithmetic before you ask anyone for money. One page: what it costs to make one, what it costs to make fifty, how many hours the fiftieth takes, what breaks between the two. Then double the schedule and see whether the price still works.
  3. Pick your rung and name it. Deposit, non-refundable payment, or paid discovery — a small fixed-price piece of real work, invoiced first, which is the version of rung four that suits most consultancies. If you sell something nobody preorders — coffee, haircuts, plumbing — rung four is a standing order: the office across the road commits to twenty sandwiches every Tuesday for a month, paid for the first week now. Nobody puts a deposit on a flat white; somebody will on a month of them. Write down which, and why not one rung higher.
  4. Go to five people from chapter two's conversations and ask. Not "would you buy" — take the money today. Record each no word for word; the reason that repeats is the thing to fix.
  5. Write down what happens if it works too well. If forty say yes instead of five: who makes them, with what money, by when. Deciding while the number is hypothetical is the only time you will decide it calmly.

Coolest Cooler — the demand test passed 265 times over

In the summer of 2014 Ryan Grepper put a cooler on Kickstarter — with a built-in blender, a speaker and a USB charger. Goal: $50,000. The archived page records the close: "62,642 Backers · $13,285,226 pledged of $50,000 goal" — two hundred and sixty-five times the target. Just over fifty thousand took the main tier: "Pledge $185 or more · 50215 backers · COOLEST COOLER." And the page gave a date: "Estimated delivery: Feb 2015."

On the first axis that is an unambiguous pass. Sixty-two thousand strangers made non-refundable payments, at $185, for an object that did not exist.

Now the second axis. In February 2015 there were no coolers. According to Matthew Hughes in The Register, the company had "spruiked over 60,000 units costing $185 apiece" and, two years after the campaign, "asked backers for a further $97 in order for them to receive their product before the July 4 holidays of 2016." A settlement with the Oregon Department of Justice followed. It shut down in December 2019 — five years past its delivery date — "with 20,000 orders unfulfilled," owing those backers money that "cannot be discharged in bankruptcy."

Why it collapsed is disputed and this chapter need not settle it; Grepper attributed the end to tariffs on Chinese imports, and there is no document here that would let anyone adjudicate that. The structural point survives whatever the cause. The pledge total was simultaneously the best demand evidence anyone in this book obtained and a contract to manufacture sixty thousand complex objects at a price fixed by someone who had never manufactured one. Both arrived in the same instant, from the same act, and only one of them felt like winning.

The demand test worked exactly as advertised; the deliverability test was never cleared. That is why making, price and date sit on a separate axis rather than as a sixth rung.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be someone who cleared both axes — independent buyers committed, making, price and date checked — delivered what they promised, and still had no business; or someone whose presale returned a clear no, who stopped and was wrong. Neither has been found in a checkable record, so the debt stands.
