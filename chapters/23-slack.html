Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 23

Capacity for Things Going Wrong

In June 2014 the two founders of WP Curve realised, in their weekly chat, that they did not have a lot to do. The same monthly report recorded that the share of happy customers had fallen from 96% to 82%. Was that free time a reserve? A free afternoon does not help a customer who needs an authorised answer at ten in the morning.

The decision What cover to arrange for one named disruption, what service it can maintain, and what you will change when its skill, time or funding limit is reached.

The month two founders had nothing to do

WP Curve sold "24 / 7 unlimited WordPress small fixes for $69 / month", which is a promise that only works if someone competent is always free to pick up the next fix. By June 2014, a year in, Dan Norris and Alex McClafferty had built that someone out of other people. "We increased the team to 15 people", Norris wrote, and "we are hiring every 2 weeks". For the first time a hire had been made by a team member rather than a founder, from "a 20 page process", with the founders "still involved at key intervals". Paying staff, approving affiliates, "90% of the Xero bookkeeping": all handed over.

Then the line every owner wants to write: "during our weekly chat, we realized that we don’t have a lot to do." "4 hour work week, baby!" And, straight after, the honest correction: "Unlikely. Even when I’m in a hammock I have my computer handy."

A few paragraphs further on, the same report: "This month our Happy % is down from 96% to 82%. This is not what we want." Norris gives his own reasons — people clicking the wrong link, a fix that "wasn’t quite correct the first time", requests far larger than a small fix. He does not blame the founders' free time, and neither does this chapter. Nobody measured whether one caused the other.

What the two facts in one report do show is the question this chapter is about. The founders had spare hours. Customers had fixes that went wrong. Spare hours become cover only if they belong to someone with the skill to redo the fix, the authority to decide it, and the time free at the moment it is needed. A hammock with a laptop in it is availability. It is not yet a plan.

Name the disruption and the promise at risk

Chapter twenty gave Maya and Sam a response route and exposed its weak point: it works only while Maya can answer. They are invented, as in the last chapter. Now Maya wants two mornings away — 09:00 to 13:00 on the Tuesday and Thursday of one week, with no calls or messages. A sudden absence across those windows would expose the same work. A month of illness would be a different scenario and a different plan.

Sam prepares a report each morning; for these two mornings he clears 09:00–12:00, an hour more than his usual two, because each draft now has to be reviewed inside the morning. The minimum service is each checked report by noon, questions acknowledged, and no unapproved calculation change reaching a customer. Routine questions are Sam's. Calculation decisions after noon and new quotes can wait for Maya at 13:00; the customers concerned have agreed that. Everything already promised still needs cover.

Write your own scenario this specifically: who or what is unavailable, for how long, what falls due inside that time, and the least you will still deliver. Then add one second pressure — two urgent requests at once. You are testing a defined limit, not proving that the business survives anything.

Count only the capacity that can do the work

Sam has hours, but not the authority to approve a new calculation, and every hour he spends reviewing comes out of production. Calling his remaining time spare would be WP Curve's hammock again. Maya looks outside: Dana, an experienced reporting specialist, can hold 09:00–12:00 on both mornings with no other client in those hours. Experience is not knowledge of these customers' calculations, so before Dana counts as cover she needs the context, her own permitted access, agreed decision limits and a tested sample, using the handover method.

Maya's estimate for each morning: thirty minutes for one calculation exception, thirty for the final review, thirty for reading requests and replying. Ninety minutes of work in a three-hour window, which looks comfortable. Then she tries the collision.

On paper, two calculation decisions both need answers by 10:00; the first is ready at 09:20, the second at 09:40, and each takes Dana thirty uninterrupted minutes. The first finishes at 09:50. The second cannot finish before 10:20. The morning has hours left, and the second deadline still fails. Hours are not interchangeable: Thursday's free time cannot repair a Tuesday deadline, and Dana's afternoon cannot answer a ten o'clock question.

Maya changes the arrangement before relying on it. She cannot count Dana twice or let Sam approve work outside his authority, so she asks the customer. They agree that a second report needing a new calculation may arrive by 13:00 instead of noon, so its decision no longer has to be made by 10:00; its draft only has to be ready for Dana by 11:30. Sam spends 09:00–09:50 on the parts of both reports that need no decision, so once each rule arrives, forty-five minutes finishes a draft. Both reports are reviewed by 12:00, and nothing after that is covered: Dana's window and Sam's both end at noon. The later deadline gives the second report time, not a person to finish it. So a review that runs past 12:00 is not absorbed by the extra hour; it is one of the triggers Maya writes down below. On the Tuesday, with Maya reachable, she finishes it herself and records the overrun. On the Thursday nobody would, which is why Thursday goes ahead only if Tuesday's reviews both finish inside Dana's window. Paying Dana to stay until 13:00 would cost another €60 a morning, and the forecast below has no room for it. A third such request is outside the plan. Keep the original promise and the agreed exception side by side in the record, so the plan never claims to preserve a service it has changed.

Dana is not the only answer. Cross-training Sam would reduce the dependency over time but costs his learning hours and room in his week. A smaller promise, or a planned closure, may suit an owner-run practice better. Whichever you compare, write down what customers receive under it. And do not call an unexplained idle hour waste, or reserve, until you know what it is doing.

Put the cover on dates

Maya's invented budget: two hours of Dana's preparation and rehearsal cost €120. Holding the two three-hour windows costs €360, paid before the week and not refundable once paid, used or not. That is €480 of cash, plus two hours of Maya's time and one of Sam's that displace other work. Keeping Dana ready afterwards would add a €60 monthly rehearsal. These are illustrative all-in amounts, not market rates or a view on how the arrangement would be classified.

In the dated forecast, day 0 is the covered Tuesday. Cash is €800 on day −7 after other planned payments; preparation is paid on day −6; a €500 receipt is expected on day −4; the €360 falls due on day −1. Her floor is €400. Move the receipt to day +3 and see what happens.

What the rehearsal showed, and what Maya decided

Dana's two paid hours go on one hour of context and access, half an hour on the collision, and half an hour on a retest. The first rehearsal finds the gap nobody predicted: Dana can read the report but cannot reach the calculation history she needs to approve it. Maya fixes the access. On the retest, using synthetic data, Dana reaches the approved answer in twenty-five minutes, and Sam can find the decision where he expects it.

That result is narrow, and Maya records it as narrow: access and one decision worked, under supervision, inside thirty minutes. It is not two full mornings, simultaneous live requests or Dana's own sick day. So Maya makes a bounded decision. She will pay the €360 on Monday and keep the Tuesday as a live test with herself reachable as a fallback, recording every delay and intervention. Thursday becomes her first unreachable morning only if Tuesday runs clean. The cover goes into the task notes with its triggers: a third overlapping request, a review running past Dana's window, Dana becoming unavailable, a changed method, or the forecast dropping below the floor. On any trigger the affected cover stops counting, and Maya finds a replacement, changes the promise with the customer, or declines the extra work. And if Thursday's held time is spent clearing Tuesday's backlog, it is not also covering Thursday.

A reader without a qualified person may end here with a real gap: the promise must change, a provider must be found, or the absence cannot be covered yet. A named gap with a date beats counting hours nobody can supply.

One question is still open, and Maya only notices it on Sunday night. The tool Dana will use was built by an outside developer; the data belongs to the customer. Is Dana actually allowed to use either? The €360 is due on Monday. Chapter twenty-four starts there.

Monday · one disruption, one real person

Do this one thing. Name one disruption you can see coming — your holiday, a supplier's shutdown, a week of illness — and the least you would still have to deliver during it. Beside it, write the one person who would cover that work, and four ticks: they have the skill, the authority, the access, and time free at the hour it is needed. You now hold either a cover plan with its first gap marked, or a precise description of the cover you do not have.

No one to name? Then the finding is the gap itself. Write what would have to change — the promise to customers, a provider to find, or the date of the absence — and the date you will decide. That is Monday done.

If you have another hour:

  1. Try the collision. Put two urgent requests into the same window and check whether both deadlines survive. Agree any changed customer promise before relying on it.
  2. Put the cost on dates. Preparation, the booking, minimum charges and upkeep go into the cash forecast, with one receipt moved late.
  3. Write the triggers. What would make the cover stop counting, who checks, and what must be restored before the next promise.
What this chapter rests on, and what it cannot show

WP Curve's June 2014 report is a founder's own monthly account, read from the registered July 2014 archive capture; later captures were not compared. It records hiring, delegation and a fall in the Happy % in the same month. It supplies no measured reserve, no inventory of usable hours and no absence that cover survived, and it does not connect the founders' free time with the fall; this chapter does not either.

Maya, Sam and Dana, their payments, workload and rehearsal are invented. The cover routine is this book's proposal.

Still owed: a small business that arranged and tested appropriate cover, accounted for its commitments and upkeep, and was made worse off by maintaining it — because preparation displaced more valuable work or the ongoing cost outweighed the disruptions it absorbed.
