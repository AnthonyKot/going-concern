Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 23

Slack

Every hour sold. Every person busy. The calendar full three weeks out and you proud of it, because full is what you spent four parts of this book getting to. Then one person is off for a month, or the biggest customer doubles the order, or it is you who is ill — and there is nothing to absorb it with, because absorbing was never on the calendar. This part asks whether the business lasts, and the first answer is a quantity you deliberately do not sell.

The decision How much of the business's capacity — people, hours, your own week — to hold unsold on purpose, against a shock you can name; in what unit to buy it so that it can be stopped; and how to tell reserve, which is sized and has a trigger, from waste, which is the same idle hours with nobody responsible for them.

Move 1 The situation

Full is what you aimed at. Chapter one told you to keep every cost variable until demand was proved; chapter nineteen made the first fixed cost a decision; and everything in between was about selling the hours you have. So the business runs at capacity, and every measure you were taught to like says so: utilisation high, nobody idle, the owner's week booked solid. It looks like efficiency. It is also the shape of a thing with no way to recover.

You already know the failure from the inside, because you are the reserve. When someone is off, you cover. When the order doubles, you work the weekend. When the process breaks, you are the process. That is slack, and it is where every small business keeps it: in the owner, unpriced, unsized, and spent first. The question this chapter asks is where else the reserve could live, how much of it there should be, and in what unit it should be bought — because a reserve you cannot stop paying for is the risk chapter one warned you about wearing a safer name.

Move 2 What actually happened

WP Curve was in this book at chapter ten for its first six months and at chapter fourteen for what one price did to two kinds of customer. This is the company one year on. Its product was a promise that cannot be kept without spare hands: "24 / 7 unlimited WordPress small fixes for $69 / month." Unlimited, around the clock, for a flat fee — a business whose whole offer is that someone is available when the customer needs them, which is to say a business that sells its slack.

Dan Norris's report for June 2014, published on 1 July, opens on the year's target: a quarter of a million in annual recurring revenue, "$20,833 / month", set when the business was six months old and under ten thousand. They passed it in June. "In June, our MRR grew by 22% to $23,535 (up from $19,274 in May)." The same percentage as May, he notes, but needing more customers to produce it — "57 net customer increase, just under 2 per day." By his own account, as with every figure in this book from him: a founder's report, and one where the revenue line, as chapter ten explained, was never cleanly the one product's.

What the report is about, though, is not the revenue. It is headed People, product and process, and the people section is where the month's real event is. "We increased the team to 15 people and we had our first staff employed by another team member, instead of me or Alex." That hire was made by a team admin in the Philippines, where most of the developers were, working from "a 20 page process" the founders had written. And the pace of hiring is stated as a fact about the business rather than a plan: "At our current growth rate we are hiring every 2 weeks, so it's important to have someone other than me looking after this process."

Then a list. Tasks that had been the founders' and were now the admin's: hiring, with the founders "still involved at key intervals"; approving affiliates; making the images for blog posts; promoting the content; paying staff and affiliates each month; "90% of the Xero bookkeeping." And the sentence that follows the list: "This has been such a weight of our shoulders that during our weekly chat, we realized that we don't have a lot to do." Norris makes the joke himself — "4 hour work week, baby!" — and kills it in the next line: "Unlikely. Even when I'm in a hammock I have my computer handy."

Read that as a month in which reserve appeared in three places at once. People: hires landing every two weeks, ahead of any one of them being needed on the day they started. Process: a hire made end to end without either founder, which means either founder could have been absent for it. The owners' week: two people who, for the first time, did not have a lot to do — and who did not, on their own account, treat that as time off.

The report has a fourth section, and it is the one that stops this from being a success story. "This month our Happy % is down from 96% to 82%. This is not what we want." The customer-rating system had been running for two months; the second reading fell fourteen points in the month the team grew and the revenue target was passed. Norris gives reasons — ratings hit by mistake, a fix that "wasn't quite correct the first time", customers sending "A4 size requests", a few let go — and does not connect the drop to the growth. The chapter cannot connect them either. What the page shows is both at once: capacity being built every fortnight, and the quality of what the capacity produced falling in the same month. His priorities for the rest of the year, in his own three words: "Continue growing"; "Work on client retention"; "Don't get bogged down doing everything ourselves."

Move 3 The reasoning

Reserve is sized, named, and bought in units you can stop

Slack is capacity you have paid for and not sold — and it is a reserve rather than waste only if you can say what it is held against, how much of it there is, and what would make you spend it. An idle afternoon nobody owns is waste. The same afternoon, held so that the one person who knows the supplier can be away for a fortnight without the business stopping, is a reserve, and the difference is entirely in whether it was decided.

The reserve lives in three places, and WP Curve's month shows all three. In people: the next hire made at the point where the current team is nearly full, not after it has been over-full for a quarter — Norris's every-two-weeks is the pace of a business that hires ahead of the day it cannot cope. In process: one thing that runs without the founder, so that the founder's absence is a fact the business can absorb rather than the shock it cannot — the hire made by the admin is the first proof that a process, not a person, was holding that job. In the owner's week: hours not booked, which is what "not a lot to do" is when it is read correctly — not a lifestyle, a margin, and the first one any business spends.

The boundary is chapter one, and it is a hard one. Webvan built its capacity ahead of demand and died of the fixed cost. Hiring ahead of need is the same act at a different scale, and the difference has to be stated, not felt: WP Curve added one person at a time, on revenue that was already recurring and had grown for eleven of twelve months, in a unit — one developer, one fortnight's pace — that could be stopped the month the growth stopped. A warehouse cannot be stopped. Reserve is bought in units you can stop buying, funded from revenue that already repeats, and sized against a shock you have named; capacity bought in any other way is a bet on demand, and chapter one already priced that. The fifteen-to-$23,535 ratio in the case is not a benchmark for anything: a flat-fee support business staffed largely in the Philippines has a cost per person the reader's business almost certainly does not.

Reserve is spent silently

The fourteen-point fall is the second half of the chapter. Reserve does not sit where you put it. It is consumed by growth, because a business adding two customers a day will use every hand it hires on the day it hires them; it is consumed by quality, because a team stretched thin produces the fix that "wasn't quite correct the first time"; and it is consumed by the owner, who is in the hammock with the laptop open. None of that is visible on the revenue line — June was the best month the company had had. It is visible only if you measure the reserve itself, which almost nobody does. Chapter twenty-two's three figures tell you whether the business is moving; the reserve needs a fourth, kept on the monthly page: hours available against hours sold, per person and for you, with the gap written down as the number it is.

The limit of the case is that it is one month of reserve being built, and no month of it being drawn on. Nothing on the page shows a developer leaving, a bad month, or a demand spike absorbed by the capacity that was hired ahead; the post was written before any of that had a chance to happen, and this chapter has not found the later report that would show it. So the case supports the accumulation and the cost of it, and nothing more — the claim that a reserve built this way holds when tested is this book's, and it is the claim Monday's last item exists to test in your business rather than in theirs.

Two other chapters own the edges. Chapter sixteen asked how much you need; this chapter never asks that, and a reserve held so that the owner can work less is sixteen's question wearing this chapter's word. And the reserve that has been spent for years — the business that runs only because its owner absorbs everything — is not a slack problem but the beginning of chapter twenty-eight's.

Monday

  1. Name the shock. One sentence each for three events: the one person off for four weeks; the largest customer doubling the order next month; you unavailable for a fortnight. For each, write what would break first. The reserve is held against those three sentences, not against a feeling.
  2. Count this week's hours, sold against available, per person and for you. The difference is the reserve you have. If it is zero everywhere, you have none, and item 3 is not optional. If it is large somewhere and nobody could say why, that is waste, and it becomes reserve the moment it is assigned to one of the three sentences.
  3. Buy the next unit in a form you can stop. The next hire made at eighty percent of full, not a hundred and ten; a contractor on a standing arrangement for the work chapter nineteen called a task; an afternoon a week blocked out on your own calendar and not sold. One unit, funded from the revenue that already repeats, sized against the sentence it answers.
  4. Take one process out of your head this month, end to end. The one that would break first in item 1. Chapter eighteen's handoff test, run to the point where somebody else has done it without you — WP Curve's admin ran a hire from a twenty-page document, and that is the standard.
  5. Run the drill. Take the fortnight. If the business cannot run two weeks without you, write down what broke, in order — that list is the reserve you actually need, and it is priced by what each item costs to hold. Then read item 2's number again a month after you are back, because the reserve you built will have been spent silently, and the only way to know is to count.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business that held reserve exactly as told — sized against a named shock, bought in a unit it could stop, funded from repeating revenue — and was killed by the reserve: the fixed cost that outlasted the revenue it was funded from when the downturn came faster than the unit could be stopped, or the capacity held idle so long that the people in it left or the skill in it rotted. WP Curve's month is not that case; it is reserve being built, with the cost showing in the same month, and no test of whether it held. Webvan, in chapter one, is not that case either — it bought capacity ahead of demand that had not been shown, which is the thing this chapter tells you not to do. The one that would count is a business that did it right and lost anyway, and the honest guess at why it is hard to find is that a reserve which failed looks, afterwards, like a cost that should never have been carried, and gets written up as that.
