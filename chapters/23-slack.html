Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 23

Slack

Every hour sold. Every person busy. The calendar full three weeks out and you proud of it, because full is what you spent four parts of this book getting to — or not full, and nobody able to say why. Then one person is off for a month, or the biggest customer doubles the order, or it is you who is ill — and there is nothing to absorb it with, because absorbing was never on the calendar. This part asks whether the business lasts, and the first answer is a quantity you deliberately do not sell.

The decision How much of the business's capacity — people, hours, your own week — to hold unsold on purpose, against a shock you can name; in what unit to buy it so that it can be stopped; and how to tell reserve, which is sized and has a trigger, from waste, which is the same idle hours with nobody responsible for them.

Move 1 The situation

Full is what you aimed at. Chapter one told you to keep as much of the cost base variable as you could until demand was proved; chapter nineteen made the first fixed cost a decision; and everything in between was about selling the hours you have. So the business runs at capacity, and every measure you were taught to like says so: utilisation high, nobody idle, the owner's week booked solid. It looks like efficiency. It is also the shape of a thing with no way to recover.

You already know the failure from the inside, because you are the reserve. When someone is off, you cover. When the order doubles, you work the weekend. When the process breaks, you are the process. That is slack, and it is where every small business keeps it: in the owner, unpriced, unsized, and spent first. The question this chapter asks is where else the reserve could live, how much of it there should be, and in what unit it should be bought — because a reserve you cannot stop paying for is the risk chapter one warned you about wearing a safer name.

Move 2 What actually happened

WP Curve was in this book at chapter ten for its first six months and at chapter fourteen for what one price did to two kinds of customer. This is the company one year on. Its product was a promise that cannot be kept without spare hands: "24 / 7 unlimited WordPress small fixes for $69 / month." Unlimited, around the clock, for a flat fee — a business whose whole offer is that someone is available when the customer needs them, which is to say a business that sells its slack.

Dan Norris's report for June 2014, published on 1 July, opens on the year's target: a quarter of a million in annual recurring revenue, "$20,833 / month", set when the business was six months old and under ten thousand. They passed it in June. "In June, our MRR grew by 22% to $23,535 (up from $19,274 in May)." The same percentage as May, he notes, but needing more customers to produce it — "57 net customer increase, just under 2 per day." The revenue line, as chapter ten set out, is the company's across several products, not WP Curve's alone.

What the report is about, though, is not the revenue. It is headed People, product and process, and the people section carries the month's event. "We increased the team to 15 people and we had our first staff employed by another team member, instead of me or Alex." That hire was made by "another team member", with the process handed to a team admin in the Philippines, where most of the developers were, working from "a 20 page process" the founders had written. And the pace of hiring is stated as a fact about the business rather than a plan: "At our current growth rate we are hiring every 2 weeks, so it's important to have someone other than me looking after this process."

Then the list of what the founders had handed over. Tasks that had been the founders' and were now the admin's: hiring, with the founders "still involved at key intervals"; approving affiliates; making the images for blog posts; promoting the content; paying staff and affiliates each month; "90% of the Xero bookkeeping." And the sentence that follows the list: "This has been such a weight of our shoulders that during our weekly chat, we realized that we don't have a lot to do." Norris makes the joke himself — "4 hour work week, baby!" — and kills it in the same breath: "Unlikely. Even when I'm in a hammock I have my computer handy."

Two of the three places this chapter will name are on the page. Process: a hire run from a written document, with the founders at the key points and not in between. The owners' week: two people who, by their own account, did not have a lot to do — and the post shows that margin being spent in the same line, in Alex McClafferty's aside: "[Alex: I think Dan will come up with something...]" The third place is not there. People were being added at the rate the customers were — "At our current growth rate we are hiring every 2 weeks" — and under the post Norris tells a commenter that "we are growing so quickly we don't want to grow faster". A hire a fortnight was the business staying level, not getting ahead.

Near the end of the report, under Service quality: "This month our Happy % is down from 96% to 82%. This is not what we want." The rating system had at least a month behind it — "another month of data from Hively" — and this reading fell fourteen points in the month the team grew and the revenue target was passed. Norris gives reasons — ratings hit by mistake, a fix that "wasn't quite correct the first time" and right the second, customers sending "A4 size requests", a few who were let go — and none of them is the size or the newness of the team. The page shows two figures in one month and draws no line between them: the best revenue the company had reported, and the one quality number it kept, down fourteen points. His priorities for the rest of the year, one under each of his headings: "Continue growing"; "Work on client retention"; "Don't get bogged down doing everything ourselves."

Move 3 The reasoning

Reserve is sized, named, and bought in units you can stop

Slack is capacity you have paid for and not sold — and it is a reserve rather than waste only if you can say what it is held against, how much of it there is, and what would make you spend it. An idle afternoon nobody owns is waste. The same afternoon, held so that the one person who knows the supplier can be away for a fortnight without the business stopping, is a reserve, and the difference is entirely in whether it was decided.

The reserve lives in three places, and WP Curve's month shows all three. In people: the next hire made while the current team still has hours unsold, not after it has been over-full for a quarter. WP Curve's month is the pace without the margin — a hire a fortnight to stay level with two customers a day — and the reserve is the one unit beyond that pace, which is this book's prescription and not something Norris reports doing. In process: one thing that runs without the founder, so that the founder's absence is a fact the business can absorb rather than the shock it cannot — the hire run by the admin from a document, with the founders at the checkpoints, is the first sign that a process rather than a person was holding most of that job. In the owner's week: hours not booked. "We don't have a lot to do" is that margin, and the post shows it being spent in the same line — the partner's prediction that Dan will come up with something — which is what happens to the owner's reserve first, in every business.

The boundary is chapter one, and it is the same rule from the other side. Webvan committed to a building whose economics needed eight thousand orders a day before the first customer had ordered; the warehouses it had not yet built it could and did stop, and the one standing it could not. Chapter one's line was never against fixed cost — it was against taking it on in the wrong order — and a reserve is a fixed cost taken on in the right one: after the revenue repeats, in a unit you can put down. WP Curve's fortnightly hire passes that test whether or not it was a reserve: one person at a time, on a company-wide revenue line that had grown by more than fifteen percent in eleven of its twelve months, in a unit that could be stopped the month the growth stopped. The reserve this chapter asks for is one such unit more than the pace needs, bought the same way. Reserve is bought in units you can stop buying, funded from revenue that already repeats, and sized against a shock you have named; capacity bought in any other way is a bet on demand, and chapter one already priced that. The fifteen-to-$23,535 ratio in the case is not a benchmark for anything: a flat-fee support business staffed largely in the Philippines has a cost per person yours almost certainly does not.

Reserve is spent silently

Reserve does not sit where you put it. It is consumed by growth, because a business adding two customers a day will use every hand it hires on the day it hires them — which is what WP Curve's fortnightly hire was; it is consumed by quality, because the hours that would have made the fix right the first time are the hours that were never unsold — and WP Curve's 82% is the only figure on the page that could have shown it, which is not the same as showing it; and it is consumed by the owner, whose partner has already predicted he will come up with something. None of that is visible on the revenue line — June was the biggest month the company had reported. It is visible only if you measure the reserve itself, which almost nobody does. Chapter twenty-two's three figures tell you whether the business is moving; the reserve goes on the monthly page that chapter set up: hours available against hours sold, per person and for you, counted weekly and read once a month, with the gap written down as the number it is.

The limit of the case is that it is one month of reserve being built and no month of it being drawn on. Nothing on the page shows a developer leaving, a bad month, or a demand spike absorbed by hands hired in time; the post was written before any of that could have happened. So the case shows the accumulation and its cost, and nothing more. That a reserve built this way holds when tested is a claim, and Monday's last item exists to test it in your business rather than in theirs.

Two other chapters own the edges. Chapter sixteen asked how much you need, and left one thing to this part: whether the gap between a normal year and a bad one is a buffer, a policy or a bigger number. The buffer is reserve in money, and it is a later chapter's; this one holds reserve in hours, and never asks how much you need — a reserve held so that the owner can work less is sixteen's question wearing this chapter's word. And the reserve that has been spent for years — the business that runs only because its owner absorbs everything — is not a slack problem but the beginning of chapter twenty-eight's.

Monday

  1. Name the shock. One sentence each for three events: the one person off for four weeks; the largest customer doubling the order next month; you unavailable for a fortnight. For each, write what would break first. The reserve is held against those three sentences, not against a feeling.
  2. Count this week's hours, sold against available, per person and for you, and keep counting every week; the monthly page reads the four weeks together. The difference is the reserve you have. If it is zero everywhere, you have none, and item 3 is not optional. If it is large somewhere and nobody could say why, that is waste, and it becomes reserve the moment it is assigned to one of the three sentences.
  3. Buy the next unit in a form you can stop. The next hire made while item 2's gap still covers item 1's first sentence, not after it has been zero for a quarter; a contractor on a standing arrangement for the work chapter nineteen called a task; an afternoon a week blocked out on your own calendar and not sold. One unit, funded from the revenue that already repeats, sized against the sentence it answers.
  4. Take one process out of your head this month, end to end. The one that would break first in item 1. Chapter eighteen's handoff test, run to the point where somebody else has done it without you — WP Curve's admin ran a hire from a twenty-page document with the founders "still involved at key intervals" — that is halfway; the standard is the version where they were not.
  5. Run the drill. Put the fortnight in the calendar this month, with a date, and take it. If the business cannot run two weeks without you, write down what broke, in order, and put a monthly cost beside each line — that list, priced, is the reserve you actually need. Then read item 2's number again a month after you are back, because the reserve you built will have been spent silently, and the only way to know is to count.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business that held reserve exactly as told — sized against a named shock, bought in a unit it could stop, funded from repeating revenue — and was killed by the reserve: the fixed cost that outlasted the revenue it was funded from when the downturn came faster than the unit could be stopped, or the capacity held idle so long that the people in it left or the skill in it rotted. WP Curve's month is not that case; it is reserve being built, with the cost showing in the same month, and no test of whether it held. Webvan, in chapter one, is not that case either — it bought capacity ahead of demand that had not been shown, which is the thing this chapter tells you not to do. The one that would count is a business that did it right and lost anyway, and the honest guess at why it is hard to find is that a reserve which failed looks, afterwards, like a cost that should never have been carried, and gets written up as that.
