Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 15

Paying Yourself

Every calculation in the last chapter charged the customer for somebody's work. One person's was missing. A business that is profitable only because nobody costed the founder is not profitable — it is a badly paid job that also carries the downside of ownership, and the arithmetic will not tell you which one you have until you put the number in.

The decision What it would cost to replace your own labour at market rate, whether the business covers that cost, and what you change if it does not.

Move 1 The situation

Owner pay is usually a residual: whatever survives after suppliers, the tax authority and anyone on payroll. That ordering feels responsible. It is also the most effective way to hide whether a business works, because a cost that is never entered is a cost that never fails a test.

Two different things are being confused when this happens, and they need separating first:

  • The owner's wage is the cost of the work you do. Somebody has to write the code, answer the customers, chase the invoices. If you left tomorrow the business would have to pay someone, redesign the work, automate it or stop doing it — each of which has a cost, and zero is not among them. That cost of operating exists whether or not money moves.
  • The owner's profit is the return on owning the thing — on the capital you put in and the risk you carry. It is what is left after all costs, including the wage.

Roll them together and you lose the ability to answer the only question Part III has been asking. A business reporting €40,000 of profit before anything is charged for your labour has told you nothing yet. If replacing that labour would cost €70,000, the adjusted result is a €30,000 loss. If replacing it would cost €25,000, the adjusted profit is €15,000 and what you take is a share of it. Same reported figure, two opposite conclusions, and the accounts as normally kept cannot distinguish them. What actually reached your household is a third number, and chapter thirteen is why.

The previous chapter computed what a customer was worth after delivery, setup and support. Every one of those lines assumed somebody was paid to do the work. Where the founder does it, the contribution figure is overstated by exactly what they are not paid — so the customer economics of an owner-operated business flatter, and the flattery grows with how hard the owner works.

Move 2 What actually happened

There is a jurisdiction that refuses to let this stay invisible, and its refusal is instructive precisely because management is not what it is for. The rule is not a viability test; nothing in it asks whether the business works. Its effect is to stop an owner who works through their own company from making the recognised value of that work arbitrarily small merely because they control both sides of the decision — and, incidentally, to enforce the discipline this chapter is arguing for, on some owners and not others.

The structure is worth seeing before the details, because the shape is what transfers and the details do not. Which side of the fork you start on turns on legal form and on your ownership-and-work relationship with the entity — not on how much you choose to take out. Size and sector can affect the figure once a regime applies; they do not move a sole trader into one. That is another legal-form fork of the kind chapter twelve found in the filing rules, and it catches people the same way: they assume a rule they have heard of applies to them, or that it does not, and the form decides.

For an owner who works for an incorporated company in which they hold a substantial interest, the state imposes a statutory method for arriving at the salary recognised for payroll-tax purposes. Not a recommendation, and not a figure the owner may set low by preference: it starts from the highest of three benchmarks, one a fixed annual amount, and a lower result must be substantiated rather than asserted. The owner is treated as receiving that salary. Read sideways, that makes the value of an owner's labour independently testable rather than whatever the owner chooses to call it — and it can recognise a labour cost without the money having conveniently moved.

For an owner trading in their own name, none of that exists. There is no salary, because there is nobody to pay one: the business is not a separate person. Money moved from the business to the household is a withdrawal, and a withdrawal is not a cost — it does not reduce the profit the owner is taxed on. Which produces the sharpest version of this chapter's problem, written into the tax system itself: for this owner, the cost of their own labour is structurally invisible. The statutory profit calculation contains no wage for their own work. The accounts can therefore be complete and correct while reporting a profit from which one of the business's largest inputs has never been deducted.

So one owner is handed the discipline whether they want it or not, and the other has to supply it themselves. The figures, articles and wording are quarantined in the box below; nothing above depends on them.

Move 3 The reasoning

Price the jobs, not the person

Chapter five established the rule this chapter runs on: your labour has a price even when no money moves, and the price is what it would cost to replace. The mistake is to ask what you are worth — unanswerable, and slightly demoralising. Ask what the business would have to pay to get the work done.

That means splitting yourself into the jobs you actually do — delivery, selling, bookkeeping, support — and pricing each one properly. Properly means four things: several vacancies or contractor quotes rather than one advert; loaded with what an employer pays on top of the gross figure; costed at the hours a competent replacement would need rather than the hours you spend, which cuts both ways; and asked, job by job, whether a successor would hire it, outsource it, automate it or stop doing it at all. The result is usually uncomfortable in a specific way: the founder discovers they are doing forty hours of skilled work and twenty hours of work they could hire for a fraction, and that the second twenty is what has been crowding out the first.

The test, and its three honest answers

Put that total into a management restatement — a copy of last year's profit and loss with one extra line for the cost of your own labour — and recompute. A copy, and only ever a copy: the box below is there because for a sole trader this charge is not deductible in the statutory calculation, and a shadow document is exactly where such a cost belongs. Never the filed accounts. One of three things is then true, and all three are useful:

  • The business covers it and still makes a profit. Then the wage is affordable and the question of whether to draw it is a cash question, not a viability one.
  • It does not cover it, and you can say why and by when. This is a financed period: the owner is deliberately subsidising the business with underpaid labour, for a stated time. That is a legitimate position and it is the position most early businesses are in. It stops being legitimate when the date is never written down, because then it cannot arrive.
  • It does not cover it and nothing identified would change that. Then over the period you measured the business did not cover the market cost of your labour, and nothing you have found closes the gap. Raise the price, cut the work, redesign the model — or this is chapter four's stopping rule arriving with a number attached rather than a feeling. Note the limit: one restatement of one year says the gap was not closed then, not that the business never worked.

The second answer is where most of the damage happens, because it looks like the third from the inside and like the first from the outside. Writing the date down is what separates them.

Counting it is not paying it

Chapter thirteen's distinction returns here in its most personal form. Deciding your labour costs €50,000 does not put €50,000 anywhere. Owners routinely leave it unpaid, or take money whenever the account looks healthy, so the household absorbs the volatility and nobody measures how much. Keep two figures and keep them apart: the imputed cost of your labour, which is a management number for judging the business, and the cash that actually reached your household, which has its own timing.

Whether that gap is also a debt the business owes you is a third question, and legal form answers it, not intention. In a company, salary due or accrued is an accounting and payroll matter — ask your accountant how it must be recorded. A sole trader cannot straightforwardly owe themselves a wage; there the imputed cost stays a management adjustment. Do not let one word do all three jobs.

Boundaries

Knowing the number is not the same as drawing it. Nothing here says to pay yourself a market wage the business cannot afford; at the wrong moment that is a way to run out of cash faster. Count the cost always, then decide what to draw as a separate question with the cash in front of you.

Where a state sets a number, it is not your number. A statutory minimum shows the discipline — somebody has to price the owner's labour and it cannot be zero by preference — but it is a tax benchmark, settled for collecting tax. Carrying it into a price or a viability test borrows a number computed for a different question. The figure this chapter wants is the one built above: what your own jobs would cost to hire.

Replacement cost is an estimate, and it flexes to suit the estimator. An owner who wants the business to look viable will price their labour low; one arguing for a raise will price it high. It is a discipline, not a measurement, and the only defence is to price the jobs against advertised rates rather than against your own sense of worth.

This chapter has no company case. Like chapter twelve, it is anchored on a regime rather than a business, by decision rather than for want of looking. No owner in this book is shown computing their replacement cost and acting on it, and that debt is real.

It also does not tell you how much you need. Everything above is about what the business must cover. What is enough for the life attached to it is a different question with a different answer, and it is the next chapter.

On Dutch ground · what the state says about paying yourself

Which of the two regimes applies to you turns on legal form — and, for the second, on working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest. Read the fork first; the figures below are meaningless attached to the wrong side of it.

Trading in your own name — an eenmanszaak — there is no salary and no minimum. Money you take out is a privéonttrekking: "Privéonttrekkingen zijn uitgaven die u uit het ondernemingsvermogen hebt betaald voor uzelf", and the consequence is the one that matters here — "omdat het geen zakelijke kosten zijn, zijn privéonttrekkingen niet aftrekbaar van uw ondernemingswinst". Not deductible: your own labour does not appear as a cost in the profit the business is taxed on. Note also that being an entrepreneur is not automatically being one for income tax — "Lang niet iedere ondernemer is ondernemer voor de inkomstenbelasting."

This box covers that form and the one below, and no others: a vof or maatschap is a different arrangement, with partner labour allowances and profit shares this chapter carries no evidence about.

Working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest, the gebruikelijkloonregeling applies: "Bent u werknemer in een vennootschap of coöperatie waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt? Dan geldt voor u de gebruikelijkloonregeling." Its principle, in the tax authority's words, is that "een aanmerkelijkbelanghouder wordt geacht een loon te krijgen dat normaal is voor het niveau en de duur van zijn arbeid".

Article 12a of the Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 sets it out. "Ten aanzien van de werknemer die arbeid verricht ten behoeve van een lichaam waarin hij of zijn partner een aanmerkelijk belang heeft", the salary "wordt het in het kalenderjaar van dat lichaam genoten loon ten minste gesteld op het hoogste van de volgende bedragen" — the highest of three: "het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking"; "het hoogste loon van de werknemers die in dienst zijn van het lichaam" or a connected company; and "c. € 58.000." The tax authority states the same figure and its recent history: "ten minste € 58.000 in 2026 (in 2025 en 2024 was dat € 56.000)".

There are routes to a lower figure and they run on evidence, not on assertion. Lid 2 applies where "de inhoudingsplichtige aannemelijk maakt dat het hoogste bedrag, bedoeld in het eerste lid, hoger is dan het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking". The comparison must be with employment "waarbij een aanmerkelijk belang geen rol speelt". And where the salary the calculation itself arrives at is small, the calculation stops forcing it upwards — lid 4: "Het eerste en tweede lid zijn niet van toepassing indien het bij toepassing van het eerste tot en met derde lid vast te stellen loon voor de arbeid in het lichaam" and connected companies, in the calendar year, "niet hoger is dan € 5.000" — a threshold the Belastingdienst says counts across everything you do: "De grens van € 5.000 geldt voor het totaal van uw werkzaamheden voor alle vennootschappen of coöperaties waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt."

This box is on a clock the statute itself sets. Lid 7: "Het in het eerste lid vermelde bedrag wordt bij het begin van het kalenderjaar bij ministeriële regeling vervangen door een ander" — the amount is replaced at the start of each calendar year. Replaced does not mean changed: it stood at € 56.000 for 2024 and again for 2025. So if you are reading this in a later year, the figure above is unverified by construction. Recheck it before using it; only the structure keeps without checking.

Checked 2 August 2026 against Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 art. 12a and the Belastingdienst's own guidance. This box states which regime attaches to which legal form and what the statute says. It does not tell you what your figure should be, whether a lower one is defensible in your circumstances, what counts as the most comparable employment for your work, or anything about dividends. Those belong to a belastingadviseur or accountant. Nothing here is a reason to choose one legal form over another; that decision is not this book's.

Monday

  1. List the jobs, not the hours. Write down every distinct role you perform in the business and roughly what share of your week each takes. Delivery, selling, support, bookkeeping, whatever else. The list itself is usually the surprise.
  2. Price each one against several real adverts or quotes. Three or four current rates, not one and not a sense of your own worth, loaded with what an employer pays on top of the advertised gross, at the hours a competent replacement would need. Where a successor would automate or drop the task, price that instead. Total it — that is your replacement cost, and the rest depends on it.
  3. Add one line to a copy of last year's profit and loss. Label it owner labour — management adjustment, not a tax deduction, and leave the filed accounts alone. Then read the profit figure again and decide which of the three answers in Move 3 you are looking at.
  4. If it is the second answer, write the date. The condition and the month by which the business must cover the wage. Put it where you will see it: this is what stops a financed period becoming a permanent one by default.
  5. Keep two columns for the last twelve months. Owner labour recognised as a cost for management; cash actually paid or withdrawn to the household. If they diverge widely, your household has been absorbing the business's cash volatility, and you should know by how much.
  6. If you have a bv, check your position against the box — then ask someone. Take the fork, the article and the figures to a belastingadviseur or accountant. The answer is defensible when right and expensive when wrong, and it is not one to settle from a book.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

Two would do, and they attack different joints. One: an owner who priced their labour honestly, found the business could not cover it, and shut down or sold — while a peer who never did the calculation carried on through the same lean years and came out with a business worth having. Two, the sharper one: an owner who priced every task at like-for-like replacement, concluded the business could not carry them, and was wrong, because the successor who never arrived would have automated, combined or dropped half that work. Internally consistent, and describing a replacement nobody would have hired. Note what is not owed — an owner who drew a full market wage the business could not afford is no counter-case, because this chapter never asks anyone to draw it.
