Paying Yourself

Every calculation in the last chapter charged the customer for somebody's work. One person's was missing. A business that is profitable only because nobody costed the founder is not profitable — it is a badly paid job that also carries the downside of ownership, and the arithmetic will not tell you which one you have until you put the number in.

The decision What it would cost to replace your own labour at market rate, whether the business covers that cost, and what you change if it does not.

Move 1 The situation

Owner pay is usually treated as a residual: whatever survives after the suppliers, the tax authority and anyone on payroll have been dealt with. That ordering feels responsible. It is also the single most effective way to hide whether a business works, because a cost that is never entered is a cost that never fails a test.

Two different things are being confused when this happens, and they need separating before anything else in this chapter makes sense:

  • The owner's wage is the cost of the work you do. Somebody has to write the code, answer the customers, chase the invoices. If you left tomorrow, the business would pay someone to do it. That is a cost of operating, and it exists whether or not money moves.
  • The owner's profit is the return on owning the thing — on the capital you put in and the risk you carry. It is what is left after all costs, including the wage.

Roll them together and you lose the ability to answer the only question Part III has been asking. A business that pays you €40,000 has told you nothing yet. If replacing your labour would cost €70,000, it is losing €30,000 a year and calling it a living. If replacing you would cost €25,000, it is making €15,000 of profit and you are drawing some of it. Same bank transfer, two opposite conclusions, and the accounts as normally kept cannot distinguish them.

The previous chapter computed what a customer was worth after direct delivery cost, setup cost and support. Every one of those lines assumed somebody was paid to do the work. Where the founder does it, the contribution figure is overstated by exactly the amount they are not being paid — which means the customer economics of an owner-operated business are systematically flattering, and the flattery grows with how hard the owner works.

Move 2 What actually happened

There is a jurisdiction that refuses to let this stay invisible, and its refusal is instructive precisely because management is not what it is for. The rule is not a viability test; nothing in it asks whether the business works. Its effect is to stop an owner who works through their own company from making the recognised value of that work arbitrarily small merely because they control both sides of the decision. Whatever the intent behind it, the effect is to enforce the discipline this chapter is arguing for — on some owners and not others.

The structure is worth seeing before the details, because the shape is what transfers and the details do not. Which side of the fork you start on turns on legal form and on your ownership-and-work relationship with the entity — not on how much you choose to take out. Size and sector can affect the figure once a regime applies; they do not move a sole trader into one. That is another legal-form fork of the kind chapter twelve found in the filing rules, and it catches people the same way: they assume a rule they have heard about applies to them, or assume it does not, and the form is what actually decides.

For an owner who works for an incorporated company in which they hold a substantial interest, the state imposes a statutory method for arriving at the salary recognised for payroll-tax purposes. Not a recommendation, and not a figure the owner is free to set low by preference: the calculation starts from the highest of three benchmarks, one of which is a fixed annual amount, and a lower result has to be substantiated rather than asserted. The owner is treated as receiving that salary. Read sideways, that makes the value of an owner's labour independently testable rather than whatever the owner chooses to call it — and it can put a recognised labour cost on the business without the same amount having conveniently moved through the bank.

For an owner trading in their own name, none of that exists. There is no salary, because there is nobody to pay one: the business is not a separate person. Money moved from the business to the household is a withdrawal, and a withdrawal is not a cost — it does not reduce the profit the owner is taxed on. Which produces the sharpest version of this chapter's problem, written into the tax system itself: for this owner, the cost of their own labour is structurally invisible. The statutory profit calculation contains no wage for their own work — there is no line for it, because there is no employment. The accounts can therefore be complete and correct while reporting a profit from which one of the business's largest inputs, often the largest, has never been deducted.

So one owner is handed the discipline whether they want it or not, and the other has to supply it themselves. The specific figures, articles and wording are quarantined in the box below, because they are Dutch, they are restated annually, and nothing above depends on them.

Move 3 The reasoning

Price the jobs, not the person

Chapter five established the rule this chapter runs on: your labour has a price even when no money moves, and the price is what it would cost to replace. Applied here, the mistake is to ask what you are worth, which is unanswerable and slightly demoralising. Ask instead what the business would have to pay to get the work done.

That means splitting yourself into the jobs you actually do — the delivery work, the selling, the bookkeeping, the support — and pricing each at what hiring it would cost, at the hours it genuinely takes. The result is usually uncomfortable in a specific way: the founder discovers they are doing forty hours of skilled work and twenty hours of work they could hire for a fraction, and that the second twenty is what has been crowding out the first.

The test, and its three honest answers

Put that total into the accounts as a cost line, whether or not it has ever been paid, and recompute last year. One of three things is true, and all three are useful:

  • The business covers it and still makes a profit. Then the wage is affordable and the question of whether to draw it is a cash question, not a viability one.
  • It does not cover it, and you can say why and by when. This is a financed period: the owner is investing labour instead of capital, deliberately, with a date. That is a legitimate position and it is the position most early businesses are in. It stops being legitimate when the date is never written down, because then it cannot arrive.
  • It does not cover it and nothing identified would change that. Then the business is not profitable and never has been, and this is chapter four's stopping rule arriving with a number attached rather than a feeling.

The second answer is where most of the damage happens, because it looks like the third from the inside and like the first from the outside. Writing the date down is what separates them.

Being owed is not being paid

Chapter thirteen's distinction returns here in its most personal form. Deciding your wage is €50,000 does not put €50,000 anywhere. Owners routinely accrue pay they never draw, or draw irregularly whenever the account looks healthy, which means the household absorbs the volatility of the business and nobody measures how much. The number you have set is a cost for judging the business. The transfers are a separate question with its own timing, and the two should be recorded separately so that neither hides the other.

Boundaries

Knowing the number is not the same as drawing it. Nothing here says to pay yourself a market wage the business cannot afford; done at the wrong moment that is simply a way to run out of cash faster. The instruction is to count the cost, always, and then decide what to draw as a separate question with the cash in front of you.

Where a state sets a number, it is not your number. A statutory minimum demonstrates the discipline — somebody has to put a figure on the owner's labour, and it cannot be zero by preference — but it is a tax benchmark, informed by comparable employment and settled for the purposes of collecting tax. It is not the management cost of your labour, and carrying it into a price or a viability test would be borrowing a number that was never computed for that question. The figure this chapter asks for is the one built above: what your own jobs would cost to hire.

Replacement cost is an estimate, and it flexes to suit the estimator. An owner who wants the business to look viable will price their labour low; one arguing for a raise will price it high. It is a real discipline and it is not a measurement, and the only defence is to price the jobs against actual advertised rates rather than against your own sense of worth.

This chapter has no company case. Like chapter twelve, it is anchored on a regime rather than a business, by decision rather than for want of looking. No owner in this book is shown computing their replacement cost and acting on it, and that debt is real.

It also does not tell you how much you need. Everything above is about what the business must cover. What is enough for the life attached to it is a different question with a different answer, and it is the next chapter.

On Dutch ground · what the state says about paying yourself

Which of the two regimes applies to you turns on legal form — and, for the second, on working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest. Read the fork first; the figures below are meaningless attached to the wrong side of it.

Trading in your own name — an eenmanszaak — there is no salary and no minimum. Money you take out is a privéonttrekking: "Privéonttrekkingen zijn uitgaven die u uit het ondernemingsvermogen hebt betaald voor uzelf", and the consequence is the one that matters here — "omdat het geen zakelijke kosten zijn, zijn privéonttrekkingen niet aftrekbaar van uw ondernemingswinst". Not deductible: your own labour does not appear as a cost in the profit the business is taxed on. Note also that being an entrepreneur is not automatically being one for income tax — "Lang niet iedere ondernemer is ondernemer voor de inkomstenbelasting."

That is one of the two forms this box covers; the other is below. It covers no others. A vof or maatschap is a different arrangement — partners agree labour allowances and profit shares between themselves, and those raise distinctions this chapter carries no evidence about. Do not read the paragraph above as covering one.

Working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest, the gebruikelijkloonregeling applies: "Bent u werknemer in een vennootschap of coöperatie waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt? Dan geldt voor u de gebruikelijkloonregeling." Its principle, in the tax authority's words, is that "een aanmerkelijkbelanghouder wordt geacht een loon te krijgen dat normaal is voor het niveau en de duur van zijn arbeid".

Article 12a of the Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 sets it out. "Ten aanzien van de werknemer die arbeid verricht ten behoeve van een lichaam waarin hij of zijn partner een aanmerkelijk belang heeft", the salary "wordt het in het kalenderjaar van dat lichaam genoten loon ten minste gesteld op het hoogste van de volgende bedragen" — the highest of three: "het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking"; "het hoogste loon van de werknemers die in dienst zijn van het lichaam" or a connected company; and "c. € 58.000." The tax authority states the same figure and its recent history: "ten minste € 58.000 in 2026 (in 2025 en 2024 was dat € 56.000)".

There are routes to a lower figure and they run on evidence, not on assertion. Lid 2 applies where "de inhoudingsplichtige aannemelijk maakt dat het hoogste bedrag, bedoeld in het eerste lid, hoger is dan het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking". The comparison must be with employment "waarbij een aanmerkelijk belang geen rol speelt". And where the salary the calculation itself arrives at is small, the calculation stops forcing it upwards — lid 4: "Het eerste en tweede lid zijn niet van toepassing indien het bij toepassing van het eerste tot en met derde lid vast te stellen loon voor de arbeid in het lichaam" and connected companies, in the calendar year, "niet hoger is dan € 5.000" — a threshold the Belastingdienst says counts across everything you do: "De grens van € 5.000 geldt voor het totaal van uw werkzaamheden voor alle vennootschappen of coöperaties waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt."

This box is on a clock the statute itself sets. Lid 7: "Het in het eerste lid vermelde bedrag wordt bij het begin van het kalenderjaar bij ministeriële regeling vervangen door een ander" — the amount is replaced at the start of each calendar year. Replaced does not mean changed: it stood at € 56.000 for 2024 and again for 2025. So if you are reading this in a later year, the figure above is unverified by construction. Recheck it before using it; only the structure keeps without checking.

Checked 2 August 2026 against Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 art. 12a and the Belastingdienst's own guidance. This box states which regime attaches to which legal form and what the statute says. It does not tell you what your figure should be, whether a lower one is defensible in your circumstances, what counts as the most comparable employment for your work, or anything about dividends. Those belong to a belastingadviseur or accountant, and a formal advance-consultation route with the Belastingdienst exists for exactly this question. Nothing here is a reason to choose one legal form over another; that decision is not this book's.

Monday

  1. List the jobs, not the hours. Write down every distinct role you perform in the business and roughly what share of your week each takes. Delivery, selling, support, bookkeeping, whatever else. The list itself is usually the surprise.
  2. Price each one against a real advertisement. Not a feeling, not a sense of your worth — an actual current rate for that work, at the hours you genuinely spend. Total it. That is your replacement cost, and it is the number the rest of this depends on.
  3. Put it in last year's accounts as a cost and recompute. One line, whether or not it was ever paid. Then read the profit figure again and decide which of the three answers in Move 3 you are looking at.
  4. If it is the second answer, write the date. The condition and the month by which the business must cover the wage. Put it where you will see it — this is the sentence that stops a financed period from becoming a permanent one by default.
  5. Separate what you are owed from what you drew. Two columns for the last twelve months. If they diverge widely, your household has been absorbing the business's cash volatility, and you should know by how much before deciding whether to keep doing it.
  6. If you have a bv, check your position against the box — then ask someone. Take the fork, the article and the figures to a belastingadviseur or accountant. This is a question with a defensible answer and an expensive wrong one, and it is not a question to settle from a book.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

Two would do, and they cut opposite ways. One: an owner who priced their labour honestly, found the business could not cover it, and shut down or sold — while a peer who never did the calculation carried on through the same lean years and came out with a business worth having. Two: an owner who insisted on a full market wage early and drained the capital that would have financed the growth. Both would show that the discipline recommended here has a cost, and that the cost falls hardest on businesses whose returns arrive slowly. The chapter does not price either, and the honest position is that counting the cost is cheap while acting on it is not.
