Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 12

Reading Your Own Business

Lea's customers have paid €2,700. Her bank holds €2,350. Her simplified accounts also show €2,350 of profit, but the work has not covered the allowance she set for her own time. To decide what to do next, she needs to explain each number.

The decision What the latest period tells you about the business, which figures you can trace to records, and what needs attention before you accept more work or take money out.

Bring the jobs into one period

Lea is the fictional developer from the previous two chapters, testing a service that automates weekly reports for small consultancies. A, B and C each paid €900. Following up revealed an access problem for B, which took another hour to correct. The customer records now contain both the delivery costs and what Lea knows about the results. Here she brings those records together with the expenses and money movements that belong to the business.

If you are starting here, choose a recently completed month and get its bookkeeping reports, invoices and bank transactions. The aim is to explain the figures you already have and find what is missing. Ask your bookkeeper to work through the report with you if its headings or treatment of a transaction are unclear.

State the boundaries before adding up

All figures below are hypothetical. To keep the example readable, place Lea's three jobs and B's follow-up correction within one review period. The period starts before the work and ends after that correction. It need not be a calendar month. She will use monthly periods once there is enough activity to compare them sensibly.

For this illustration Lea operates as a sole trader, has no employees and pays no wage to herself. The calculation excludes VAT and taxes throughout, including the model bank balance; it is a simplified teaching model, not a complete set of statutory or tax accounts. Assume no equipment assets, depreciation, loans, inventory or unfinished work. Your actual bank and bookkeeping must include the items that apply to you.

At the opening date, the business holds €500 that Lea previously contributed, with no debts or other balances. During the period she earns and collects all €2,700 for the completed jobs. Each job has €50 of direct purchases, all paid: €150 altogether. Now add two business expenses outside the customer rows: €100 of shared software services used and paid for during the period, and a €100 bookkeeping bill for work already received but payable next period. Lea also transfers €600 to her personal account as drawings.

Before reading further, sort those items. Which belong to the period's earnings and costs? Which change the bank? Which leave something owed at the closing date? In particular, decide what to do with the unpaid bill and the €600 transfer. They are where a bank-only reading would give you the wrong explanation.

Keep the source beside the total

Lea can trace revenue to the three completed jobs and invoices, receipts to the bank, direct costs to purchase receipts, and the bookkeeping expense to its bill. Her own transfer has a bank entry and a description. A number without a traceable source is a question to resolve, even if the spreadsheet happens to balance.

Do not copy every customer detail into another document. The customer record explains the work; the bookkeeping records its financial treatment. The review below brings selected totals together and points back to both. A correction to B's hours should remain visible in B's record, while the new total flows into Lea's review.

Read three views of the same business

A profit-and-loss account reports revenue and expenses over a period. A balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and the owner's interest at a date. A cash-flow statement explains cash movements over a period. These are three useful views, not the whole of financial reporting: notes and changes in equity also matter. The SEC's introductory guide, linked in Reading, explains these distinctions. Lea's small example lets you follow the same transactions through each view.

The bookkeeping bill belongs to the period because the work has been received, despite payment being later. The €600 drawings do not appear as an expense in this sole-trader model: Lea has moved money to herself rather than incurred another cost of serving a customer. Her initial €500 contribution is not sales revenue either.

Those distinctions explain why neither the amount Lea withdraws nor the amount customers pay can substitute for reading the accounts. In a different legal form, owner salary and distributions may be treated differently. Use the classifications in your own accounts and ask about unfamiliar entries rather than forcing them into this example.

Where did the cash go?

Start with the opening €500. Add customer receipts of €2,700, subtract €150 of direct purchases, €100 for software and €600 of drawings. The closing bank balance is €2,350. The unpaid bookkeeping bill has no cash movement yet.

There is a second route to that answer. The model's €2,350 profit includes a €100 expense not yet paid, so cash generated by these operations is €2,450. Subtract the €600 drawings: cash increased by €1,850. Add the opening €500 and you reach €2,350 again. This is a simple cash reconciliation; a full cash-flow statement also separates operating, investing and financing movements where they occur.

Profit and the closing bank balance happen to be equal here. That is a coincidence of the opening balance, the unpaid bill and the drawings. If Lea had drawn €700 instead, closing cash would be €2,250 while profit would still be €2,350. Check that change yourself before treating a matching pair of figures as evidence that you understand them.

What remains at the closing date?

The simplified balance sheet contains one asset, cash of €2,350, and one liability, the €100 bookkeeping bill. The remaining €2,250 is the owner's equity in this model. The balance is €2,350 of assets = €100 of liabilities + €2,250 of equity.

You can explain the equity independently: €500 opening capital, plus €2,350 profit, less €600 drawings, gives €2,250. It is not another bank account or an extra €2,250 available to spend. The business has the assets already listed, with claims against them.

All customer invoices are paid in this example, so nothing is owed by customers at the closing date. If an invoice were unpaid, a customer balance would remain and the bank would differ. A bank statement alone would not show that claim or the unpaid supplier bill. For your own review, use the balance sheet and supporting lists of amounts owed to and by the business. Do not infer that an absent cash movement means no obligation exists.

Read what the accounts leave out of the decision

Put the owner's work beside the profit

Lea's €2,350 model profit looks very different from the narrow remainder calculated in chapter ten. The difference has an explanation. Those earlier calculations allowed €50 for every hour of her labour. In this sole-trader model, that allowance is not an actual wage expense in the accounts.

The three jobs now contain ten sales hours and 38 delivery hours: eight for A, twelve for B after the correction, and eighteen for C. Keep the six hours of unsuccessful selling from chapter ten's extended example. Add two hours of general administration, including this review. For the illustration, these 56 hours include all Lea's work in the period; if further follow-up takes time, it must be added.

At €50 an hour, the labour allowance is €2,800. Put that beside the €2,350 profit and the economic remainder is −€450. This is a separate test of whether the work covers the value assigned to Lea's time. It is not a claim that her bank lost €450 or that she should enter an invented wage into her tax return.

Do not subtract the €600 drawings again from that test. It is a transfer of money already accounted for, not another six hundred euros of work. Also avoid counting the six unsuccessful sales hours in every customer row. They belong once in the period total. If someone has actually been paid for work, distinguish that expense from any additional owner-time allowance.

Lea can now state the problem precisely: her early jobs generated cash, but at the current effort and shared costs they did not cover her chosen labour allowance. She still needs to find out whether clearer scope and better handover reduce that effort. One experimental period does not establish a sustainable income, even when the accounts show a profit.

Choose the next review around a decision

For the coming week, Lea puts three questions beside the accounts: what payments fall due, what work she has actually agreed to do, and how much time that work needs. These are a proposed starting point for this consultancy, not three universal indicators established by a company case.

The bookkeeping bill is a known €100 payment next period. Before deciding what else she can draw, she needs the other upcoming costs and, in a real business, the tax amounts excluded from this model. She cannot treat the simplified €2,350 balance as a spending allowance. Chapter thirteen develops the timing question.

For work ahead, separate accepted jobs from unanswered proposals. Put the delivery dates and hours beside the amount agreed, with cancellation or access uncertainties noted. An accepted order is useful evidence, but it is not money already received. For capacity, include corrections and follow-up alongside new work: B has shown why the delivery tick may need reopening.

Lea chooses a short weekly check while the next jobs are in progress, then another full review at the period end. If a payment cannot be met or a promised date is already impossible, she acts on that fact immediately. She does not need six weeks of data before recognising a specific shortfall. To judge a trend later, she will compare periods of similar length and explain changes in workload, prices and exceptional costs.

Ask for the report you need

Your accountant or bookkeeper may already provide management reports or help you set them up. Ask what period they cover, which entries are provisional and how to trace a line back to its source. Annual accounts, current bookkeeping and public filings serve different purposes; receiving one does not tell you whether another is available.

For a Dutch small company, KVK describes the filing as an abbreviated balance sheet with notes. That public document will not contain Lea's customer-level effort or outcome notes. Filing requirements depend on the business; the dated Dutch section below retains the legal detail. The practical task here is to connect your current reports to the decisions you need to make, whether or not your business files publicly.

Monday

Use the business-review worksheet beside your existing customer record and bookkeeping. It includes Lea's example figures and blank columns for yours and their sources. It contains no automatic formulas.

  1. Choose one period and trace the material lines. Write its dates, then locate the revenue, expenses, opening and closing cash, and amounts still owed. Separate owner transfers from business expenses. If something is missing or its treatment is unclear, record the question and the person or document that can resolve it.
  2. Explain the balances in plain words. Reconcile opening cash plus receipts minus payments to closing cash. Read the closing assets, liabilities and equity from your bookkeeping. Keep a separate calculation for any unpaid owner labour you want the business to cover; do not add it to the accounts as if it were a real payment.
  3. Choose the next action. Name one conclusion the records support, one uncertainty that could change it, and when you will review again. That may mean correcting an entry, collecting a missing bill, reducing delivery effort or revisiting an offer. Put upcoming payments and promised work beside the conclusion before committing more money.

You have completed the exercise when you can explain where the period's money went and why the next action follows. A report whose unexplained difference has been named is a better starting point than a balanced-looking spreadsheet with a guessed adjustment.

What the example leaves open

Lea's transactions are deliberately sparse. Real accounts can include prepayments, assets, depreciation, taxes, stock, borrowing and work spanning periods. The example is not evidence that this review schedule improves survival, and no company case establishes that claim here. Use it to learn the questions and relationships; resolve the treatment of your own entries from their records and the accounting basis your business uses.

On Dutch ground · which class you are in, and what it means

Book 2, Title 9 of the Burgerlijk Wetboek sets the size classes. You fall into a class by meeting two of the three tests on two consecutive balance-sheet dates.

First, whether you file at all. That turns on legal form, not size: "De rechtsvorm van uw bedrijf bepaalt of u een jaarrekening moet deponeren." A "besloten vennootschap (bv)" and a "naamloze vennootschap (nv)" must; an eenmanszaak and an ordinary vof are among the forms that need not, and the guidance links a full list of them. From financial year 2025 those who do file must do so via Standard Business Reporting.

Micro (art. 2:395a): assets not more than €450.000, turnover not more than €900.000, and "het gemiddeld aantal werknemers over het boekjaar bedraagt minder dan 10". Those are the operative figures; the article body still reads €350.000 and €700.000, with the consolidated text carrying the uprating as an editorial note — "per 13 maart 2024: € 450.000" and "per 13 maart 2024: € 900.000".

Small (art. 2:396): assets "niet meer dan € 7.500.000", turnover "niet meer dan € 15.000.000", fewer than 50 employees. Medium (art. 2:397): "niet meer dan € 25.000.000", "niet meer dan € 50.000.000", fewer than 250.

The publication limit for a small company is art. 2:396 lid 8: "Artikel 394 is slechts van toepassing met betrekking tot een overeenkomstig lid 3 beperkte balans en de toelichting." Article 394 is the publication duty, and lid 1 of it requires "openbaarmaking van de jaarrekening binnen acht dagen na de vaststelling", by deposit at the handelsregister. In practice the guidance gives both limbs — "uiterlijk 8 dagen na vaststelling bij de KVK" and "binnen 12 maanden na afloop van het boekjaar" — and states that "U kunt geen uitstel krijgen voor het indienen van uw jaarrekening."
