Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 12

Reading Your Own Business

The accounts you are legally required to publish are, for a business at your scale, the ones that tell you least about whether it works. That is not a defect in the law. It is a fact about who the law was written for, and it means the instrument you need does not arrive by post.

The decision Which three numbers you will look at every week, and what you will stop expecting the annual accounts to tell you.

Move 1 The situation

You have customers who paid, used what they bought, and in some measurable number of cases got what they came for. Now the question is whether the money works — and answering it means reading the business rather than feeling it.

There are two ways to get this wrong and the second is more dangerous than the first. The obvious failure is to read nothing: to run on the bank balance and a general sense of whether things are busy. The subtler failure is to read the annual accounts, understand them, and believe that you have therefore read the business.

The annual accounts are a real document, prepared by a professional, containing true numbers. They are also late, aggregated across twelve months in which several different things were happening, and — this is the part that surprises people — assembled to satisfy a reader who is not you. Once a year, some months after the year has ended, you receive a careful description of a period you can no longer do anything about.

None of that is an argument against having them. It is an argument for knowing what they are for, which turns out to be knowable exactly, because a statute says so.

Move 2 What actually happened

This chapter is the first in the book with no company in it. The case is a filing regime, because the useful evidence here is not what one business did but what the law obliges every business of a given size to publish — and, more interestingly, what it obliges them not to.

Dutch company law sorts businesses by size, and the boundaries are drawn on three tests: assets, turnover and average headcount. Meet two of the three, in two consecutive years, and you fall into a class. The smallest class is micro; above it small, then medium, then everything else. The reader of this book is almost certainly in one of the first two, and will remain there for a long time. The exact figures are in the box below.

What matters is what each class must make public. For a company in the small class, the statute limits the publication duty to a restricted balance sheet and the notes to it. That is the entire public filing. There is no profit-and-loss account in it. Nobody reading your filed accounts can see your revenue, your costs, your margins, or whether the year was good.

And the filing arrives when it arrives. The obligation is to deposit the accounts at the trade register within eight days of their adoption — and adoption itself happens after the year has closed and the books have been made up. By the time the document is public, it describes a business that has moved on.

One distinction has to be nailed down before it does damage, because getting it wrong would be expensive. The statute governs publication, not preparation. A small company still prepares a profit-and-loss account, and still files one with the tax authority. What it is excused from is putting that account on the public record. If you take nothing else from this chapter, do not conclude that you need not produce one.

Move 3 The reasoning

Three statements, three questions

Financial statements are less mysterious than their presentation suggests. There are three, and each answers exactly one question. Learning which question belongs to which statement is most of the literacy, and it does take about an afternoon.

  • The profit and loss account answers: over a period, did we make money, and where did it go? It covers a stretch of time — a month, a quarter, a year — and it ends in a single number that is either positive or not.
  • The balance sheet answers: at one instant, what do we own and what do we owe? It is a photograph, not a film. It has no period; it has a date.
  • The cash flow statement answers: over a period, where did the money actually move? Not what was earned or incurred, but what left and entered the bank.

Now set the public filing against that list. What a small company publishes is the middle one, restricted, dated once a year, some months after the fact. The statement that says whether you made money is absent. The statement that says where the money went is absent. You are publishing a photograph of one moment and nothing about the year that produced it.

Why that is not a scandal

The temptation is to read this as the law failing the small owner. It is not. Ask who the published accounts are for, and the design becomes obvious: they are for creditors. A supplier deciding whether to invoice you on thirty days wants to know what you own and what you already owe. That is a balance sheet question, and it is the only question they have standing to ask.

Your margins are none of their business, and — worth saying plainly — none of your competitors' either. The exemption that deprives you of a useful public document also protects you from having your economics read by anyone who cares to look. The regime is doing its job competently. It is simply not doing yours, and it was never going to.

Which leaves the actual conclusion of this chapter, and it is unwelcome: the instrument you need to run the business does not arrive by post, is not produced by your accountant as a by-product of compliance, and will not exist unless you build it.

The three numbers worth watching weekly

Weekly, because monthly is too slow to change anything and daily is noise. Three, because a number you look at every week has to be one you can hold in your head. What follows is a recommendation rather than a finding, and the chapter says so again in the boundaries.

One: cash in the bank, and the change since last week. Two figures, thirty seconds, no interpretation. Not a forecast — the actual balance and the actual movement. Why the movement diverges from profit is the next chapter, and it diverges more than almost anyone expects; this chapter asks only that you be looking at it before that chapter explains it.

Two: committed revenue for the coming weeks. Money that is signed, scheduled or contracted — not proposed, not likely, not verbally agreed. This is chapter three's distinction, applied to your own forecast rather than to a customer's enthusiasm. If the number falls two weeks running, that is the earliest honest warning the business is capable of producing.

Three: the operating quantity that turns into money later. Hours booked for a consultancy; units made or shipped for a product; installations scheduled, seats filled, jobs on the board. It is the physical fact that money is downstream of, and it moves before the money does. Which one it is depends on the business, and choosing it is the only part of this that requires thought.

The reason for exactly these three is that they sit at different distances from the present. Cash is what has already happened. Committed revenue is what is going to happen. The operating quantity is what is happening now and will show up in the other two later. Read together, they tell you which direction you are moving before the accounts can.

Boundaries

Publication is not preparation. Repeated because it is the error with real consequences. The exemption is from publishing a profit-and-loss account, not from producing one.

The regime described here is Dutch. Other jurisdictions have their own size tests and their own exemptions, and this book has checked none of them. Do not assume the thresholds or the treatment travel.

The three weekly numbers are a prescription with no case behind it. No business in this book is shown adopting them and doing better. They are reasoning from the chapters around them, and that is a thinner thing than an argument supported by a case — the same class of debt as chapter five's alternative resignation standard, and recorded in the same place.

Reading the numbers is not the same as understanding the business. These three will tell you the direction and the speed. They will not tell you which customer is about to leave, which is why the previous chapter exists and why its columns belong beside these.

On Dutch ground · which class you are in, and what it means

Book 2, Title 9 of the Burgerlijk Wetboek sets the size classes. You fall into a class by meeting two of the three tests on two consecutive balance-sheet dates.

Micro (art. 2:395a): assets "niet meer dan € 350.000", turnover "niet meer dan € 700.000", and "het gemiddeld aantal werknemers over het boekjaar bedraagt minder dan 10". The consolidated text carries an editorial note raising the first two to €450.000 and €900.000 from 13 March 2024.

Small (art. 2:396): assets "niet meer dan € 7.500.000", turnover "niet meer dan € 15.000.000", fewer than 50 employees. Medium (art. 2:397): "niet meer dan € 25.000.000", "niet meer dan € 50.000.000", fewer than 250.

The publication limit for a small company is art. 2:396 lid 8: "Artikel 394 is slechts van toepassing met betrekking tot een overeenkomstig lid 3 beperkte balans en de toelichting." Article 394 is the publication duty, and lid 1 of it requires "openbaarmaking van de jaarrekening binnen acht dagen na de vaststelling", by deposit at the handelsregister.

Checked 2 August 2026 against the consolidated text of Burgerlijk Wetboek Boek 2 on wetten.overheid.nl. This box states which class publishes what; it does not tell you which class you are in, what your filing deadline is in a given year, or what your accountant should prepare. Those depend on facts about your company — ask them.

Monday

  1. Download your own filed accounts. They are public. Read what is in them, and make a list of the questions you had that they do not answer. That list is the specification for everything below.
  2. Write the three statements out by hand, once. One page each, last completed month, from your own bank and invoices rather than from software. You will not do this again; doing it once is what makes the software legible afterwards.
  3. Choose your operating quantity. The physical thing that turns into money later — hours, units, jobs, seats. Write down why it leads rather than lags, and how you will count it without a project.
  4. Put the three numbers in one place and set a recurring half hour. Same day each week. Cash and its change, committed revenue, the operating quantity. A spreadsheet is sufficient and a dashboard is procrastination.
  5. Record them for six weeks before drawing any conclusion. One reading is a number; six is a direction. Deciding anything from week one is how people talk themselves out of businesses that were fine.

What this chapter owes

Two debts, and they are of different kinds. The first is the ordinary one: no counter-case, and here it would be an owner who watched a small set of weekly numbers faithfully and was misled by them — steering by three indicators while the thing that killed the business moved somewhere they were not looking. That must be common. It is unpublished for the usual reason.

The second is worse and is stated in the boundaries as well: the three weekly numbers are reasoning, not evidence. Nothing in this book shows a business adopting them and faring better than one that did not. The method page records the difference between a prescription lacking a counter-case and a prescription lacking any case at all. This is the latter.
