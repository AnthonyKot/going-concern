Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 12

Reading Your Own Business and Paying Yourself

Lea's customers have paid €2,700. Her bank holds €2,350. Her accounts show €2,350 of profit. And when she puts her own hours beside that profit, the period lost €450. All four numbers are correct. None of them alone tells her whether to keep going or how much she can take home. What does each one answer, and what do they say together?

The decision What the latest period tells you about the business, what your own work cost, and how much you propose to pay yourself next.

One period, three views

Lea is invented, continued from chapter ten. She has finished three report jobs at €900 each, including B's one-hour access correction, and now reads them as a business. The period runs about two months, from her first approach to B's correction, which is why it holds more than one month of her 32 evening hours. For the example she trades in her own name, has no employees and pays herself no wage; VAT and tax are left out, and so are equipment, loans and stock. Your own accounts must include whatever applies to you.

At the start of the period the business holds €500 she put in. During it she collects all €2,700, pays €150 for the three jobs' tools and €100 for shared software, receives a €100 bookkeeping bill she will pay next period (together the €100 a month of fixed cost from chapter one), and transfers €600 to her own account. Before reading on, decide where the unpaid bill and the €600 belong. Those are the two places a bank-only reading goes wrong.

What the period earned is the profit: €2,700 − €150 − €100 − €100 = €2,350. An expense is something used up to earn this period's revenue, so the bookkeeping bill counts: the work was done for this period, although it is not yet paid. The €600 is different. Nothing was used up to earn anything; Lea moved money the business already had into her own account. In a sole trader's accounts that is a drawing. It lowers the bank balance and what the business owes her, not the profit. Count it as an expense and the period looks €600 worse than the work was, and once her hours are priced in the next section, the same €600 would be counted against the business twice. Her opening €500 is not revenue either; it was never earned.

Where the cash went: €500 at the start, plus €2,700 received, less €150, €100 and the €600 drawing, leaves €2,350 in the bank. That it matches the profit is a coincidence you can take apart. Cash includes the opening €500, which profit does not (+€500); cash has paid the €600 drawing, which profit ignores (−€600); and profit has charged the €100 bill, which cash has not yet paid (+€100 to cash). +€500 − €600 + €100 = €0, so the two happen to land on the same figure. Had she drawn €700, cash would be €2,250 and profit still €2,350. A matching pair is not evidence that you understand either number.

What remains at the closing date: one asset, €2,350 of cash; one liability, the €100 bill; and €2,250 that is hers, which is €500 put in, plus €2,350 profit, less €600 drawn. That €2,250 is not another bank account. A bank statement alone would not show the unpaid bill, or an unpaid invoice from a customer; an absent payment does not mean an absent obligation.

What her time cost

None of those three views contains Lea's work, because a sole trader's own hours are not an expense in the accounts. So she counts them separately, once each: ten hours winning the three jobs, 38 delivering them (eight for A, twelve for B after the correction, eighteen for C), six on approaches that sold nothing, and two on administration, including this review. Fifty-six hours. At the €50 an hour she would pay someone competent, that is €2,800. Beside the €2,350 profit, the period is €450 short. Chapter ten reached −€150 for the same work; the extra €300 is the €200 of software and bookkeeping and the two administration hours, which that chapter left for this one. Its €2,550 was receipts less tools only; this chapter's €2,350 in the bank adds the €500 she started with and takes off the software and her €600.

That does not mean her bank lost €450, and it is not a wage she owes herself. It is a separate test: did the work cover what her time is worth? And it does not subtract the €600 drawing again, which was money moving, not work. The period covered about €41.96 an hour, €2,350 over 56. At €50 an hour the gap is nine hours: the same work in 47 hours would have broken even. Those nine hours cannot be removed from the past, and nothing yet shows they can be removed from the next jobs.

Read together, the four numbers from the opening give a plain answer. The business earned money (€2,350 profit). It holds cash, but whether that is enough for what comes next is a question about dates, not about this period (€2,350 in the bank, a €100 bill owed). And it has not yet paid for her time (€450 short). So: continue, change one thing, and put a limit on how long she accepts the shortfall.

Lea has three owner figures, and keeps them apart: what her work cost (€2,800, an estimate for judging the business), what the accounts pay her (nothing, as a sole trader), and what reached her household (€600, on a date). A business can cover its owner's time over a period and still lack the cash on payday; it can also pay the owner from savings while the work earns too little.

She now knows where the nine hours came from: B's unclear preparation instructions and C's second source system, agreed in a call without a new quote. That gives her a decision, not a verdict. She keeps the €900 offer, sends clearer instructions before each job, quotes any second source separately, and reviews the next three jobs against the ten hours she estimated. She is accepting a period below her own rate while she tests one change, and she writes that down as a choice, with its limit: the next three single-file jobs, or three months, whichever comes first. Chapter ten set the review; this adds its limit. If those jobs still run well over ten hours, she makes the choice chapter ten listed: change the offer, allow more time, reprice, or stop the test. Chapter sixteen asks the personal half of that question: how long, and for what.

The next payment, proposed not paid

Lea proposes a further €300 to herself on day 14 of the next period, on top of the €600 already drawn. She carries forward €2,350 in cash and the unpaid €100 bill. Whether the €300 fits depends on what else falls due before day 14 and what new work she accepts, which the accounts cannot tell her. So it stays marked proposed, with its date, until chapter thirteen's dated forecast tests it. Postponing it is not free either: something else then has to cover the household.

Monday · one period, your hours beside the profit

Do this one thing. For your last completed month, write four numbers on one page: revenue, profit, closing bank balance, and your own hours for the period multiplied by what you would pay someone competent for them. Under the last two, one line each on why they differ from the profit. You now hold the period's result and what your work cost beside it.

No profit figure yet? Ask your bookkeeper or accountant for the month's profit-and-loss report and the list of unpaid bills and invoices, with a date for the reply. Sending that request, with your hours estimated beside it, counts as finishing. If you keep your own books, the invoices and bank statement are enough to start.

If you have another hour:

  1. Reconcile it. Use the business-review worksheet (Lea's figures and blank fields, no formulas): opening cash to closing cash, and what is owed to and by the business at the closing date.
  2. Propose your next payment with a date and amount, marked proposed, for chapter thirteen's forecast.
  3. If the period fell short of your rate, name the one change you will test, and the limit on how long you will accept the shortfall while testing it.
What this chapter rests on, and what it leaves out

Lea's transactions are invented and deliberately sparse. Real accounts include prepayments, assets, depreciation, taxes, stock, borrowing and work that spans periods; a full cash-flow statement also separates operating, investing and financing movements. The three views follow the SEC's introductory guide. The €50 rate is illustrative, not a sourced replacement cost: for your own, gather dated evidence for comparable work (vacancies, quotations), match skill, hours and responsibility, and use a range where evidence is weak. If your accounts already contain owner remuneration for the work, replace that component with the estimate in a management copy rather than subtracting both.

Ask your bookkeeper what period each report covers, which entries are provisional, and how to trace a line to its source. Annual accounts, current bookkeeping and public filings serve different purposes; for a Dutch small company, KVK describes the public filing as an abbreviated balance sheet with notes, which will not contain anything like Lea's hours.

No company case here shows that a regular review of this kind improves survival. For a trade that buys stock before selling, the optional stock-and-cash example applies the same distinctions to a market stall.

On Dutch ground · which class you are in, and what it means

Book 2, Title 9 of the Burgerlijk Wetboek sets the size classes. You fall into a class by meeting two of the three tests on two consecutive balance-sheet dates.

First, whether you file at all. That turns on legal form, not size: "De rechtsvorm van uw bedrijf bepaalt of u een jaarrekening moet deponeren." A "besloten vennootschap (bv)" and a "naamloze vennootschap (nv)" must; an eenmanszaak and an ordinary vof are among the forms that need not, and the guidance links a full list of them. From financial year 2025 those who do file must do so via Standard Business Reporting.

Micro (art. 2:395a): assets not more than €450.000, turnover not more than €900.000, and "het gemiddeld aantal werknemers over het boekjaar bedraagt minder dan 10". Those are the operative figures; the article body still reads €350.000 and €700.000, with the consolidated text carrying the uprating as an editorial note — "per 13 maart 2024: € 450.000" and "per 13 maart 2024: € 900.000".

Small (art. 2:396): assets "niet meer dan € 7.500.000", turnover "niet meer dan € 15.000.000", fewer than 50 employees. Medium (art. 2:397): "niet meer dan € 25.000.000", "niet meer dan € 50.000.000", fewer than 250.

The publication limit for a small company is art. 2:396 lid 8: "Artikel 394 is slechts van toepassing met betrekking tot een overeenkomstig lid 3 beperkte balans en de toelichting." Article 394 is the publication duty, and lid 1 of it requires "openbaarmaking van de jaarrekening binnen acht dagen na de vaststelling", by deposit at the handelsregister. In practice the guidance gives both limbs — "uiterlijk 8 dagen na vaststelling bij de KVK" and "binnen 12 maanden na afloop van het boekjaar" — and states that "U kunt geen uitstel krijgen voor het indienen van uw jaarrekening."

Checked 2 August 2026 against the consolidated text of Burgerlijk Wetboek Boek 2 on wetten.overheid.nl. This box states which class publishes what; it does not tell you which class you are in, what your filing deadline is in a given year, or what your accountant should prepare. Those depend on facts about your company — ask them.

On Dutch ground · what the state says about paying yourself

Which of the two regimes applies to you turns on legal form — and, for the second, on working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest. Read the fork first; the figures below are meaningless attached to the wrong side of it.

Trading in your own name — an eenmanszaak — there is no salary and no minimum. Money you take out is a privéonttrekking: "Privéonttrekkingen zijn uitgaven die u uit het ondernemingsvermogen hebt betaald voor uzelf", and the consequence is the one that matters here — "omdat het geen zakelijke kosten zijn, zijn privéonttrekkingen niet aftrekbaar van uw ondernemingswinst". Not deductible: your own labour does not appear as a cost in the profit the business is taxed on. Note also that being an entrepreneur is not automatically being one for income tax — "Lang niet iedere ondernemer is ondernemer voor de inkomstenbelasting."

This box covers that form and the one below, and no others: a vof or maatschap is a different arrangement, with partner labour allowances and profit shares this chapter carries no evidence about.

Working for a company in which you hold a substantial interest, the gebruikelijkloonregeling applies: "Bent u werknemer in een vennootschap of coöperatie waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt? Dan geldt voor u de gebruikelijkloonregeling." Its principle, in the tax authority's words, is that "een aanmerkelijkbelanghouder wordt geacht een loon te krijgen dat normaal is voor het niveau en de duur van zijn arbeid".

Article 12a of the Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 sets it out. "Ten aanzien van de werknemer die arbeid verricht ten behoeve van een lichaam waarin hij of zijn partner een aanmerkelijk belang heeft", the salary "wordt het in het kalenderjaar van dat lichaam genoten loon ten minste gesteld op het hoogste van de volgende bedragen" — the highest of three: "het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking"; "het hoogste loon van de werknemers die in dienst zijn van het lichaam" or a connected company; and "c. € 58.000." The tax authority states the same figure and its recent history: "ten minste € 58.000 in 2026 (in 2025 en 2024 was dat € 56.000)".

There are routes to a lower figure and they run on evidence, not on assertion. Lid 2 applies where "de inhoudingsplichtige aannemelijk maakt dat het hoogste bedrag, bedoeld in het eerste lid, hoger is dan het loon uit de meest vergelijkbare dienstbetrekking". The comparison must be with employment "waarbij een aanmerkelijk belang geen rol speelt". And where the salary the calculation itself arrives at is small, the calculation stops forcing it upwards — lid 4: "Het eerste en tweede lid zijn niet van toepassing indien het bij toepassing van het eerste tot en met derde lid vast te stellen loon voor de arbeid in het lichaam" and connected companies, in the calendar year, "niet hoger is dan € 5.000" — a threshold the Belastingdienst says counts across everything you do: "De grens van € 5.000 geldt voor het totaal van uw werkzaamheden voor alle vennootschappen of coöperaties waarin u een aanmerkelijk belang hebt."

This box is on a clock the statute itself sets. Lid 7: "Het in het eerste lid vermelde bedrag wordt bij het begin van het kalenderjaar bij ministeriële regeling vervangen door een ander" — the amount is replaced at the start of each calendar year. Replaced does not mean changed: it stood at € 56.000 for 2024 and again for 2025. So if you are reading this in a later year, the figure above is unverified by construction. Recheck it before using it; only the structure keeps without checking.

Checked 2 August 2026 against Wet op de loonbelasting 1964 art. 12a and the Belastingdienst's own guidance. This box states which regime attaches to which legal form and what the statute says. It does not tell you what your figure should be, whether a lower one is defensible in your circumstances, what counts as the most comparable employment for your work, or anything about dividends. Those belong to a belastingadviseur or accountant. Nothing here is a reason to choose one legal form over another; that decision is not this book's.
