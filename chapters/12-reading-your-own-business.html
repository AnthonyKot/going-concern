Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 12

Reading Your Own Business

There are three different financial documents and most owners have met only one of them. If you run through a company that files, the version you publish tells you least about whether the business works; if you run an eenmanszaak, you publish nothing at all. Either way the instrument you actually need does not arrive by post.

The decision Which three numbers you will look at every week, and what you will stop expecting the annual accounts to tell you.

Move 1 The situation

You have customers who paid, used what they bought, and in some measurable number of cases got what they came for. Now the question is whether the money works — and answering it means reading the business rather than feeling it.

Three documents get confused with each other, and separating them is most of this chapter:

  • The annual accounts — the full year-end statements prepared for the company: balance sheet, profit-and-loss account and notes.
  • The public filing — the reduced version deposited at the trade register, if your legal form has to deposit anything at all.
  • Management information — the numbers you use to make decisions while the year is still happening, which nobody obliges you to produce and nobody will produce for you.

The obvious failure is to read none of them and run on the bank balance and a sense of whether things feel busy. The subtler failure is to read the first two, understand them properly, and believe you have therefore read the business.

Understanding the annual accounts is necessary. They are a real document, often prepared with professional help, with numbers produced under defined accounting rules. What they are not is sufficient: they are late, aggregated across twelve months in which several different things were happening, and — in their published form especially — shaped to answer somebody else's question. Some months after the year has ended you receive a careful description of a period you can no longer do anything about.

Move 2 What actually happened

This chapter is the first in the book with no company in it. The case is a filing regime, because the useful evidence here is not what one business did but what the law obliges — and, more interestingly, whom it obliges nothing of at all.

Start with who is obliged to file anything, because the answer excludes a great many readers of this book. In the Netherlands it turns on legal form, not on size: "De rechtsvorm van uw bedrijf bepaalt of u een jaarrekening moet deponeren." A besloten vennootschap files. So does an nv, a co-operative, and a handful of other forms. An eenmanszaak does not appear on the list at all, and nor does an ordinary vof whose partners are Dutch. If you are a sole trader, you have no public filing, and the argument below reaches you by a different route: the document you are missing was never going to be produced for you in the first place.

For those who do file, Dutch company law then sorts them by size on three tests — assets, turnover and average headcount. Meet two of the three in two consecutive years and you fall into a class: micro, then small, then medium, then everything else. Most readers here are in the first two and will be for a long time. The figures are in the box.

What matters is what each class must make public. For a company in the small class, the statute limits the publication duty to a restricted balance sheet and the notes to it. That is the entire public filing. There is no profit-and-loss account in it. Nobody reading your filed accounts can see your revenue, your costs, your margins, or whether the year was good.

And it arrives late by design. The accounts must be deposited "uiterlijk 8 dagen na vaststelling bij de KVK" and in any event "binnen 12 maanden na afloop van het boekjaar" — with, the guidance adds, no extension available. So the outer limit is a document about a year that ended twelve months ago. By the time it is public it describes a business that has moved on, and it was never meant to do anything else.

One distinction has to be nailed down before it does damage. The exemption concerns public disclosure, not preparation. The company must still keep accounts and must still produce the balance-sheet and profit-and-loss information its tax return needs — though fiscal values can differ from the commercial ones, which is a separate trap. What it is excused from is putting any of that on the public record. And note that this cuts both ways: a sole trader with no filing duty at all still supplies balance-sheet and profit-and-loss information with an income-tax return. Nobody escapes producing the numbers. Some people merely escape publishing them.

Move 3 The reasoning

Three statements, three questions

Financial statements are less mysterious than their presentation suggests. There are three, and each has one primary question it exists to answer. Learning which question belongs to which statement is most of the literacy, and it does take about an afternoon.

  • The profit and loss account answers: over a period, did we make money, and where did it go? It covers a stretch of time — a month, a quarter, a year — and it ends in a single number that is either positive or not.
  • The balance sheet answers: at one instant, what do we own and what do we owe? It is a photograph, not a film. It has no period; it has a date.
  • The cash flow statement answers: over a period, where did the money actually move? Not what was earned or incurred, but what left and entered the bank.

Now set the public filing against that list. What a small company publishes is the middle one, restricted, dated once a year. The statement that says whether you made money is absent. The statement that says where the money went is absent. You publish a photograph of one moment and nothing about the year that produced it — and if you are a sole trader you publish neither, which changes who can see your position but not what you need to run it.

Why that is not a scandal

The temptation is to read this as the law failing the small owner. It is better read as a compromise. Creditors, counterparties and prospective investors need some view of what a company owns and owes before extending credit or capital. Against that, the smallest companies are spared both the administrative burden of full disclosure and the competitive exposure of publishing their economics. The reduced filing is where those two pressures settle.

Which is why the public filing is not a management report and was never shaped to be one. Note the limit of the point, though: shareholders, lenders and contractual counterparties may all quite properly receive your full figures privately. What the compromise withholds is an automatic right for the general public — and your competitors — to read them. The regime does that job competently. It is simply not doing yours, and it was never going to.

Which leaves the actual conclusion of this chapter, and it is unwelcome: the instrument you need to run the business does not arrive by post, is not produced by your accountant as a by-product of compliance, and will not exist unless you build it.

A starting set of three weekly views

The legal argument ends here. What follows is a proposed starting instrument, generated by reasoning from the chapters around it and not established by anything above. For many owner-operated businesses a useful weekly set contains one liquidity measure, one committed-demand measure, and one operating quantity that leads revenue. The cadence and the precise measures have to match the length of your operating cycle — a cash-tight retailer may need to look daily, a consultancy on annual contracts may learn almost nothing from a weekly commitment figure.

One: available cash, alongside the obligations falling due before your next review. Not the bank balance alone, which flatters — it contains VAT you are holding, payroll taxes, and customer deposits that were never yours. Why cash and profit diverge is the next chapter's whole subject; this chapter asks only that you be looking at the right figure before that chapter explains it.

Two: committed demand. Contracted work, confirmed orders, bookings or subscriptions expected inside a horizon you define. Signed, not proposed, not verbally agreed — chapter three's distinction turned on your own forecast. A sustained fall here is among the earlier warnings a business can produce, though a seasonal business can fall for a fortnight and be behaving exactly as expected, so read it against last year rather than last week.

Three: an operating quantity demonstrably connected to demand. Booked hours, fulfilled orders, occupied seats, completed installations. Deliberately not units made — production without orders builds inventory, consumes cash, and makes the dashboard look healthier as the position gets worse. Choosing this measure is the only part of the exercise that requires real thought, and it is where a template is least likely to fit you.

The reason for a set of three at different distances is that each covers the others' blind spot. Available cash is what has already happened. Committed demand is what is going to. The operating quantity is happening now and will surface in both later. Read together they suggest a direction sooner than the accounts can — which is a claim about timeliness, not about accuracy.

Boundaries

Publication is not preparation. Repeated because it is the error with real consequences. The exemption is from publishing a profit-and-loss account, not from producing one.

The regime described here is Dutch. Other jurisdictions have their own size tests and their own exemptions, and this book has checked none of them. Do not assume the thresholds or the treatment travel.

The evidence supports the negative claim, not the prescription. The filing regime establishes that public annual filings are not management information. It establishes nothing whatever about which three weekly views an owner should use: no business in this book is shown adopting them and faring better. That is the same class of debt as chapter five's alternative resignation standard, and recorded in the same place.

Reading the numbers is not the same as understanding the business. These three will tell you the direction and the speed. They will not tell you which customer is about to leave, which is why the previous chapter exists and why its columns belong beside these.

On Dutch ground · which class you are in, and what it means

Book 2, Title 9 of the Burgerlijk Wetboek sets the size classes. You fall into a class by meeting two of the three tests on two consecutive balance-sheet dates.

First, whether you file at all. That turns on legal form, not size: "De rechtsvorm van uw bedrijf bepaalt of u een jaarrekening moet deponeren." A "besloten vennootschap (bv)" and a "naamloze vennootschap (nv)" must; an eenmanszaak and an ordinary vof are among the forms that need not, and the guidance links a full list of them. From financial year 2025 those who do file must do so via Standard Business Reporting.

Micro (art. 2:395a): assets not more than €450.000, turnover not more than €900.000, and "het gemiddeld aantal werknemers over het boekjaar bedraagt minder dan 10". Those are the operative figures; the article body still reads €350.000 and €700.000, with the consolidated text carrying the uprating as an editorial note — "per 13 maart 2024: € 450.000" and "per 13 maart 2024: € 900.000".

Small (art. 2:396): assets "niet meer dan € 7.500.000", turnover "niet meer dan € 15.000.000", fewer than 50 employees. Medium (art. 2:397): "niet meer dan € 25.000.000", "niet meer dan € 50.000.000", fewer than 250.

The publication limit for a small company is art. 2:396 lid 8: "Artikel 394 is slechts van toepassing met betrekking tot een overeenkomstig lid 3 beperkte balans en de toelichting." Article 394 is the publication duty, and lid 1 of it requires "openbaarmaking van de jaarrekening binnen acht dagen na de vaststelling", by deposit at the handelsregister. In practice the guidance gives both limbs — "uiterlijk 8 dagen na vaststelling bij de KVK" and "binnen 12 maanden na afloop van het boekjaar" — and states that "U kunt geen uitstel krijgen voor het indienen van uw jaarrekening."

Checked 2 August 2026 against the consolidated text of Burgerlijk Wetboek Boek 2 on wetten.overheid.nl. This box states which class publishes what; it does not tell you which class you are in, what your filing deadline is in a given year, or what your accountant should prepare. Those depend on facts about your company — ask them.

Monday

  1. Get the most recent accounts that exist for your business. If your legal form files publicly, download the filing — it is public. If it does not, use the balance sheet and profit-and-loss account prepared for your tax return. Then list the questions you had that the document does not answer. That list is the specification for everything below.
  2. Trace five lines back to reality. Take last completed month's profit-and-loss account and month-end balance sheet from your bookkeeping, and follow five material lines back to the invoices, bank movements or entries behind them — with your bookkeeper if that is what it takes. Do not try to build a balance sheet from your bank statements: receivables, payables, VAT, inventory, depreciation and deferred revenue are not in there, and you will produce something confidently wrong.
  3. Reconcile opening cash to closing cash in plain words. Profit, then working-capital movement, then investment, then borrowing, then what you took out. If you cannot get from one figure to the other, the gap is the most valuable thing you will find this month.
  4. Choose your operating quantity. The physical thing that turns into money later — hours, units, jobs, seats. Write down why it leads rather than lags, and how you will count it without a project.
  5. Put the three numbers in one place and set a recurring half hour. Same day each week. Cash and its change, committed revenue, the operating quantity. A spreadsheet is sufficient and a dashboard is procrastination.
  6. Record six comparable weeks before reading a trend — and act at once on an absolute. One reading is a number; six is a direction, and deciding from week one is how people talk themselves out of businesses that were fine. But a single reading can still show that the cash for payroll or tax is not there, and that needs handling on the day rather than after the sixth observation.

What this chapter owes

Two debts, and they are of different kinds. The first is the ordinary one: no counter-case, and here it would be an owner who watched a small set of weekly numbers faithfully and was misled by them — steering by three indicators while the thing that killed the business moved somewhere they were not looking. That must be common. It is unpublished for the usual reason.

The second is worse and is stated in the boundaries as well: the three weekly numbers are reasoning, not evidence. Nothing in this book shows a business adopting them and faring better than one that did not. The method page records the difference between a prescription lacking a counter-case and a prescription lacking any case at all. This is the latter.
