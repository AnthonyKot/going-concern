Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 16

Enough

Five chapters of arithmetic have told you what the business earns, what it costs, what a customer is worth and what your own labour should be charged at. None of them can tell you when to stop. That number is not in the accounts, because it is not a fact about the business — it is a fact about your life, and if you do not name it the business will name it for you.

The decision What your household actually needs, what the business must throw off to cover it, and the date on which you will decide both again.

Move 1 The situation

A business has no opinion about how much money you need. It will absorb capital, hours and attention up to whatever limit you set, and if you set none it will find the limit experimentally — usually at the point where something in your life breaks. That is not a moral failing of business. It is what an unbounded system does.

So the number has to come from outside. And there are two ways of not having it, which look nothing alike from inside and cost about the same.

  • Never naming it. The business grows, the drawings grow with it, the household expands to fit, and the question of whether this was worth doing has no answer because there is no standard to answer it against. More is always available, so more is always the plan.
  • Naming it once. Worse, because it feels finished. A number set in year three is defended for a decade against a life that has changed, prices that have moved and a market that did not agree to stand still. Flat looks like stability for as long as you do not check what it is flat against.

The first failure is the famous one and this book has already used most of the machinery for it: chapter fifteen put a cost on your labour, and once that cost is in the restatement, a business that cannot cover it stops being a lifestyle question and becomes a viability one. But note the direction of that test. It asks what the business must carry. It says nothing about what your life costs, and the two numbers are not related except by accident. A replacement for your labour might cost €70,000 in a year when your household needs €45,000, or €30,000 in a year when it needs €60,000. Both are ordinary. Neither is discoverable from the other.

Move 2 What actually happened

Pinboard is a bookmarking service that one person, Maciej Cegłowski, launched in July 2009 and still runs. Every year on the anniversary he published a table of statistics, and in 2016 he added a revenue row to it — deliberately, and with a stated reason: he was "no longer afraid of competitors" and wanted to encourage people considering their own "one- or zero-person business". The result is an unusually honest artefact for this genre: eleven consecutive years of first-party numbers, published while the outcome was still unknown, by someone with no investors to impress.

By 2016 the table read: revenue of $117K, $178K, $181K, $175K, $193K, $160K and $234K for 2010 through 2016, against funding of zero in every one of those years. Costs, he wrote, were "something like $17K/year to run, so you can make a good living at this artisanal SaaS stuff". Two years earlier he had put the same point without figures — the site "makes roughly the same amount of money (around $200K) every year", and "the number of active users stays roughly the same" — and drew the conclusion the whole of Part III has been circling: stability was not a symptom, it was the product. What a bookmarking service sells is the belief that it will still be there, which requires "actually earning enough money to do that". In five years of running it he had, he wrote, not received "a single email from an investor".

Read the flat line correctly. It is not an absence of growth; it is a business earning several times its running costs, every year, for one person, without external money — which is a specification most businesses in this book would take. The number was named, it was met, and it was published so that other people could see what the bar actually was.

Then the same table, four years later. Revenue for 2017 through 2020: $259K, $253K, $222K, $212K. The 2017 figure is the peak of the whole series. Active users, on the same tables, run 24K in 2016 and 29K in 2017, then 21K in 2019 and 19K in 2020. Funding is still zero — that row never changes, across all eleven years.

What happened in between is in his own account rather than inferred. The 2017 post records a year of travel for a political organising project, in which "all the travel meant I sometimes ignored support emails for weeks at a time", followed by what he called a "reverse sabbatical to try to stabilize the site". Set the two records side by side and that is the whole of it: a business that had reached enough, an owner who spent a year on something else — which is what enough is for — and a revenue line that had been flat for six years and then was not.

Two records, one sequence, and no arrow between them. This chapter does not claim the neglect caused the decline, and nothing in it depends on that being true. Attention was not the only thing that changed, the interval is years long, and the source supplies its own confounder: revenue "dropped a bit in 2015 (as I moved to an annual subscription system)" and rose again as the first renewals came due, so this line moves on pricing mechanics as well as on demand. What the figures establish is the sequence. What the chapter uses the sequence for is narrower and survives the missing arrow entirely: a sufficiency number can stay fixed while the business underneath it changes — gradually enough to be invisible in any single year, and with a flat line that means one thing on the way up and something else later while looking identical.

Move 3 The reasoning

Enough is four numbers, not one

The word hides an arithmetic problem behind a feeling. Separate it into three, in this order, because each one is answerable and the feeling is not:

  • The household floor, which you already have — and which is not chapter nine's price floor, a different quantity wearing the same word. Chapter five defined it before you resigned: the mortgage, the insurance, the food, the minimum the household needs, plus the benefits your employer used to pay for and you now pay yourself. The quantity has not changed. What changes here is its job. There it was a gate you crossed once — clear the floor for six months and give notice. Here it is a standing requirement the business has to meet every year, recounted from twelve months of statements rather than remembered from the year you left, because budgets are aspirations and statements are evidence.
  • What else already covers it. A partner's salary, rental income, a pension in payment, savings you have decided in advance to spend at a stated rate. Subtract it. Almost every reader of this chapter has some, and leaving it out is how a business gets asked to carry a household it was never the only support for — which sets the bar too high and makes a perfectly good business look like a failure.
  • The throw-off: what remains, grossed up. What this business must earn for that remainder to arrive in your account after tax, social charges and the cost of the business itself. This is where chapter fifteen's restatement is reused rather than repeated: you already know what the business must cover to break even honestly, and this is an additional, separate requirement on top of it.
  • The gap. The difference, with a sign. A positive gap is the only thing in this book that can honestly be called a return on ownership. A negative one is a deadline.

Four numbers rather than one, because the interesting failures live in the joins. A business that covers the labour charge but not the floor is telling you it is a viable business attached to the wrong life. A business that covers the floor but not the labour charge is telling you the opposite: your household is fine and the business is quietly being subsidised by you, which the previous chapter says is a legitimate position only while it has a date on it. Neither of those is visible if enough stays a single feeling about whether things are going all right.

Sufficiency is a run of periods, not a good month

The throw-off is not met when it is met once. Chapter five settled the form of this test before you resigned and it does not change now that you have: a number fixed before you see the result, a stated number of consecutive periods, a date by which it must have happened, and a consequence you wrote down while calm. There the run was six consecutive months of revenue clearing the floor. Here it is the same instrument pointed at a business you already own — pick the run, six months or four quarters, and pick it before you look at this year's figures rather than after.

Three things follow, and the third is the one people skip. A single month above the line proves nothing and neither does a single month below it, so a miss is not an instruction to stop. The number does not move while you are approaching it — a threshold rewritten in the month it is about to be tested is not a threshold, it is a mood. And "not enough evidence yet" is a permitted verdict, with its own date attached: two quarters into a four-quarter run, the honest answer is that the run is incomplete, not that the business has passed or failed. What is not permitted is letting that answer stand for a third year.

The number expires, and the date is the whole mechanism

An unrenewed sufficiency number is how a business dies quietly, and the case shows the shape: a line that is flat because it is held, and a line that is flat because it is falling slowly, are the same line. The only way to tell them apart is to have written down, in advance, what you expected it to be — and then to look.

So the number carries a review date, the same way chapter four's stopping rule carries one. Once a year, on a date chosen now: recount the floor, recount the throw-off, and compare the last three years of revenue and customers rather than the last one. Three years is the shortest window in which a slow decline is visible at all. The review is not a formality — it is permitted to conclude that the number should go up, that the business should grow after all, or that it is time to take the next chapter's question seriously.

Time is the second ledger

Money is not the only thing the business consumes, and a chapter about sufficiency that counts only money will be wrong for a predictable set of readers. Count hours against a stated ceiling the same way you count euros against a floor: what you are willing to spend, written down, with the same review date. But keep it a parallel ledger and not a substitute. A business that runs out of money stops; a business that runs out of its owner's patience usually has months of warning and several remedies, which is why this book puts the money first and says so.

Boundaries

This is not permission to stop selling. The most likely misreading of this chapter, by exactly the reader it is written for, is that a named number licenses the coasting that the previous fifteen chapters were arguing against. It does the opposite: a number with no review date is what licenses coasting, because nothing ever contradicts it. Enough is a decision that has to be won again every year, and losing it is allowed to mean growing.

Flat is cheap in some businesses and expensive in others. The case is one person, no staff, and near-zero marginal cost — a shape that can hold revenue steady while spending almost nothing to do it. A business with payroll, stock or premises has fixed costs that rise on their own, so its flat line requires continuous work just to stay level. Do not import the conclusion without checking which kind you own.

The floor is not a forecast. Twelve months of statements tells you what last year cost, not what a bad year costs. Children, illness, a mortgage rate reset and a broken car are not in the sample. Whether that gap belongs to a buffer, an insurance policy or a bigger number is a Part V question — chapter twenty-three onwards — and this chapter does no more than mark it.

One case, and it is the easy kind. Pinboard is a single business, run by one person with no dependants named in the record, in a category with unusually loyal users. It shows an enough number being reached, published and left undefended. It cannot show what the same discipline does to a household with two incomes, three children, or a partner whose own sufficiency number is different — and every reader of this chapter has one of those situations rather than this one.

Monday

  1. Count the floor from twelve months of statements. Download the year, total the outgoings, and write the annual figure down. Not a budget, not an estimate you feel good about — what the household actually spent.
  2. Add back what a salary used to hide. Pension payments your employer used to make, sick pay you no longer get, holiday you now fund yourself. Total those separately and add them to the floor, so the number is comparable with the salary you left.
  3. Subtract what already covers it. A partner's salary, rent received, a pension in payment, savings you have decided in advance to draw at a stated rate. One figure. What is left is what this business — not your household, this business — has to supply.
  4. Work out the throw-off. What the business must earn for that remainder to land in your account after tax and social charges, on top of covering its own costs and the labour charge from chapter fifteen. One figure. Write it next to last year's actual revenue.
  5. Finish the sentence before you look at this year's numbers. "This business must throw off €X in six consecutive months / four consecutive quarters, by date; if it has not, I will raise prices / cut the offer / take contract work / stop." Choose the run length first, then check the figures. Doing it the other way round is how a threshold becomes a description of what already happened.
  6. Put the five lines on one page and date it. Household floor · other dependable income · what this business must supply · the run required · what happens if it misses. That page is the artefact this chapter exists to produce, and it is the thing you compare against next year rather than your memory of how the year felt.
  7. Put the annual review in the calendar now, for the same week every year. At that review, look at three years of revenue and customer counts side by side, not one — a decline slow enough to matter is invisible year to year, which is the entire lesson of the case above. "Not enough evidence yet" is an allowed verdict once, with a date; twice is a decision you are avoiding.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

What is owed is an owner who did all of it properly — named a sufficiency number, held it deliberately, re-decided it every year on schedule — and lost the business anyway, because a deliberately static offer was overtaken by a market that moved faster than the annual review could see. That is the real risk in this chapter's advice, and the annual cadence recommended above is exactly the thing such a case would falsify. Pinboard is not it: the number was named and published, and then not re-decided for several years, which makes the later stretch the advice half-followed rather than the advice followed and failing. Note also which direction the missing evidence runs. Businesses that stayed deliberately small and survived write anniversary posts; businesses that stayed deliberately small and were overtaken stop writing, and the archive of quiet endings is not published anywhere.

Closing Part III · Does the money work?

Six chapters, and they were not a ladder. Parts I and II each climbed something — evidence the buyer would pay, then the cost of getting them to. Part III is a single question asked six times of the same business, and the answers do not stack so much as they correct each other:

the outcome arrived and the customer stayed → the statements are legible → cash is not profit → a customer has a value → your labour has a price → your life has a number

Each one falsifies a flattering reading of the one before it. Retention makes acquisition look less impressive. The statements make retention arithmetic. Cash flow makes the statements survivable or not. Customer value makes revenue growth conditional. The owner's wage makes profit provisional. And sufficiency makes all five of them serve something outside the business, which is the only reason to have run the arithmetic at all.

What Part III cannot do is decide. A business whose numbers work is not therefore worth your forties, and a business that misses its number by twenty per cent is not therefore over — chapter four has the only apparatus in this book that decides anything, and it works because it was written down in advance by someone calmer than you will be on the day. Everything in this part is input to that.

The part should leave you holding something, not agreeing with something: one dated page carrying the household floor, the income that already covers part of it, what this business must therefore supply, the run of periods that counts as supplying it, and what you do if it misses. Everything from chapter eleven onwards is an input to those five lines.

One thing to carry forward. Five of these six chapters measured the business as it is with you in it — your labour, your hours, your unpaid attention holding the gaps together. Part IV asks the question that assumption has been hiding: whether any of it survives your absence, and whether you are building a practice or an asset. Both are legitimate. Not knowing which you have is not.
