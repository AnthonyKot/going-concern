Enough

In 2014, five years after launching Pinboard, Maciej Cegłowski reported a business making "around $200K" a year, with "the number of active users" steady and no outside money in it. In the same post he wrote: "So the biggest risk in a project like this remains burnout." The money worked. What else has to be true before a business is enough?

The decision What this business needs to provide for your household, and whether you want to keep doing the work required to provide it.

Pinboard: a good living, and the strain of other people's stuff

Pinboard — Maciej Cegłowski, 2009–2020

Cegłowski launched the bookmarking service in July 2009 and ran it alone. In 2016 he published the revenue: $117,000 in 2010, rising to $234,000, with no outside funding at all. "The site costs something like $17K/year to run, so you can make a good living at this artisanal SaaS stuff." He published it, he said, to encourage people thinking of a very small business of their own.

Revenue did not settle the other question. "What burns you out is the constant strain of being responsible for a lot of other people's stuff." People had saved years of links with him; continuity was part of what they paid for. In 2017 he was travelling for political organising, and "All the travel meant I sometimes ignored support emails for weeks at a time". He came back for what he called a "reverse sabbatical to try to stabilize the site".

He also wrote what kept him going: "It is pleasant to work on something that people draw benefit from." And: "the longer I run the site, the more resistant I become to the idea of ever giving it up, even if I need to take the occasional break."

By 2020 revenue had eased to $212,000 and active users from 29,000 to 19,000, and the work of keeping the service running had not ended; chapter seventeen takes up that part.

So there are two tests, and neither answers the other. One is money: does enough reach the household? The other is the work: is this the responsibility you want to keep carrying? Pinboard shows how much strain can remain when the first test is passed; its owner's own answer to the second, in 2014, was still yes, breaks included. Yours may not be. A business can pass the first test and fail the second, or pass the second and leave the household short. And earning a living does not end the work of keeping the thing alive.

What must reach the household

Start from the household, not the business. Take twelve months of bank statements and write down what the household actually spent, separating fixed commitments from what you would willingly give up. Add what you can see coming: a mortgage reset, childcare, and the pension and paid leave your employer used to provide. Subtract other income you can rely on, such as a partner's pay after its own costs. What remains is the amount this business must supply, after tax, for a stated period. If you plan to live on savings for a while, write how much and for how long: that is a financed period, not an income.

Keep that number apart from chapter twelve's cost of your time. The €50 an hour judges whether the business pays for the work. The household figure says what your life needs from it. They are different questions, and one can pass while the other fails; neither is added on top of the other.

Lea, the invented developer from chapter one, can now close a sum that began there. She is still employed full time, so her salary covers the household. The business has sent it €600 over the two months of chapter twelve, with €300 proposed for the month after: about €300 a month. Her real question is the four-day week. Giving up that day would take €800 a month off what reaches the household, and the business costs about €100 a month to run whatever she sells. So enough, for the step she is weighing, is €800 a month reaching the household, plus the business's own €100: €900. Two single-file jobs leave €1,700 after tools, chapter one's Oakland number; the €800 beyond the €900 is what is left for tax and a bad month, and whether it is enough for tax is a question for her accountant, not this example. At €300 a month so far, from evenings alone, she is well short, and chapter ten has already shown why the four-day week waits. The number does not tell her to give up. It tells her what the freed day would have to produce, and it is the same number whether she is enthusiastic or tired.

The work you agree to keep

Now write down a working week you would willingly repeat, including selling and administration, and then what happens outside it. A service that promises emergency help has made a different commitment from a shop that can close its door until morning. Being available can spoil a weekend even if nobody calls; responsibility for a customer's data or an employee's work continues between tasks. Name the obligation that is wearing on you, if there is one. It may be possible to change the promise, train someone or pay for cover, and each has a cost to put beside its benefit.

Lea's is specific. Her customers' reports run on Monday mornings, when she is at her employer's desk. If one fails, who answers? At three customers she can apologise and fix it that evening. At two jobs a month for a year, she will have promised something she cannot keep from nine to five. That is not a reason to stop. It is exactly the kind of dependency chapter seventeen is about. Lea steps aside here and returns in the last chapter; the next chapters follow Noor, an invented owner a year or two further on, whose consultancy already has the problem Lea can see coming.

Talk it through with anyone whose income or time makes the business possible. One partner may welcome another year of growth while the other wants predictable evenings; agreeing a spending figure does not settle that. And the answer can be this is enough: if the income covers the household and the responsibilities are ones you want, you have a reason to continue at this size. You do not need an expansion plan to make that answer respectable.

Write it as one sentence with a date

The financial part fits in one line: “This business needs to supply our household with €X a month after tax from [date] to [date]; on [date] we will review the result and decide whether to raise prices, cut the offer, take contract work or stop.” Add the working conditions you are not willing to keep. Review over a period that includes the business's ordinary ups and downs, not a busy month; once a year is a sensible default, sooner if income, commitments or working conditions change. At the review, a changed need is not an excuse: a partner losing their job changes the household sum, and the old number should not be defended for its own sake.

Monday · one number for the household

Do this one thing. Open the last twelve months of bank statements and write one number: what the household needs each month, less other income you can rely on. That is what the business must supply after tax. Beside it, write what the business has actually sent the household per month so far. You now hold the gap, or the margin, in euros.

No twelve months to hand, or a partner's side unknown? Use the last three months, mark the number provisional, and list the irregular bills it will have missed. Asking your partner for their figure, with a date, counts as finishing.

If you have another hour:

  1. Write the week you would willingly repeat, and the one obligation outside it that is wearing on you, if there is one.
  2. Write the sentence above with a review date, and add the working condition you will not keep.
  3. Estimate the profit before tax your monthly figure needs, from last year's tax bill or the tax authority's own calculator; ask an accountant if you have one.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Pinboard's figures are the owner's own anniversary posts, not audited accounts. They do not disclose a household budget or a personal income target, and nothing in them shows he set such a target and failed to review it. The revenue table alone does not explain its changes: an earlier dip followed a switch to annual subscriptions. The 2017 post does not say he regretted the organising; it shows customers still needed support while he was away. The household review is this book's proposal. Lea, her €800, her drawings and her Monday reports are invented.

Still owed: a business held deliberately at a comfortable size that lost its customers to competitors it chose not to match, where a review like this one came too late. None has been found. A personal definition of enough does not oblige customers to keep buying, so watch the market as well as the household. The debt stands.

Closing Part III · Does the money work?

You now have several ways to question an apparently healthy business: whether customers get the result, whether cash arrives in time, whether the work pays for itself, and whether enough reaches you. A favourable answer to one does not settle the others.

By now the book has asked you to write several things down. Four are worth keeping current: your rule from chapter four, the customer record from chapter ten, the dated cash forecast from chapter thirteen, and the household number from this chapter. Everything else is consulted when it is relevant, and kept up only while it is in use: the period review when a period closes, and, in the chapters ahead, notes on who decides what when someone else does the work, a cover plan before a planned absence, a list of what you rely on but do not own, and three columns when a shock arrives. Most of them are a page, and most readers will not need all of them.

The next part asks what depends on your being there. That matters whether you want an organisation someone else could run or a practice you intend to keep. Both need arrangements for the work you cannot always do yourself.
