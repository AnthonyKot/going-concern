Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 16

Enough

The business pays its bills and there is money for you. You could take on more work, but you are no longer sure you want to. Before you call that complacency, ask what the extra work would buy you — and what running the business already requires.

The decision What this business needs to provide for your household, and whether you want to keep doing the work required to provide it.

When the business is already working

You can want a business to stay small. A practice that gives you good work and a good living is a legitimate thing to own, even if nobody will ever buy it from you. But staying small does not settle how much you need to earn, or who will answer the customer when you want a week away. Those questions remain after the decision against expansion.

Start by separating two numbers. Replacing your work might cost €70,000 in a year when your household needs €45,000, or €30,000 in a year when it needs €60,000. These are illustrative amounts, but the distinction matters. The price of your labour tells you something about the economics of the business. The cost of your life tells you what owning it needs to do for you. Neither amount can be discovered from the other.

The previous chapter asks whether the business could afford to pay for the work you do. Here the question is whether the money that reaches you is enough, and what you are giving up to earn it. A business can pass the first test and leave your household short. It can support the household while paying poorly for your hours. It can pass both and still occupy more of your life than you want.

That last possibility deserves attention before a crisis makes it urgent. Perhaps you like the work but dislike being permanently available. Perhaps the household is content with the income while you still want to build something larger. There is a decision to make in either case. An accountant can help establish what the choices cost; the accounts cannot tell you which life to prefer.

What Pinboard could afford

Maciej Cegłowski launched the bookmarking service Pinboard in July 2009. In his fifth anniversary post, he described a business making around $200,000 a year, with a roughly stable active-user count. For a service entrusted with people's saved links, continuity was part of what customers were buying. He wanted them to believe it would remain available, and earning enough to keep operating was part of that promise.

He also identified a problem the revenue did not solve: “the biggest risk in a project like this remains burnout.” His account dwelt on the continuing responsibility for other people's material. He valued making something useful and lasting, and described taking occasional breaks. The same service could be satisfying to own and tiring to be responsible for. Both observations belonged in his account of its success.

In 2016 he added revenue to the anniversary statistics. The published series ran from $117,000 in 2010 to $234,000 in 2016, with no external funding recorded. He put annual running costs at about $17,000 and said he was publishing the figures to encourage people thinking about a very small business of their own. These were his own figures, not audited accounts. They showed why the model interested him; they did not disclose his household spending or set out a personal income target.

The following year's post described another commitment. Travel for political organising had left some support emails unanswered for weeks. He then returned to work on stabilising the site and catching up with the backlog. The account does not say he regretted the organising. It does show that customers still needed support while his attention was elsewhere.

By the 2020 anniversary, revenue in the published table had fallen from its 2017 peak of $259,000 to $212,000. Active users had fallen from 29,000 to 19,000 over those years. The table alone does not explain the changes: an earlier dip and rebound in revenue had followed a switch to annual subscriptions. Nor does the table show whether the later income remained sufficient for him.

That 2020 post was largely about maintenance. Cegłowski had been upgrading old software and overdue server systems while keeping the service running. An established business was still generating work that its customers might barely notice if it went well. The next chapter examines the operational dependence; here it matters because earning a living had not brought the owner's obligations to an end.

Nothing in these four posts establishes that he set a household sufficiency target and then failed to review it. That private decision cannot be read out of a public revenue table. The record shows an owner earning a living, pursuing other interests and returning to responsibilities that continued in his absence. That is a more demanding version of enough than a revenue line alone can describe.

What would make this enough for you?

Work out what must reach the household

Use twelve months of statements to establish what the household has actually spent. Separate regular commitments from purchases you would willingly forgo. Then add foreseeable changes: a mortgage reset, childcare, a dependant's needs, or the pension provision and paid time off that employment used to supply. Last year's spending is a starting record. It is not a forecast and need not be your preferred standard of living.

Subtract other income you can depend on, such as a partner's earnings or a pension in payment. Use what is available to the household after its associated costs, rather than a headline figure. If there is no other income, this line is zero. If you plan to spend savings, record how much and for how long; that describes a financed period, not a permanent source of income.

The remainder is the net amount this business needs to supply. Work back from it to the sales and cash the business requires, allowing for its costs and the taxes that apply to you. Label the period each figure covers. Keep the net household amount alongside the business calculation so that you do not compare a household need with gross revenue. Ask your accountant to check the conversion where pay, drawings and taxes make it difficult to follow.

Keep the replacement-cost test separate. An imputed wage for your work is a way to judge the business; it is not another payment your household needs on top of the money it lives on. Reconcile actual owner pay or drawings with the household figure instead of adding the same income twice. The two tests can then give different answers without muddling what has actually been earned or paid.

Suppose the business could pay someone else to do your work but could not support your household at the income you need. Improving its efficiency may help, but the calculation has not established that this particular business can meet that need. Conversely, a low-cost household may be content with earnings below the replacement wage. You can choose that arrangement while recognising what the extra hours cost you.

Describe the work you are agreeing to keep

Write down a working week you would willingly repeat. Include the administration and selling as well as the work customers pay for. Then name what has to happen outside those hours. A service that promises emergency help has made a different commitment from a shop that can close its door until morning. The owner of either needs to know who deals with the exceptions.

Hours will not capture all of it. Being available to answer a call can restrict a weekend even if nobody calls. Responsibility for customer data or an employee's work continues between tasks. Name the obligation that is wearing on you, if there is one. It may be possible to change the promise, train someone else or pay for cover. Each possibility needs costing before you count its benefit as yours.

For a hypothetical bakery, the difficulty might be finding someone who can open early enough for the owner to take a week away. The present income might be adequate until cover is included. That creates a choice about prices, staffing or the service offered. Pinboard's figures cannot answer it, but its account should discourage the assumption that reaching an income target automatically buys freedom from the work.

Talk through these choices with anyone whose income or time makes the business possible. One partner may welcome another year of expansion while the other wants predictable evenings. Agreement on a household spending figure does not resolve that difference. Say what each person is being asked to contribute before treating growth or stability as a shared aim.

Choose what you will review

Once you have an income requirement and a description of the work, compare them with a period that includes the business's ordinary variation. A busy month is a poor guide to a seasonal year. Use a full cycle where you have one; where you do not, say what the record cannot yet show. Do not invent a run of successful months by choosing the start after seeing the result.

An annual household review is a useful default, with earlier checks when income, commitments or working conditions change materially. Read several periods together when the figures are available, looking at customer numbers and costs alongside revenue. Investigate a decline when it becomes apparent; do not wait for an anniversary when you already know a bill cannot be met.

You can put the financial part in one sentence: “This business needs to supply our household with €X after tax over [period]; on [date] we will review the result and decide whether to raise prices, cut the offer, take contract work or stop.” Those are possible responses, not a compulsory sequence. Add the working conditions you are unwilling to keep. A page that records only money will miss an agreement about evenings or time away.

When you review it, distinguish a changed need from an excuse to ignore the result. A partner losing their income changes the household calculation. A customer paying late changes the cash available. Neither is made easier by insisting that the old number must stand. Write what changed and what you now intend to do; if more time would help, explain what you expect that time to reveal and how you will pay for it.

A business whose numbers work is not therefore worth your forties. But it may be worth them: you may enjoy the work, value the independence and be content with what it pays. If the income covers the household and the responsibilities are ones you want to keep, you have a reason to continue at this size. You do not need an expansion plan to make that answer respectable.

Monday

  1. Calculate what the household needs from the business. Start with a year's spending, adjust for known changes and subtract dependable outside income. Keep the net requirement beside the business calculation needed to supply it.
  2. Put the actual work beside that number. Use your calendar and name the responsibilities that continue outside working hours. Identify one condition you want to change, or state that the present arrangement suits you.
  3. Agree on the next decision. With anyone sharing the cost, write what would count as enough, the period you will examine and a review date. Choose an action now for any shortfall or working condition that is already clear; leave unresolved questions visible.

Where this advice could fail

A business held at a comfortable size might lose customers to competitors whose investment it cannot match. An annual review could discover that too late. I have not found a documented case of an owner using the financial and working-condition review proposed here and losing the business because of that choice. Pinboard cannot establish its safety. Use the review to notice a change in your position, and investigate changes in the market when they appear; a personal definition of enough does not oblige customers to keep buying.

Closing Part III · Does the money work?

You now have several ways to question an apparently healthy business: whether customers get the result, whether cash arrives in time, whether the work pays for itself, and whether enough reaches you. A favourable answer to one does not settle the others.

The next part asks what depends on your being there. That matters whether you want an organisation someone else could run or a practice you intend to keep. Both need arrangements for the work you cannot always do yourself.
