Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 24

What You Signed

You have signed a lease, a bank mandate and a platform's terms you did not read. You have not signed the agreement with the freelancer who built your website, the terms your customers are supposedly bound by, or anything at all about who pays when a job goes wrong. All of those are agreements anyway. The question is only who wrote their terms — you, in advance, or the law and the other side, on the day it matters.

The decision Which of the four agreements every business already has — with the people who make things, with the customers who buy them, with the suppliers and platforms it depends on, and with the world about who pays for a mistake — to put in writing this quarter, in what order, and which of them to spend an hour of a professional's time on before signing anything else.

Move 1 The situation

Nothing has gone wrong. That is the situation, and it is the only time this chapter is cheap. The website was built by someone you paid by bank transfer against an emailed quote. Your terms of business are a page you wrote from a template and linked in the footer of the invoice. The supplier delivers on a handshake and a monthly statement. The one time a job went badly you absorbed the cost yourself, quietly, because it was easier than finding out whose it was. Every one of those is an agreement, and every one has terms. You did not write them, so somebody else did.

The instinct that keeps them unwritten is not laziness. It is that writing them down feels like the moment the business stops being a relationship and starts being a contract, and you would rather it stayed the former. But the relationship was never what would decide a dispute. On the day the freelancer says the code is theirs, or the customer says they never saw the clause, or the insurer asks what exactly you had agreed to deliver, what decides it is what was written, where it was written, and whether it was handed over in time — and if the answer is nothing, nowhere and no, the terms are whatever the law's default says, which you have never read either.

Move 2 What the rules establish

No company case with a primary document establishing the loss was found for this chapter. Instead, it examines four kinds of agreement an owner needs to locate and read. Use the four questions to organise the review, but check the applicable law before relying on an answer.

The people who make things. Someone who is not you has produced something the business depends on: the site, the logo, the product photographs, the code, the recipe cards. You paid for it. Whether you own it is a separate question, and the answer is not in the invoice alone. Find the agreement describing the rights you acquired: ownership, a licence to use the work, or something narrower. The Dutch provisions distinguish the maker's rights, work made in employment, and written transfers or exclusive licences. Other jurisdictions need their own check. Payment by itself is not a reliable record of the rights your business needs.

The customers who buy. You have terms: a limit on what you owe if a job goes wrong, a payment period, what happens to a deposit. Find when and how the customer received them. The cited Dutch provisions address both their content and the opportunity to read them, subject to the scope and exceptions described below. A link first supplied on an invoice after the deal may be too late. Ask whether your actual process makes the terms part of the agreement; do not assume that possessing a terms document settles it.

The suppliers and platforms you depend on. Here the position is reversed. You are the customer. Read the terms you accepted for the payment processor, marketplace, software and premises. Look for provisions about reserves, delisting, price changes and termination. These are the supplier's terms; this chapter cannot know who drafted them or whether you can negotiate them. Establish what they permit before depending on the service.

The world, about who pays. The fourth question is what happens when your work causes a loss to someone else. The customer's site is down for a week because of your update; the food you supplied made someone ill; the advice you gave was wrong and they acted on it. Somebody pays. Who — you, the business, an insurer — is a question with three parts: your legal form, which is the fork chapter twelve met in the filing rules and the notaris's subject rather than this book's; what your terms say and whether they can be held to; and a policy you may or may not hold. This book gives no figure for what such a loss could be, because any figure would be wrong for someone. It gives the shape: the largest loss one customer could suffer from your mistake is a number, you can estimate it, and the estimate is the question you take to an insurance adviser.

Move 3 The reasoning

An unwritten agreement has terms anyway

An unwritten arrangement can still create obligations. Writing it down gives you a record to compare with the other party's understanding and to put before an adviser. Whether a contract exists, which terms apply and what formalities are required depend on the arrangement and the applicable law. Do not wait for a dispute to discover what each person thinks was agreed.

For the website, brand or code, locate the rights agreement while the person who made the work is still available to discuss it. Check whether the business can use, change and hand over the work as it needs to. For customer terms, retain the version supplied and the evidence of when it was supplied. For supplier contracts, mark the provisions that could interrupt your work or change its cost. These records make a legal review possible without pretending that the same wording works everywhere.

When to pay someone who knows

One failure is the handshake business that discovers its terms on the day of the dispute. The other is the two-person studio with thirty pages of terms nobody reads, copied from a much larger company without checking whether they fit the work. A professional's hour is owed when the agreement carries more than a month's revenue, lasts longer than a year, contains the words unlimited, indemnify or exclusive, or concerns something the business could not replace — and the hour is cheap precisely because you arrive with the four agreements listed and the question written down. Everything below that line is a template, read once, handed over in time, and kept, provided it fits the transaction. These thresholds are prompts for seeking help, not assurance that smaller agreements are safe.

The boundary is that this chapter maps where the cost is; it does not say what any clause should say, and it cannot, because the defaults differ by country and the right terms differ by trade. Bring the agreements to an adviser who knows the applicable law. Start with the agreement whose failure would most disrupt your business. For one owner that will be rights to the product; for another, the lease or an uninsured obligation. The four headings organise the review, not a universal ranking of the risks.

Monday

  1. List the four agreements as they exist today. Makers, customers, suppliers and platforms, liability. For each, three columns: written or not; where the writing is; who else has a copy. Any row that reads no, nowhere, nobody is a term somebody else will write.
  2. For every piece of work you rely on and did not make yourself, locate the agreement covering your rights. Site, logo, code, photographs, copy. If it is missing or unclear, ask the maker what rights they believe they granted and take any gap to a lawyer. Establish whether ownership or a licence supplies what the business needs.
  3. Move your terms from the invoice to the quote. Attach them to whatever the customer sees before they say yes, and keep the evidence that you did — the dated email, the signed page. Then read them once as the customer would, and cut anything you would refuse if it were put to you.
  4. Write the largest-loss sentence. If we get this wrong, the customer loses … — with a number, estimated, for the worst job you take. Take the sentence, the number and your legal form to an insurance adviser and ask what a policy against it costs. Decide with the two numbers side by side.
  5. Buy one hour. Take items 1 to 4 and the two agreements that carry the most money to a lawyer, and ask one question: what have I agreed to that I do not know about? Write the answer down and date it.

What this chapter owes

Two things. The case: a small self-funded business with a dated agreement — a client contract, a standard-terms page, a contractor's quote, a policy — and a cost that followed from what it did or did not say; none was found with a primary document rather than a law firm's retelling. And the counter-case: a business that wrote all four agreements down, delivered its terms in time, bought the hour — and was worse for it, because the written agreement was what the customer walked away from, or doing it right cost more than every loss it prevented. Until either arrives, the argument rests on what the statute does by default, which is at least a thing the reader can check.

On Dutch ground · who owns the work, and whether your terms bind

This section is for readers in the Netherlands; elsewhere the four agreements keep their shape and the defaults differ. Two of the four agreements have statutory answers this section can quote. The other two — what your suppliers' terms say, and what you are liable for and insured against — it cannot, and names the professional instead.

Who owns the work. The Auteurswet starts from the maker: "Het auteursrecht is het uitsluitend recht van den maker van een werk van letterkunde, wetenschap of kunst, of van diens rechtverkrijgenden, om dit openbaar te maken en te verveelvoudigen, behoudens de beperkingen, bij de wet gesteld." Article 7 moves that default to the employer where making such works is what the employee is employed to do, unless the contract says otherwise: "Indien de arbeid, in dienst van een ander verricht, bestaat in het vervaardigen van bepaalde werken van letterkunde, wetenschap of kunst, dan wordt, tenzij tusschen partijen anders is overeengekomen, als de maker van die werken aangemerkt degene, in wiens dienst de werken zijn vervaardigd." Two conditions, then: in another's employ, and employed to make this kind of thing; and a contract can undo the default. In dienst van een ander is the line, and a contractor is not in your employ; chapter nineteen's Dutch-ground section is where that line is drawn. For everyone who is not an employee, article 2 lid 3 governs: "De overeenkomst op grond waarvan het auteursrecht geheel of gedeeltelijk wordt overgedragen of waarin een exclusieve licentie wordt verleend, wordt schriftelijk aangegaan." In writing. And when the person transferring is the one who made it — the freelancer, the friend — the transfer reaches only as far as the writing goes: "De overdracht of de verlening van een exclusieve licentie door de maker … omvat alleen die bevoegdheden die uitdrukkelijk in de overeenkomst staan vermeld of die uit de aard en de strekking ervan noodzakelijkerwijs voortvloeien." Lid 5 takes that second sentence away where the transferor is itself a maker under articles 7 or 8 — "Het derde lid, tweede volzin, en het vierde lid zijn niet van toepassing op een maker als bedoeld in artikel 7 en 8" — so an agency transferring its employees' work is the jurist's question, not this section's.

Whether your terms bind. Book 6 of the Civil Code, article 233: "Een beding in algemene voorwaarden is vernietigbaar a. indien het, gelet op de aard en de overige inhoud van de overeenkomst, de wijze waarop de voorwaarden zijn tot stand gekomen, de wederzijds kenbare belangen van partijen en de overige omstandigheden van het geval, onredelijk bezwarend is voor de wederpartij; of b. indien de gebruiker aan de wederpartij niet een redelijke mogelijkheid heeft geboden om van de algemene voorwaarden kennis te nemen." Two grounds: unreasonably onerous, or no reasonable opportunity to read. Article 234 says what the opportunity is: "De gebruiker heeft aan de wederpartij de in artikel 233 onder b bedoelde mogelijkheid geboden, indien hij de algemene voorwaarden voor of bij het sluiten van de overeenkomst aan de wederpartij ter hand heeft gesteld" — handed over before or at the conclusion of the contract — with the further routes the article lists after that clause, and with the consequence that if they were not handed over then, the terms are also voidable where the user does not send them "op verzoek van de wederpartij onverwijld op zijn kosten". Whom this section reaches is its own question: article 247 makes it inapplicable between businesses "die niet beide in Nederland gevestigd zijn", and article 235 takes the grounds away from large counterparties — among them a party "bij haar vijftig of meer personen werkzaam zijn" — which is one of the things to put in front of the jurist with your customer list.

None of this is advice, and this section stops short of the two agreements it cannot quote. What you are liable for, whether a limit in your terms would hold, and what a professional indemnity or business liability policy would cover in your trade are questions for a jurist or advocaat and for an insurance adviser, with your actual terms and your largest-loss estimate in front of them. Ask them, in those words, which of your agreements has terms you have not read.
