Agreements and Protection

When the Glif, the tripod clamp from chapter two, took off, its makers were "freaked out we didn’t have a patent on the design." Copies appeared from Germany and Taiwan. They decided they would not have chased them even with a patent. Their question is the one to ask of every protection you hold or buy: what would you actually do with it? Maya is asking it the other way round, about what she is allowed to do, with €360 due on Monday.

The decision Which exposure to address first, what an agreement or protective measure is supposed to change, and what action, evidence and professional review are needed before relying on it.

A patent they decided not to get

Studio Neat's founders, Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost, wrote the story in the “A Word on Patents” section of their account. When the Glif started selling, they panicked about the missing patent. Then they looked into it, and what they learned, in their own words and with their own warning to "contact an expert rather than relying solely on the advice from two dudes with no legal expertise", was that getting one looked like "a black hole that sucks up money and spits out sadness." They passed.

The copies came anyway: "there have been a few direct imitations that have cropped up, out of Germany and Taiwan." And here is the part worth keeping. They imagined the strongest protection they could have had and asked: "Would we really be willing to sink the time and money into going after them? Our guess is no." They would rather design the next product, and they bet that "As long as our brand remains strong, we think enough customers will choose the real deal versus the knockoff."

That is not legal advice, and this book does not use their description of patents as any. It is a decision about money and time: a protection is worth what you would actually do with it, at the price of doing it. Whether the brand bet paid off, their account does not measure.

Maya's Sunday night

Maya, Sam and Dana are invented, continuing from chapter twenty-three. The cover plan is ready. The €360 for Dana's two mornings is due on Monday and cannot be refunded once paid. On Sunday night Maya asks the question the plan skipped: if Dana cannot use the reporting tool and the customer's export, which promised report becomes impossible?

She finds what exists. The tool was built by Eli, an outside developer: there is a paid invoice, a quote marked version 1, and an email saying the tool is for the consultancy's internal reporting. None of it mentions an outside provider using it, or who may change it when Eli is away. Maya does not turn that silence into permission, or into a ban. It is a question. She also finds the customer's order and the terms attached when it was accepted, Dana's unsigned proposal, and the insurance schedule with its policy wording. For each she notes the title, version, date, parties, the passage that matters and the evidence it was accepted. A missing document gets a line too: what she searched for, and whom she will ask.

Four headings keep the search honest, not because every business has four contracts. What may the business do with work others made? What has it promised the customer? What have suppliers promised it? And if something goes wrong, who pays, and who must act? Owning a tool and being permitted to use it are different things. Maya does not need to own Eli's code. She needs to know the use she plans is covered.

Two sentences for every protection

For each document or measure, Maya completes two sentences: This is intended to change… and To use it, we would need…. Studio Neat did the same with a patent they did not have, and the second sentence decided it.

Eli's terms are intended to settle what the consultancy may do with the tool; to rely on them for Dana, Maya needs his permission for that use, in writing. The customer's terms are intended to govern who sees their data; to let Dana in, Maya needs the customer's approval under those terms. The insurance is intended to meet the cost if a wrong report is used; to rely on it, she needs to know whether it covers work done by an outside provider and what she must report, and when. Dana's proposal promises two windows; if Dana falls ill, it offers no named replacement. Whether Maya could get money back is a question for the terms. Either way, a refund does not supply a qualified reviewer at 09:20. Keeping the promise and getting compensated are two separate problems.

Every measure gets judged by what it changes before anything goes wrong, not only by what a dispute would cost. A licence that lets Dana work has done its job on the first morning. Where you cannot tell what a clause does, the uncertainty is itself the finding. For readers in the Netherlands, the section at the end quotes the rules on who owns commissioned work and when general terms bind, and says what they leave to a lawyer.

What Monday brought

At 22:00 on Sunday Maya sends three short emails, each naming the exact use: to Eli, to the customer and to her insurance broker. She asks the people who can grant permission before paying anyone to interpret silence.

Eli answers at 09:10. Dana may run the tool and review its output for the consultancy's customer reports; changes to the code stay with him. He sends it as a short signed note. The customer answers at 11:30: Dana may see the exports under their confidentiality terms once she signs their agreement, which she does before lunch. The broker has not answered by 16:00.

Two of three answers arrived in writing within a day, and one of them changed the plan. A fault that needs a code change is now outside Dana's permission: it goes to Eli, or it waits, and the customer is told which. Maya adds that to the recovery steps. None of the answers cost money, so the €20 margin in the forecast still stands; a paid review would have needed its own date and funding before she could count on it.

The insurance question is still open, so Maya pays the €360 at 16:00 for what is now permitted, not for what she hopes. Tuesday runs as planned, with Dana on the permitted work and Maya reachable. Thursday, her first truly unreachable morning, stays paused until the broker answers; she will recheck at 17:00 on Wednesday, and without an answer she works Thursday herself. If any permission itself had stayed unclear, that use would have stayed paused too, however convenient the plan. Her record now reads: exposure — outside cover using a tool and data the consultancy does not own; answered — use permitted, code changes excluded, data access approved under confidentiality; open — insurance position; next — broker, Wednesday 17:00.

Eli's note is good enough for Tuesday. Whether it would be enough for anything larger, such as selling the business with the tool inside it, is a question to take to an adviser before it matters; the optional chapter Built to Leave is about that day. First, though, everything here has assumed the customers stay where they are. Chapter twenty-six is about the month they do not.

Monday · one dependency, two sentences

Do this one thing. Name one thing your business cannot run without and does not own: a tool someone built for you, a customer's data, a platform account, a supplier. Find the document that governs your use of it and write its two sentences — this is intended to change…, to use it, we would need…. You now hold one dependency, the document behind it, and either a confirmed use or a precise question.

Document missing, silent, or unclear? Ask the party who can grant the use, in writing, naming exactly what you want to do. Until the answer comes, that use stays paused: tell the customer what service continues meanwhile, and write the date you will check again. If you cannot reach or afford an adviser for a question only an adviser can answer, the same rule holds. Do not treat an unanswered question as permission. That is Monday done.

If you have another hour:

  1. Run the four headings — work made by others, promises to customers, suppliers' promises, loss and response — over your business, and list each document's version and parties.
  2. Put the costs on dates. Any fee, licence or policy change goes into the cash forecast; a possible payout or refund does not.
  3. Write one question for an adviser that they could answer in an hour: the documents, the exact use, and the decision that waits on it.
What this chapter rests on, and what it cannot show

Studio Neat's section is two founders' account of their own decision, with their own warning that they are not legal experts. It supports a choice about time and money and an untested belief about brand. It does not establish what a patent would have done, what the imitations did to sales, or whether the brand held. Their description of US patents is not used as legal guidance. WIPO's guidance supports the distinction between ownership and permission to use, not the interpretation of any agreement.

Maya, Eli, Dana, the customer and every reply are invented. No legal reading of Eli's note, the customer's terms or the insurance position is established here.

Still owed: a documented small business that did this kind of review competently and was worse off because its preparation and protection costs exceeded the benefit, or because a carefully chosen measure obstructed necessary work.

On Dutch ground · who owns the work, and whether your terms bind

This section is for readers in the Netherlands. It quotes dated provisions relevant to two review questions: rights in work and general terms. It does not determine the permissions in the fictional example, a supplier's obligations or insurance coverage.

Who owns the work. The Auteurswet starts from the maker: "Het auteursrecht is het uitsluitend recht van den maker van een werk van letterkunde, wetenschap of kunst, of van diens rechtverkrijgenden, om dit openbaar te maken en te verveelvoudigen, behoudens de beperkingen, bij de wet gesteld." Article 7 moves that default to the employer where making such works is what the employee is employed to do, unless the contract says otherwise: "Indien de arbeid, in dienst van een ander verricht, bestaat in het vervaardigen van bepaalde werken van letterkunde, wetenschap of kunst, dan wordt, tenzij tusschen partijen anders is overeengekomen, als de maker van die werken aangemerkt degene, in wiens dienst de werken zijn vervaardigd." Two conditions, then: in another's employ, and employed to make this kind of thing; and a contract can change the default. Do not decide employment from a contractor label: the Belastingdienst's assessment guidance considers the actual facts and circumstances. See chapter nineteen's Dutch-ground section. Article 2 lid 3 addresses transfer and exclusive licensing: "De overeenkomst op grond waarvan het auteursrecht geheel of gedeeltelijk wordt overgedragen of waarin een exclusieve licentie wordt verleend, wordt schriftelijk aangegaan." In writing. And when the person transferring is the one who made it — the freelancer, the friend — the transfer reaches only as far as the writing goes: "De overdracht of de verlening van een exclusieve licentie door de maker … omvat alleen die bevoegdheden die uitdrukkelijk in de overeenkomst staan vermeld of die uit de aard en de strekking ervan noodzakelijkerwijs voortvloeien." Lid 5 takes that second sentence away where the transferor is itself a maker under articles 7 or 8 — "Het derde lid, tweede volzin, en het vierde lid zijn niet van toepassing op een maker als bedoeld in artikel 7 en 8" — so an agency transferring its employees' work is the jurist's question, not this section's. Article 2 lid 3 also states the delivery requirement for a transfer: "De levering vereist voor overdracht geschiedt ingevolge artikel 95 van Boek 3 van het Burgerlijk Wetboek bij een daartoe bestemde akte." The formalities for the proposed transaction need their own review.

Whether your terms bind. Book 6 of the Civil Code, article 233: "Een beding in algemene voorwaarden is vernietigbaar a. indien het, gelet op de aard en de overige inhoud van de overeenkomst, de wijze waarop de voorwaarden zijn tot stand gekomen, de wederzijds kenbare belangen van partijen en de overige omstandigheden van het geval, onredelijk bezwarend is voor de wederpartij; of b. indien de gebruiker aan de wederpartij niet een redelijke mogelijkheid heeft geboden om van de algemene voorwaarden kennis te nemen." Two grounds: unreasonably onerous, or no reasonable opportunity to read. Article 234 says what the opportunity is: "De gebruiker heeft aan de wederpartij de in artikel 233 onder b bedoelde mogelijkheid geboden, indien hij de algemene voorwaarden voor of bij het sluiten van de overeenkomst aan de wederpartij ter hand heeft gesteld" — handed over before or at the conclusion of the contract — with the further routes the article lists after that clause, and with the consequence that if they were not handed over then, the terms are also voidable where the user does not send them "op verzoek van de wederpartij onverwijld op zijn kosten". Whom this section reaches is its own question: article 247 makes it inapplicable between businesses "die niet beide in Nederland gevestigd zijn", and article 235 takes the grounds away from large counterparties — among them a party "bij haar vijftig of meer personen werkzaam zijn" — which is one of the things to put in front of the jurist with your customer list.

This section stops short of determining liability or coverage in your arrangement. What you are liable for, whether a limit in your terms would hold, and what a professional indemnity or business liability policy would cover in your trade are questions for a jurist or advocaat and for an insurance adviser, with your actual documents, proposed work and exposure scenario in front of them. Ask which permissions, obligations and response requirements remain unresolved.
