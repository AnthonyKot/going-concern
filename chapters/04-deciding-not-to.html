What Would Justify the Next Step

In seven years Rachel Draelos's company collected two patents, a clinical study, a competitive grant and four paying customers. Any one of them would have passed the rule most founders write, if there is no sign of traction by next summer, I stop. She shut it down anyway. What evidence would justify your next commitment: another year, another €10,000, or your salary?

The decision What evidence, written down now, would justify your next commitment, and what you will do on the date if it has not arrived. Leaving your job is one such commitment.

Cydoc: seven years of the wrong evidence

Cydoc — Rachel Draelos, April 2018 – August 2025

Rachel Draelos is an MD/PhD. She started Cydoc to automate the taking of patient histories, funded at first by nothing but, in her words, "my $35,000 annual MD/PhD stipend."

Over seven years it accumulated a working, regulation-compliant platform deployed in real practices. A study with eighteen medical students showed the system "saved 11 minutes per visit". Two U.S. patents. A competitive grant. Four paying customers, four-figure recurring revenue and five-figure custom development revenue. She calls these "what many consider to be validation milestones: paying customers, patents, and demonstrated clinical impact." She drained her savings, raised $265,000 from family, friends and physicians, and pitched "over 35 different VC firms, but they were not interested in funding a solo founder without any revenue."

Then the arithmetic arrived. The small practices she was selling to "wanted to pay fewer than $100 per month." After adding an AI scribe, hosting and AI costs had risen to "about $70 per doctor." And reaching each practice meant connecting to its electronic health record system, where "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000". Her conclusion: "it would take 11 years per practice just to break even". She shut Cydoc down in August 2025.

Duration7 years
Raised from family, friends, physicians$265,000
Paying customers4
Left per doctor~$30/mo
Cost per integration$4–6k
Break-even per practice11 years

Her own prescription: "ask them to buy it before you build it."

Every milestone on that list was real, and none answered the question the business needed answered. A patent is evidence of novelty, not demand. A study is evidence that it works, not that anyone will pay. A grant says a panel liked the proposal. Four customers proved people valued it. None of it asked whether Cydoc could keep finding practices willing to pay a price that covered winning, connecting and serving them. That is why collecting them felt like progress, and why a rule written in the language of traction would have been satisfied again and again, for years.

Write the rule in the currency you need

You will be a worse judge of this later than you are today. In eighteen months you will have spent money, evenings and some of your sense of yourself as someone who finishes things, and all of it will be in the room when you reassess. Writing the rule now costs an evening and is the only time you can write it without a stake in the answer. It needs three parts.

1. A number in money, from customers who could have said no. Not interest, pilots, users or press. Money that has cleared, from buyers who could comfortably have refused, at a price and scope you could repeat, and leaving enough after what it cost to win and serve them. Each clause matters. One of Cydoc's four customers came "through a very warm intro": her husband's uncle. Five-figure custom work is real money and says little about whether a product will sell. And the last clause is the one that would have failed Cydoc: four practices paid, and each needed eleven years to repay its integration. The number the money has to clear is the one you worked out in chapter one.

2. A date, and the test actually run before it. Fixed now, in the calendar. A date only means something if the experiment runs in the time it bounds. Draelos decided who her customer was and then, she writes, "An entire additional year elapsed before I talked to my first real potential customers." A threshold missed because nobody asked measures the calendar, not the market.

3. What failing it means. This is the part almost nobody writes, and stop cannot be the automatic answer. The number on the date comes from two things the rule cannot tell apart: whether the market wants this, and whether you have yet learned the parts of the job you did not know at the start, usually selling. So choose from the whole menu: stop; mothball it and keep the domain; cut to the one part that showed life; hand it to someone else; or go back to paid work and continue more slowly. The next story is why the last option belongs on the list.

Gymdesk: the date passed, and he went back to a salary

Gymdesk — Eran Galperin, 2016–2019

Eran Galperin launched Gymdesk, gym-management software, at the beginning of 2016. He had a date and a limit: he hoped, by his own later account, to "reach enough revenue within the 6 months runway I had built". He had no customers until April. After six months the answer was plainly no.

He did not stop, and he did not carry on as before. He took contract work, then a salaried job, and put in "a stipulation that I would only be working 4 days a week". That left three days for the business. By the end of 2018 it had reached $10k in monthly recurring revenue, and in June 2019 he went back to it full time. His verdict on the original plan: "I was wrong. In reality it took 3 whole years for the business to grow to the point it could provide for me full time."

Galperin returned to salaried work and kept building Gymdesk on three days a week. His account does not say whether the business would have survived another route, so it cannot show that the salary saved it. It does show what stop would have ended: a business that, three years later, supported him full time. On his own telling, the six months measured less whether Gymdesk could work than how much he still had to learn about selling it. That is the case for putting more than stop on the menu, and for choosing from it now, while you are calm, rather than improvising under pressure as he did.

The salary is one more commitment

Galperin's four-day job points at the commitment most readers of this book are really weighing. Leaving employment is not a test of nerve. It is a next step like any other, and it gets the same question: what evidence would justify it?

Bingo Card Creator — Patrick McKenzie, Nagoya, 2006–2010

In 2006 an American engineer working in Nagoya started selling a small program that printed bingo cards for primary-school teachers. His ambition was "eventually making perhaps $200 a month". He kept the job for about four years, and it was not a gentle one: he left the office "at 7:30 PM on a very good day", with an hour and a half of commute each way. The business ran, on his estimate, on about five hours a week.

He published the numbers while it was happening. In 2009, still employed, it took $31,156.18 net of refunds and returned $18,525.71 in profit, which he reckoned at $125–$150 for each hour he put in. In March 2010 he wrote that it "has made it possible to quit my day job at the end of this month." He went full time on 1 April 2010.

Time on the business~5 h/week
Years before leaving~4
2009 profit$18,526
Effective rate$125–150/h

He did not leave when the business was exciting. He left when it was boring: four years old, two published annual cycles, a profit line that grew while he barely touched it. The evidence he acted on was not a good month. It was a shape.

His path is one way to build evidence while employed, and it suits a business that sells while you are at work. It is not the only one. Whatever the path, the comparison is your floor, not your salary: what the household needs (mortgage, insurance, food) plus the benefits your employer pays now and you would pay yourself. It is usually lower than your gross salary and higher than people guess. Three paths, each with its own cost and its own gap:

  • Repeat revenue while employed. Months of paid sales from more than one buyer and route, as McKenzie had. The strongest evidence, and the slowest: it needs a business that earns on evenings, and years of a finite career. It says little about what happens when your full attention arrives, which may help less than expected.
  • Paid projects while employed. A few fixed-price jobs done in evenings or on leave, from buyers who could have said no. They show that people pay and what the work really takes, as Lea's do. They do not show that enough of them will arrive every month, and the hours you can sell part-time cap how much they can prove.
  • Signed commitments and savings. Where the work needs you in working hours, written commitments from independent buyers who know you are not free yet, plus savings counted in months of floor to the date clients actually pay, not the date they sign. This does not show the business is viable. It shows how long you can afford to find out, and it only works as the rule above, with a consequence you can honour, such as a return to paid work.

None of these removes the uncertainty. Each moves the decision from nerve to a bet with the odds written down, and says which gap you are betting across.

Lea, the invented developer from chapter one, is on the second path: her reports are her hours. Her first rule was if three customers have paid by the end of March, and the jobs took roughly the ten hours each I estimated, I ask for the four-day week. Its second half, if not, I don't, and I find out why, is not yet a consequence. So she writes one from the menu. If fewer than three have paid, she keeps five days and spends April only asking people like the ones who paid. If three have paid but the jobs ran well over ten hours, she keeps five days and fixes delivery before asking again. Only if both hold does she ask for the four-day week, and only with the answer she already sought: that she can move back, and on what notice. Her next commitment is not resigning. It is one day, with a door. Chapter ten shows what the end of March brought.

Monday · one sentence with a number, a date and a consequence

Do this one thing. Name your next commitment: the next thing that costs real money or time and is hard to undo, whether that is a year of evenings, a purchase, a four-day week or your notice. Then write one sentence: if fewer than [N] customers who could have said no have paid me [amount] by [date], I will [action]. Choose the action from the menu (stop, mothball, cut to what worked, hand it on, or back to paid work and slower), not from "stop or don't". Put the date in your calendar with the sentence pasted in. You now hold the rule your future self will be tempted to renegotiate.

Cannot fill in N or the amount? Write what is missing and the action that will get it, and pause the commitment until then. For example: threshold unknown; get the cost of one sale and chapter one's number by Friday; the four-day week stays unrequested until the sentence is complete. That counts as finishing. A blank with only a date does not.

Before changing your employment, whether reducing your hours or resigning, three checks are required, not optional. They are not part of Monday's twenty minutes; they are a gate, and the employment decision waits until all three are done. Write your household floor as one number. Divide the savings you could actually use by it, to get months. Read the side-activities and non-compete clauses in your contract, and if either would bite, book an hour with an employment lawyer first. If you are only testing an offer, these can wait until the commitment is on the table.

If you have another hour:

  1. List everything you currently count as encouraging, then cross out every item that is not money from someone who could have said no. Keep the crossed-out list; it will grow, and its growth will feel like progress.
  2. Tell one person the rule, someone who will not let you be vague. You will renegotiate it silently and find that harder to do out loud.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Cydoc's account is Draelos's own, written months after the shutdown, which makes it weaker evidence than a document produced at the time. It took outside money from people who knew her, which changes the pressure. She also reports a major personal health event in this period, so the calculation was not the only reason for the closure, and the account does not show that those economics held throughout the seven years. It is used because post-mortems this specific are rare.

Galperin's is also a later retelling. He improvised the four-day job under pressure. The case shows the menu has to be wider than stop; it does not show that choosing the consequence in advance was necessary, or that the salary was what kept Gymdesk alive.

McKenzie's figures were published at the time, but his 2010 review restates 2009 as $31,855.08 in sales and $16,369.80 in profit: a small business's single figure is provisional until you know when the books closed. His patience was also cheap for him partly because he was twenty-six; four years of a finite career is a different price at forty-five.

Still owed: an owner who wrote all three parts, missed the number, honoured the consequence, and should not have because the market was there; and someone who left only after repeat paid revenue covered their floor, then failed because leaving itself damaged the business. The standard for a business that cannot earn without you is reasoning, with no case behind it. None has been found in a checkable record, so the debt stands.

Closing Part I · Is there anything here?

observation → conversation → payment → a rule → the next commitment

Before going on to Will someone pay?, you should be able to point at these. Not feel them. Point at them.

  1. A problem you have observed, and the commitment it would justify. Chapter one.
  2. Accounts of recent behaviour from people who could comfortably have told you no, and at least one costly commitment from one of them. Chapter two.
  3. A separate answer to whether that commitment can be delivered, at that price, by that date. The same chapter.
  4. A written number, date and consequence for your next commitment, including, if it is next, your salary. This chapter.

All of it concerns one sale, or a handful. You still do not know whether customers can be found repeatedly, what they should be charged, or whether the economics survive doing it a hundred times. Those are the questions of Parts II and III, and they are where most of the businesses that clear Part I still die.

On Dutch ground · what your employment lets you do

This section is for readers employed in the Netherlands. Three questions have statutory answers, and all three are commonly got wrong in both directions — by employers who overstate their rights and by employees who assume they have none.

Can your employer forbid you from building it? Article 7:653a of the Civil Code says a clause by which the employer prohibits or restricts the employee from performing work for others outside working hours is void — "is nietig" — unless the clause can be justified on the ground of an objective reason. The burden sits with the employer, and it sits there at the moment they invoke it. Note that the statute never uses the word nevenwerkzaamheden, which is the term you will meet in every article written about it.

Can a non-compete stop you afterwards? Article 7:653 makes such a clause valid only where the contract is for an indefinite term ("de arbeidsovereenkomst voor onbepaalde tijd is aangegaan") and it was agreed in writing with an adult employee. In a fixed-term contract it is permitted only where the employer's written motivation shows it is necessary on grounds of "zwaarwegende bedrijfs- of dienstbelangen". A court may annul such a clause in whole or in part.

What does resigning do to unemployment benefit? Under article 24 of the Werkloosheidswet, an employee is culpably unemployed where the employment ended by or at their own request ("door of op verzoek van de werknemer") without continuation being something that could not reasonably be expected of them. Resigning to start a business is a choice, and you should plan on the basis that it carries no safety net.

None of this is advice, and the general rules have exceptions that are exactly the sort that get expensive. Before you hand in notice, pay an arbeidsrechtadvocaat for an hour to read your actual contract, and ask your accountant about the structure and the insurances you will be buying for yourself. An hour is cheap relative to the mortgage.
