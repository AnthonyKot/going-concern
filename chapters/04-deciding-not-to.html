Deciding Not To

Nobody plans to spend seven years on something that was never going to work. They plan to reassess in six months, and then they do reassess, and the reassessment says continue.

The decision What evidence, specified now, would make you stop — and what "stop" is going to mean when the day arrives and you do not want to.

Move 1 The situation

You are going to be a worse judge of this project later than you are today. That is the whole problem, and it is not a character flaw you can resolve by being unusually honest with yourself.

Today you have spent nothing. In eighteen months you will have spent money, evenings, conversations in which you told people what you were doing, and some portion of your sense of yourself as a person who finishes things. Every one of those is a reason to continue that has nothing to do with whether the business works, and all of them will be present in the room when you sit down to reassess objectively.

So the stopping rule is not an act of pessimism. It is a message to a version of you who will be worse-informed about the only question that matters and much better-informed about how much has been spent. Writing it now is the single cheapest thing in this book: it costs one evening, it can be done before any money moves, and it is the only decision in Part I you can make while genuinely disinterested.

The hard part is not the discipline. It is knowing what to put in it — because the thing that kills people here is not weakness, it is that by month twenty they have accumulated a genuinely impressive pile of evidence that means nothing.

Move 2 What actually happened

Cydoc — Rachel Draelos, April 2018 – August 2025

Rachel Draelos is an MD/PhD. She started Cydoc to automate the taking of patient histories, intending it as the first piece of a much larger vision, funded initially by nothing but — in her words — "my $35,000 annual MD/PhD stipend." The plan was that profits from the first product would fund the rest.

Over the following seven years the project accumulated the following. A HIPAA-compliant platform on web, Android and iOS, deployed in multiple practices across multiple specialties. A study run with eighteen medical students, not practising doctors, showing the system "saved 11 minutes per visit" — with real customers later reporting similar savings of around ten. Two U.S. patents. A competitive NC IDEA grant. Four paying customers. Four-figure recurring revenue and five-figure custom development revenue.

She calls these, in her own post-mortem, "what many consider to be validation milestones: paying customers, patents, and demonstrated clinical impact."

Along the way she drained her savings, converted to a Delaware corporation, and between 2023 and early 2024 raised $265,000 from family, friends and physicians. She pitched "over 35 different VC firms, but they were not interested in funding a solo founder without any revenue."

Then she did the arithmetic that the seven years of milestones had never required her to do. The small practices she was targeting "wanted to pay fewer than $100 per month." Hosting and AI costs had risen to "about $70 per doctor." Reaching those practices meant integrating with their electronic health record systems, and "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000" — with almost every customer needing their own. Her conclusion: "With revenue only $30 above hosting costs, and $4,000 to $6,000 per EHR integration, it would take 11 years per practice just to break even, not even factoring in ongoing personnel costs."

She shut Cydoc down in August 2025.

Two clarifications before drawing anything from this. Cydoc is not purely a self-funded business of the kind this book is otherwise about — it took outside money, from people who knew her, which changes both the pressure and the obligations. And this account is Draelos's own, written months after the shutdown, which makes it weaker evidence than a document produced at the time. It is used here because post-mortems this specific are rare, and because she is unusually hard on herself in a genre that mostly is not.

Now look at the list again, because it is the most important list in this chapter.

Patents. A peer-reviewable clinical result. Deployments in real practices. A grant awarded competitively. Four customers who actually paid. Any one of those, arriving in month fourteen, would feel like the thing you had been waiting for — and a stopping rule written in ordinary language, something like "if there is no sign of traction by next summer, I stop", is satisfied by every one of them, again and again, for years.

None of them answered the question the business needed answered, which was not whether the product worked or whether anyone valued it — both were settled — but whether Cydoc could repeatedly find practices willing to pay a price that covered acquiring, integrating and serving them. Four customers proved the first two. Thirty dollars a month against a four-thousand-dollar integration answered the third, and it took seven years to ask. Draelos identifies the substitution herself, and her prescription is one sentence long: "ask them to buy it before you build it." What she found when she finally did was that "doctors would often say that they liked our product, but if I asked them to invest or subscribe, most made excuses. This is a red flag."

Move 3 The reasoning

A stopping rule fails for one of two reasons. Either it is never written, or it is written in a currency that the project can pay in without ever becoming a business.

The second is the interesting failure, because it looks like rigour. Traction, progress, momentum, validation — every one of these is satisfiable by something real that is not the thing you need to know.

And the signals are not worthless. That is what makes them dangerous. A patent is evidence of novelty, not of demand. A study is evidence of efficacy, not of willingness to pay. A grant is evidence that a panel found the proposal fundable, not that customers will fund the business. A pilot proves somebody will try it; it does not prove that enough people will buy it again at a price that works. Each is genuine evidence, correctly answering a question you did not need answered yet — which is precisely why collecting them feels like progress and reads, in a loosely-worded stopping rule, as a pass.

So a usable stopping rule has exactly three components, and the third is the one everybody omits:

1. A metric denominated in money from independent customers

Not interest, not pilots, not letters of intent, not users, not press. Money that has cleared, from customers who could comfortably have said no, at a price and scope you could plausibly repeat.

Each clause is load-bearing. Could comfortably have said no rules out the purchase made because refusing you would have been awkward — Cydoc's four customers came, in Draelos's account, "one through a cold call, one through a cold email, one through a very warm intro (the endocrinologist: he was my husband's uncle), and one via a consulting network", and only some of those are evidence about a market. At a price and scope you could plausibly repeat rules out the bespoke project: five-figure custom development revenue is real money and tells you almost nothing about whether a product will sell, which is a distinction chapter ten returns to. Note that a former colleague at arm's length can be better evidence than a stranger on a discounted one-off. The test is independence, not unfamiliarity.

2. A date

Fixed in advance, in the calendar, not "when I have had a proper go at it." The date is what converts an open-ended endeavour into a bounded experiment, and it is the part your future self will most want to renegotiate. Expect to want to move it. That wanting is data.

3. What failing it means

This is the component that makes the difference between a rule you keep and a rule you break, and almost nobody writes it. Stop is not the only available consequence, and treating it as the only one is why so many people quietly decline to set a rule at all — because the stakes of the rule become total, and nobody wants to pre-commit to demolishing something they love.

The honest menu is wider: stop entirely; mothball it and keep the domain; cut the scope to the one part that showed signs of life; hand it to somebody else; or go back to a salary and continue at reduced speed.

Two claims live in that sentence and they are not equally supported, so take them apart. That the menu has to be wider than stop is demonstrated by the block below, where the last option on the list is the one that saved a business. That you should decide which one now, rather than in the room on the day, is a different claim and a smaller one. The block below does not prove it — the man in it improvised, and it worked. What the block does show is the alternative in operation: a rule with no written consequence does not remove the decision, it defers it to the worst available moment and hands it to the most compromised available judge. Deciding now does not make you righter. It makes the rule easier to honour than to renegotiate, which is all this chapter needs it to do.

Boundaries

Two, and they are serious enough that the rest of the chapter should be read through them.

A stopping rule measures the business and the operator at the same time, and cannot tell them apart. When the date arrives and the revenue is not there, you are looking at a single number produced by two different things: whether the market wants this, and whether you have yet learned the parts of the job you did not know at the start — usually sales and distribution. The rule cannot distinguish them. It will read the second as the first.

The rule is only as good as the evidence you gathered, not the evidence available. Draelos spent an additional year after deciding who her customer was before she spoke to one: "An entire additional year elapsed before I talked to my first real potential customers." A threshold you fail because you never ran the test tells you nothing about the market. It tells you about your calendar, and stopping on it would have been the wrong conclusion drawn from the right rule.

Monday

  1. Write one sentence with a number, a date, and a consequence. The template: if fewer than [N] strangers have paid me [amount] by [date], I will [specific action]. If you cannot fill all four slots, you do not have a rule, and the missing slot is the one you are avoiding.
  2. Choose the consequence from the full menu, not from "stop or don't." Stop, mothball, cut scope to the one piece that worked, hand it on, or return to a salary and continue slowly. Pick one now. Picking now is what makes it possible to honour later.
  3. List everything you would currently count as encouraging — the pilots, the compliments, the introductions, the awards, the sign-ups. Then draw a line through every item that is not money from a stranger. Keep the crossed-out list. In eighteen months it will have grown, and its growth will feel like progress.
  4. Tell one person the rule. Not for accountability theatre — for the specific reason that you will renegotiate it silently and will find it harder to renegotiate out loud. Choose someone who will not be kind about it.
  5. Put the date in your calendar now, with the sentence pasted into the invitation. Your future self should not have to go looking for what you decided, because your future self will not look.

Gymdesk — a rule that failed because it was badly written

Eran Galperin set himself a bounded period with a resource limit attached: by his own later account, he hoped "to reach enough revenue within the 6 months runway I had built." A date, then, and a ceiling on what he was prepared to spend getting there.

Enough revenue is not a number; it is a threshold he could argue himself past. The consequence was not written down either.

The six months passed and the answer was no. Not marginally: he had begun with no customers, and revenue only started some months in. He did not stop. He took contract work, then a salaried job on the condition that he work four days a week, keeping three for the business. It reached $10k in monthly recurring revenue by the end of 2018, and he left employment again in June 2019. By his account: "I was wrong. In reality it took 3 whole years for the business to grow to the point it could provide for me full time."

The result supports a wider menu of consequences: retreating to salary and continuing slowly saved the business. It does not show that choosing the consequence in advance was necessary; Galperin improvised under pressure and chose well. Writing it earlier would not have made him righter, only made the rule easier to honour than to renegotiate. Chapter five is about the other end of the same decision.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be an owner who wrote all three components — a money threshold from independent customers, a date and a consequence — missed it, honoured the consequence, and should not have because the market was there. Gymdesk did not follow that complete rule. No qualifying case has been found, and the shortfall is recorded.
