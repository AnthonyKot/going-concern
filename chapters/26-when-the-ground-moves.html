When the Ground Moves

In early 2020 Gymdesk, gym-management software run by one person, was having its best months: "consistent $1k+ MRR growth every month, which we have never experienced before." Then "almost all gyms were closed for months", and its customers began asking to "freeze or cancel". Nothing the business did caused it. When the ground moves under your customers, what do you do first?

The decision Whether to read an external shock as a change in your own business, and cut, discount or wait, or as a change in what your customers can now do, and sell to them as they now are; and how to tell, months later, what the shock actually cost.

Gymdesk: a market closed by decree

Gymdesk — Eran Galperin, 2020–2021

Eran Galperin's company appeared in chapter four and chapter nineteen. His 2023 account of 2020 starts with a business accelerating after a relaunch: "We had extremely strong trial-to-paid-account conversion rates, at around 40%." Then: "COVID hit the fitness industry hard – almost all gyms were closed for months, and then reopened slowly with very restrictive guidelines for in-person and indoor training."

It reached the software through the gyms: "As a business that services that industry, we were likewise impacted. Users were reaching out to freeze or cancel their accounts. We had significant churn from March to around August." He gives no churn rate. By June, "things were starting to stabilize".

There was almost nothing to cut: until June 2021 this was "a one-man, owner run business". So he built, for gyms as they now were: "We kept adapting to the new environment, and added many features to support our users during those times – from booking functionality with capacity restrictions, COVID vaccination checks, online training features and more." A booking limit, a check at the door, a class for people who cannot come to the room.

The year ended "having grown our MRR by 60% to around $19.5k", and 2021 "strongly, having grown our MRR by around x2.5 compared to 2020." His own reading is offered as a belief: "I strongly believe our ability to adapt during this period of uncertainty laid the foundation for future success." Gyms also reopened, whatever their software could do. The account shows the order of what he did, not what caused the recovery.

Read it as your customers' event first

When the shock lands, the signals you watch move in a week: cancellations, pause requests, people simply not coming. The instinct is to act on your own business, because that is what you can reach: cut a cost, drop the price, wait. Any of those may turn out right, but none of them is a reading of what happened. The shock arrived as a change in what your customers can do, open, trade, travel, spend, and your revenue is its shadow, a few weeks behind.

So the first question is three columns, one line per kind of customer: what can they no longer do, what can they still do, and what do they now need that they did not need last month? Gymdesk's features are the third column for a gym under restriction. A restaurant in the same spring had the same first column, nobody may sit down, and its third was collection at the door and dishes that survive a twenty-minute drive. The café whose office customers are at home may find they still eat lunch; the plumber whose clients will not let anyone in may find their houses still leak. Whether what they now need is smaller, nearer or cheaper is something to ask, not assume, and in chapter two's tense: what they did last week, what they are doing instead, and what that is costing them.

The cut waits for the reading, because a shock arrives as an announcement and is paid for over months. A cut in week one is priced on the announcement. Put the receipts you still expect, dated as the cancellations and pauses now leave them, into chapter thirteen's forecast, with every payment still due on its date. The day the balance would cross your floor is your reserve, and it is a number, not a feeling in week one.

Offer the pause before they cancel, and count the cost later

The customers asked to "freeze or cancel", and the difference between those words is the difference between a customer paused and a customer lost. The account says what they asked for, not what Gymdesk offered. The rule this book takes from it is its own proposal: a customer who cannot use what you sell will leave unless leaving is not the only way out. A pause, dated, cheap and easy to reverse, keeps the relationship. Test it in the forecast first, and be exact about what a pause does to money. A free freeze does not postpone the paused months' payments; it waives them. So take those payments out altogether, restart each customer's normal billing only from the date you assume they return, and keep the cost of holding their service ready. Then run the worse cases separately: they come back a month later than assumed, or some never come back. Check it against what you agreed with them, in chapter twenty-four's record. If the low point still clears your floor, offer it before it is asked for.

Then measure the shock when it is over, not when it hits. The March figure is the announcement. The cost is which customers came back when they could and which did not, taken from chapter ten's customer record months later, with their reasons in their own words. Gymdesk's account gives year-end revenue, not how many of March's customers returned; a business that keeps the record can know.

Not every shock lifts. The road that stays closed and the regulation that stays are a different event, and the reading may say so. Then the question is no longer how to hold on until it passes, but whether this business, as the ground now is, still gives your household what it needs. Chapter twenty-eight is about that decision.

Monday · three columns for each kind of customer

Do this one thing. For each kind of customer you have, write one line in three columns: what they can no longer do, what they can still do, and what they now need that they did not need before. Use a shock that has happened, or the one you most fear: the platform that sends you customers changing its rules, the road outside closing, your largest customer's budget frozen. You now hold the reading that should come before any cut.

The third column is empty? That is the usual answer at first. Book one conversation with a customer this week and ask, in the past tense, what they did last week and what they are doing instead. Booking it counts as finishing.

If you have another hour:

  1. Find your reserve date. Extend your dated forecast with the receipts you would still expect and every payment still due, and circle the day it crosses your floor.
  2. Test a pause in the forecast: remove the waived payments, restart billing at the assumed return date, try a later return and a non-return, check it against your agreements, and write the offer you would make, with a return date on it.
  3. Put a date six months out to count who came back, who did not, and why.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Gymdesk's is a founder's own account, written three years after the events. No churn rate is given and none is derived here; “March to around August” is his recollection; the 60% and 2.5× figures are self-reported monthly revenue. The features are not dated in the account, and the vaccination check cannot have been a 2020 feature. The account reports adaptation and recovery together and does not test whether one produced the other. The restaurant, café and plumber are this book's translations, and the pause is this book's proposal.

Still owed: a business that read the shock as its customers' event, offered the pause, built for customers as they now were and held its reserve to the date the reading gave, and did not come through, because the ground did not settle in time or the customers who paused had somewhere else to go. This is one survivor's account of one shock that lifted; the vendor to the same gyms that did not survive has left no post. The debt stands.
