When the Ground Moves

Nothing you did caused it. The road outside is dug up for a year; the platform that sent you half your customers changed a rule; a regulation arrived; the anchor tenant next door closed; a virus emptied every building your customers work in. The shock is not yours, and the first mistake is to treat it as if it were — as something the business did wrong and can correct. It is something that happened to your customers, and it reaches you through them.

The decision Whether to read an external shock as a change in your own business — and respond by cutting, discounting, waiting — or as a change in what your customers can now do, and respond by selling to them as they now are; and how to tell, months later, what the shock actually cost, which is not the month it hit.

Move 1 The situation

The figures from chapter twenty-two moved in a week, and not because of anything on your side of the counter. Customers are cancelling, or asking to pause, or simply not coming, and when you ask why the answer is the same from all of them and has nothing to do with you. The instinct is to act on your own business, because that is the only thing you can act on: cut the cost that chapter twenty-three told you to hold, drop the price, wait for it to pass.

Each of those may turn out to be right. None of them is a reading of what has happened, and a response before a reading is a guess. The shock arrived as a change in what your customers can do — open, trade, travel, spend — and your revenue is the shadow of that change, a few weeks behind it. So the first question is not what do we cut. It is what can our customers no longer do, what can they still do, and what do they now need that they did not need last month — because whatever the answer, it is the only thing you will be selling to until the ground settles.

Move 2 What actually happened

Gymdesk's customers are gyms and martial-arts studios: rooms that people go to. Eran Galperin's account of 2020, written in May 2023, gave chapter nineteen one sentence on the shock and a year-end figure, on its way to the hires that came after. The paragraphs around that sentence are the shock itself, and they start with a business accelerating. Having relaunched with a new interface at the beginning of the year, "The first few months of 2020 were going splendidly – we saw consistent $1k+ MRR growth every month, which we have never experienced before. We had extremely strong trial-to-paid-account conversion rates, at around 40%."

Then, in his words, "COVID hit the fitness industry hard – almost all gyms were closed for months, and then reopened slowly with very restrictive guidelines for in-person and indoor training." The shock reached the software company through its customers: "As a business that services that industry, we were likewise impacted. Users were reaching out to freeze or cancel their accounts. We had significant churn from March to around August." No rate is given for that churn. What the account gives is the span and the shape: customers who could not open, asking either to pause or to leave. And, in the same paragraph, the founder's view from inside it: "Yet, things were starting to stabilize around June, and it seemed like we would manage through this unexpected turn of events." Only then does the account turn to what the company built.

What the account says Gymdesk did next is build, and the post is silent on everything else — there was, by the same document, almost nothing to cut: the first employee came in June 2021, and until then this was "a one-man, owner run business". It built for gyms as they now were: "We kept adapting to the new environment, and added many features to support our users during those times – from booking functionality with capacity restrictions, COVID vaccination checks, online training features and more." A booking system with a headcount limit, a check at the door, a class for people who cannot come to the room: features for a gym under restriction, none of them dated in the account, and the vaccination check cannot have been a 2020 feature at all.

The year ended: "We ended 2020 on a pretty good pace still, having grown our MRR by 60% to around $19.5k, and maintaining strong conversion rates from trials to paying users." The next year, with "most people … starting to get used to this new reality, and gyms … following suit": "We ended 2021 strongly, having grown our MRR by around x2.5 compared to 2020." Vaccines, in his account, were "starting to become available" as that year closed, and gyms "opening again, or scaling up their operations" is his description of 2022.

The account reports the building and the recovery side by side and does not test whether the one produced the other; gyms reopened whether or not their software had a capacity limit, and Galperin's own reading of the sequence is stated as a belief: "I strongly believe our ability to adapt during this period of uncertainty laid the foundation for future success." What the case shows is a business whose entire market was closed by decree, that lost customers to it and can say roughly when, that built for the customers it still had as they now were, and that ended the year larger than it began it — and which of those produced which, it cannot say.

Move 3 The reasoning

Read the shock as your customers' event first

An external shock reaches you through what your customers can no longer do, and the response is to find out what they can still do and now need, and sell that — before any decision about your own costs. Gymdesk's list is the concrete form: capacity limits, checks, online classes were the shape of a gym under restriction, and a vendor still selling the shape of a gym in February 2020 would have had nothing to sell — which is this book's reading of the list, not a claim the account makes. The same move should exist for a business with no software in it, and here the book is guessing on your behalf: the café whose office customers are at home may find they still eat lunch; the tradesman whose clients cannot let anyone in may find their houses still break. Whether what they now need is smaller, nearer or cheaper than before is the thing to ask, not to assume, and it is on offer only to a business that asked. The asking is the same instrument as chapter two's, run again on ground that has moved, and in the same tense: what they did last week, what they are doing instead, and what that is costing them — the workaround with money in it is where the thing you can sell now is.

The reason the question comes before the cut is timing. A shock arrives as an announcement and is paid for over months, and a cut made in the first week is priced on the announcement. Chapter twenty-three held reserve in hours; a demand shock draws on the other kind, money, and the buffer that chapter left to a later one is drawn on here. Gymdesk had no reserve of either kind to hold in 2020 except its owner, and the post shows that one being spent — the features were built by the same person who was answering the freeze requests. That is the order the case shows, and the rule that the cut waits for the reading is this book's, not his. It comes when the reading says what your customers can now do is less than the business needs, for longer than the reserve lasts, and that is a number, not a feeling in week one.

Freeze before cancel, and count the shock later

The customers asked "to freeze or cancel", and the difference between those two words is the difference between a customer paused and a customer lost. The account does not say what Gymdesk offered them; it says what they asked for. The rule this book takes from that word is the book's: a customer who cannot use what you sell will leave unless leaving is not the only exit, and a pause — dated, cheap, easy to reverse — is the exit that keeps the relationship the record in chapter eleven was built to hold. Offer it before it is asked for.

Then measure the shock when it is over, not when it hits. The March figure is the announcement; the cost is which customers came back when they could and which did not, and that is a reading taken from the record months later. Gymdesk's account gives the year-end number and the following year's; it does not give the share of the March customers who returned, and a business that keeps chapter eleven's columns can. That figure — returned, not returned, and the reason in their words — is what the shock cost, and it is the only version of the event worth planning the next one against.

The boundary is the shock that does not lift. This is one account of one that did; the road that stays closed, the regulation that stays, is a different event, and the reading may say so. When it does, the sentence you wrote in chapter sixteen is the one that decides — the run length, the date, the consequence — and only if that consequence is the last of its four does chapter twenty-eight open. And the recovery is reported, not explained: what the case gives is the order of the response and the shape of the features, not a promise that the same order produces the same year.

Monday

  1. Write the shock as it hits your customers, not you. One line per kind of customer: what they can no longer do, what they still can, and what they now need that they did not last month. If the third column is empty for everyone, ask five of them this week — chapter two's five questions, past tense, on the changed ground; what would you pay to have it back is not one of them.
  2. Offer the pause before it is asked for. A dated freeze, at no cost or a nominal one, with a return date written on it — to every customer who could ask to cancel. Record who took it and who left anyway; that split is the first reading of the shock.
  3. Build the smallest thing in the third column, within a month. Not the product you would have built anyway — the thing the changed customer needs now, in the form they can use now. Put a date on it, and sell it to the customers on the pause first.
  4. Read the reserve against the churned level, not the old one. Take chapter thirteen's cash — the bank balance less what is held for the tax authority — and divide it by the gap between a month's outgoings, counted as chapter sixteen counted them, and this month's takings. The answer is months. Write the date that number gives you. The cut, if it comes, comes on that date on the reading, not in week one on the fear.
  5. Date the count of what came back. Six months on, or when the ground has visibly settled, go through chapter eleven's record: of the customers who paused or left in the shock, who returned, who did not, and what they said. That is the cost of the shock, and it is the number the next one is planned against.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is the business that did this — read the shock as its customers' event, offered the pause, built for customers as they now were, held the reserve to the date the reading gave — and did not come through, because the ground did not settle in time, or the customers who paused had somewhere else to go when they returned, or what they now needed was something the business could not make. That is the honest counter-case and it is the natural shape of the missing evidence: the account above is written by the founder of the company that survived, and the vendor to the same gyms that did not survive has left no post. The nearer debt is the survivorship itself — one account, one vertical, one shock that lifted — and the chapter carries it by claiming only the order of the response and none of the result.

The account reports adaptation and recovery together and does not test whether one produced the other; no account from a comparable vendor that did not recover was found, and the chapter treats the case as one survivor's sequence, not as a result.
