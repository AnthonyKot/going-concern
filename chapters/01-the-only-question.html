Part I · Is there anything here? · Chapter 1

The Only Question That Matters

Every business book eventually says that you cannot create demand, only find it. This is true, and stated that way it is nearly useless — because the businesses that die of it usually had the demand.

The decision Whether to commit money to building the thing, before you know that enough people want it at a price that covers what it costs you to make.

Move 1 The situation

You have an idea you cannot stop thinking about, and the thinking has reached the stage where it costs money to continue. Not much, at first. A deposit on a unit. A machine. Three months of your own time, which you have stopped counting because counting it is discouraging.

Everyone you have described it to thinks it is a good idea. This is the least informative sentence in this book, and we will come back to it in chapter two.

The advice you will receive at this point, from every direction, is that you should validate demand before you build. It is correct advice. It is also, in the crude form in which it is almost always given, a piece of advice you can follow scrupulously and still lose everything — because it treats demand as a fact about the world, a yes or a no, when it is actually a relationship between three things you control none of individually:

  • how many people want it,
  • what they will pay,
  • and what it costs you to serve them at that volume.

Move any one of those and the answer changes. A market that is real at ten thousand customers is imaginary at four hundred. A product people genuinely want at £40 does not exist at £95. The question is never is there demand. The question is whether there is demand in the shape your cost structure requires, and the second question is much harder to ask, because it requires you to know your cost structure before you have built anything.

What follows is the most expensive illustration available of the difference between the two questions.

Move 2 What actually happened

Webvan — Foster City, California, 1996–2001

Louis Borders, who had co-founded the Borders bookshop chain, started an online grocery company in 1996 on a premise that was not stupid and has since been proved correct: people would rather not go to the supermarket.

The bet was on automation. Rather than picking orders from shop shelves, Webvan built purpose-designed distribution centres — each one intended to hold around 50,000 product lines and fill roughly 8,000 orders a day, enough to generate on the order of $100 million in annual revenue from a single building. In 1999 the company signed a contract with the engineering firm Bechtel, reported at around $1 billion, to build them out across the United States at roughly $30 million apiece. Webvan went public in November 1999 and raised $375 million.

Then the orders arrived, and there were not enough of them.

Webvan told the SEC that as of 31 December 1999, its first distribution centre — Oakland, the flagship, the proof of the model — was operating at less than 25% of the capacity for which it was designed. Nine months later, as of 30 September 2000, it had climbed to less than 40%. That is the whole story, disclosed by the company in its own filings, in two sentences.

The Bay Area business was running at roughly 2,350 orders a day at an average order size of about $105. Cash-flow breakeven for the facility required somewhere between 3,300 and 3,500 orders a day at about $110. Webvan filed for Chapter 11 in July 2001, having consumed something on the order of $1.2 billion.

Webvan was not off by an order of magnitude. It was running at about seven-tenths of the volume it needed to break even. Thousands of households in the Bay Area were, every single day, doing the thing the company had been founded on the belief that they would do. The demand was there. It was simply not there in the quantity required by a building designed to fill 8,000 orders a day, which had been commissioned before anyone knew what the real number would be.

The company did not fail because it was wrong about people wanting groceries delivered. It failed because it had converted an unproven demand estimate into concrete, steel and a billion-dollar construction contract, and concrete does not scale downward. A business with a lighter cost structure would have looked at 2,350 orders a day and seen a going concern. Webvan looked at it and saw a shortfall of a thousand orders a day it had already paid for.

Move 3 The reasoning

The principle underneath all of this is usually called the iron law of the market, and it says that no quality of execution will save a business selling something nobody wants. That is true. Keep it. It disposes of a large category of doomed ideas at no cost, and if you take nothing else from this book, take the habit of asking the question before rather than after.

But state it in its useful form, which has a second clause:

Demand must exist — and it must exist at a volume and price that the cost structure you are about to commit to can survive.

The first clause protects you from building something nobody wants. The second protects you from the much more common and much more painful failure, which is building the right thing on a cost base that requires a level of demand you have not yet demonstrated. The graveyard is fuller of the second kind, and they are sadder, because they were nearly right.

Three consequences follow, and they are the working content of this chapter.

Your cost structure is a demand forecast in disguise

When you sign a five-year lease, hire your second employee, or buy a machine that only pays for itself at a certain throughput, you are not making an operational decision. You are making a quantitative prediction about demand and putting money behind it, usually without noticing that you have done so. Every fixed cost you take on has a number hidden inside it: the volume at which it stops being a mistake.

Most owners could not state that number for their largest fixed cost. It is worth ten minutes to find out, because it converts a vague anxiety into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic problems can be solved.

Variable beats fixed until you know the number

Before you have real demand data, the correct instinct is to convert as much as possible of your cost base from fixed to variable, even when the variable option is more expensive per unit. Renting is worse than owning at scale and better than owning at uncertainty. Contractors cost more per hour than employees and can be stopped. A worse per-unit economics that you can switch off is, in the pre-evidence phase, strictly superior to a better one you cannot.

This inverts once you know the number. After demand is demonstrated and stable, the fixed-cost version is usually the one that survives competition, and an owner who stays variable forever is leaving margin on the table for someone else to take. The trap is not fixed costs. The trap is taking them on in the wrong order.

Boundaries of the principle

Two, and they matter.

The first: some businesses genuinely cannot start small. You cannot build a quarter of a steel mill or license half a pharmacy, and there are real markets where the minimum viable physical footprint is large. If you are in one of those, this chapter's advice does not soften; it hardens. The correct response is not to build the small version — there isn't one — but to demand far more evidence before committing, and to secure the demand contractually before the capital goes in rather than after. Which is chapter three.

The second, and the one that catches thoughtful people: demand can be real and simply early. Webvan's market existed in 1999. It did not exist at 1999's cost of labour, smartphone penetration, or consumer habit. Being ten years early is indistinguishable, from inside the business, from being wrong — the spreadsheet looks the same, the bank balance looks the same, and the market research says people want it, which they do. This is why the second clause is stated in terms of your cost structure rather than in terms of the market. You cannot control when a market arrives. You can control whether you are still solvent when it does.

Monday

  1. Write down your breakeven in units, not money. Take your fixed monthly costs, divide by the contribution you make on one sale — price minus what that one sale costs you to deliver. The result is the number of sales a month at which you stop losing money. Most owners have never calculated it. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the single most useful number in the business.
  2. Compare that number to your best month so far. Not your average, your best. If your best month is below breakeven, you do not have a marketing problem, you have a cost-structure problem, and marketing spend will make it worse.
  3. List every fixed commitment over twelve months — leases, contracts, salaried people, financed equipment — and write next to each one the monthly volume that justifies it. Any commitment whose number you cannot state is a demand forecast you made without meaning to.
  4. Find the one fixed cost you could make variable this quarter and price the switch. If demand is not yet demonstrated, pay the premium.
  5. Set the number that would make you stop. Write it down now, while you are calm, with a date attached. Deciding in advance what evidence would change your mind is the cheapest insurance in this book, and chapter five is entirely about it.

The counter-case: Instacart — the same demand, a decade later

Apply this chapter's advice to Webvan in 1998 and it tells you to proceed. Ask whether people want groceries delivered, and the answer was yes, and it was verifiable, and it was correct.

Instacart, founded in 2012, went after the identical demand and did not build a single warehouse. It used the shops that already existed and paid people to walk around them. In 2022 it handled 262.6 million orders — around 720,000 a day — on gross transaction value of $28.8 billion, and posted its first annual net profit, $428 million, of which $358 million was a tax benefit rather than operations. That last clause matters: even the winner took a decade to make money, and the year it did, most of the profit was an accounting event.

So the demand Webvan bet on was real, and the market eventually reached three hundred times the daily volume of a single Webvan distribution centre. Webvan was not wrong about the world. It was wrong about the calendar, and it had signed a billion-dollar construction contract that left it no way to be wrong about the calendar cheaply.

Which is the uncomfortable thing this chapter has to leave you with. Validating demand is necessary and it is not sufficient, and the residue that validation cannot remove is timing. What you can do about timing is not predict it. It is stay cheap enough to still be there.
