The Only Question That Matters

Every business book eventually says that you cannot create demand, only find it. This is true, and stated that way it is nearly useless — because a business can die of this while holding the very demand it bet on. Webvan did.

The decision Whether to commit money to building the thing, before you know that enough people want it at a price that covers what it costs you to make.

Move 1 The situation

You have an idea you cannot stop thinking about, and the thinking has reached the stage where it costs money to continue. Not much, at first. A deposit on a unit. A machine. Three months of your own time, which you have stopped counting because counting it is discouraging.

Everyone you have described it to thinks it is a good idea. This is the least informative sentence in this book, and we will come back to it in chapter two.

The advice you will receive at this point, from every direction, is that you should validate demand before you build. It is correct advice. It is also, in the crude form in which it is almost always given, a piece of advice you can follow scrupulously and still lose everything — because it treats demand as a fact about the world, a yes or a no, when it is actually a relationship between three quantities that move independently of one another:

  • how many people want it,
  • what they will pay,
  • and what it costs you to serve them at that volume.

Move any one of those and the answer changes. A market that is real at ten thousand customers is imaginary at four hundred. A product people genuinely want at £40 does not exist at £95. The question is never is there demand. The question is whether there is demand in the shape your cost structure requires, and the second question is much harder to ask, because it requires you to know your cost structure before you have built anything.

What follows is the most expensive illustration available of the difference between the two questions.

Move 2 What actually happened

Webvan — Foster City, California, 1996–2001

Louis H. Borders, who founded Borders Books in 1971, founded Webvan in December 1996 on a premise that was not stupid and has since been proved correct: people would rather not go to the supermarket.

The bet was on automation. Rather than picking orders off shop shelves, Webvan built purpose-designed distribution centres. Each was sized, in the company's own words, "to process product volumes equivalent to approximately 18 supermarkets" — a designed capacity of 8,000 orders per day. At that volume, with an average order of $103, Webvan told investors a centre would run at a 12% operating margin and generate $300 million a year. One building, doing the work of eighteen shops.

In July 1999 it signed an agreement with the engineering firm Bechtel to build up to 26 more of them over three years, a programme the company estimated at approximately $1.0 billion, with each centre costing $30–35 million.

Then the Webstore opened, on 2 June 1999, and the orders were not enough.

As of 31 December 1999 the Oakland centre — the flagship, the proof of the model — was running at less than 25% of the capacity for which it was designed. Nine months later, as of 30 September 2000, it had reached less than 40%. Both figures are Webvan's own disclosures to the SEC.

The order size problem the company could fix, and did. The average order in the fourth quarter of 1999 was $81.31, more than $20 short of the $103 the model required; by the fourth quarter of 2000 it had reached $112. What Webvan could not fix was volume. In the third quarter of 2000, after five full quarters of operation, the Oakland prototype still did not break even on a cash-flow basis, and the company estimated that order volume would have to rise "by a minimum of forty percent" from September's level to get there. It also disclosed, flatly, that "to date no Webvan facility has become profitable on a cash-flow basis."

The accumulated deficit ran $159.4 million at the end of 1999, $440 million by September 2000, and $612.7 million by December 2000 — a net loss of $453.3 million in that year alone. Webvan filed for Chapter 11 in July 2001.

Read those numbers again, because almost every retelling of this story gets the moral wrong.

Webvan was not off by an order of magnitude. It needed forty per cent more orders. Under 40% of eight thousand is still upwards of three thousand grocery deliveries a day, every day, in one metropolitan area, in the year 2000 — thousands of households doing precisely the thing the company had been founded on the belief that they would do. The demand was there. It was not there in the quantity required by a building sized for eighteen supermarkets, which had been designed before anyone knew what the real number would be.

And here the popular version of the story needs correcting, because the Bechtel agreement is usually presented as the fatal act — a billion dollars signed away on warehouses nobody wanted. The filings say otherwise. Webvan disclosed that it had "no obligation under the Bechtel agreement to build any distribution centers and, consequently, no capital commitment under the agreement until Webvan determines to proceed" with a particular site. By late 2000 it had no plans to build any more, and said so. The option was real and the company exercised it.

It died anyway — because optionality on the centres you have not built yet does nothing about the ones already standing. Webvan's error was not the contract for twenty-six warehouses. It was the first one: committing to a facility whose economics required eight thousand orders a day before a single customer had placed one. Everything after that was an attempt to grow demand up to a cost structure that had been fixed in advance — a race run on the cost structure's terms, against a deadline set by the bank balance.

Move 3 The reasoning

The principle underneath all of this is usually called the iron law of the market, and it says that no quality of execution will save a business selling something nobody wants. That is true. Keep it. It disposes of a large category of doomed ideas at no cost, and if you take nothing else from this book, take the habit of asking the question before rather than after.

But state it in its useful form, which has a second clause:

Demand must exist — and it must exist at a volume and price that the cost structure you are about to commit to can survive.

The first clause protects you from building something nobody wants. The second protects you from a different failure, and a harder one to see coming: building the right thing on a cost base that requires a level of demand you have not yet demonstrated. That failure is the subject of this chapter. It is difficult to recognise from inside because everything except the arithmetic looks like a business that is working.

Three consequences follow, and they are the working content of this chapter.

Your cost structure is a demand forecast in disguise

When you sign a five-year lease, hire your second employee, or buy a machine that only pays for itself at a certain throughput, you are not making an operational decision. You are making a quantitative prediction about demand and putting money behind it, usually without noticing that you have done so. Every fixed cost you take on has a number hidden inside it: the volume at which it stops being a mistake.

Most owners could not state that number for their largest fixed cost. It is worth ten minutes to find out, because it converts a vague anxiety into an arithmetic problem, and arithmetic problems can be solved.

Variable beats fixed until you know the number

Before you have real demand data, the correct instinct is to convert as much as possible of your cost base from fixed to variable, even when the variable option is more expensive per unit. Renting is worse than owning at scale and better than owning at uncertainty. Contractors cost more per hour than employees and can be stopped. A worse per-unit economics that you can switch off is, in the pre-evidence phase, usually worth the premium over a better one you cannot.

Usually, and not strictly superior, which is what an earlier draft said. The stronger phrase would claim variable wins in every state of the world, and the debt this chapter carries is precisely a state where it does not: a business that committed early to fixed capacity and took a market a variable competitor could not have served. No such case has been found here. That is a reason to keep looking, not a licence to promote the claim while nobody is checking. Worth the premium is a statement about the odds, which is all the evidence below supports.

This inverts once you know the number. After demand is demonstrated and stable, the fixed-cost version is often cheaper per unit, and an owner who stays variable long after the volume is proven may be leaving margin for a competitor to take. The trap is not fixed costs. The trap is taking them on in the wrong order.

Boundaries of the principle

Three, and they matter.

The first, and the one that keeps this chapter honest: execution does more to demand than "find it or don't" allows. It is not true that demand sits in the world as a fixed quantity waiting to be discovered. Repositioning an identical product for a different buyer can turn an indifferent market into an eager one. Removing friction — a form that takes two minutes instead of twenty, delivery on Thursday instead of "within three weeks" — routinely converts people who wanted the thing in principle into people who buy it. Naming a category can assemble a demand that was real but had no word for itself, and therefore no way to search for you. All of that is execution, and all of it changes the number.

What execution cannot do is make people value something they fundamentally do not want. That is the defensible version of the law, and it is much narrower than the version you will hear. Creating awareness of a product people would like if they knew about it is an ordinary, winnable job. Manufacturing a preference that is not there is not. Most businesses that believe themselves to be doing the first are doing the first; the reason to keep the distinction sharp is that the failure mode looks identical from inside for about eighteen months.

The second: some businesses genuinely cannot start small. You cannot build a quarter of a steel mill or license half a pharmacy, and there are real markets where the minimum viable physical footprint is large. If you are in one of those, this chapter's advice does not soften; it hardens. The correct response is not to build the small version — there isn't one — but to demand far more evidence before committing, and to secure the demand contractually before the capital goes in rather than after. Which is chapter three.

The third, and the one that catches thoughtful people: demand can be real and simply early. Webvan's market existed in 1999. It did not exist at 1999's cost of labour, smartphone penetration, or consumer habit. Being ten years early is indistinguishable, from inside the business, from being wrong — the spreadsheet looks the same, the bank balance looks the same, and the market research says people want it, which they do. This is why the second clause is stated in terms of your cost structure rather than in terms of the market. You cannot control when a market arrives. You can control whether you are still solvent when it does.

Monday

  1. Name the unit you are selling, and put a price on it. One thing: an hour, a month of access, a project, a box. Not a range. If you cannot write a single number down, you do not yet have an offer — you have an interest, and chapters six and nine exist for that.
  2. Work out what one unit actually leaves you. Price minus everything that sale drags in with it: payment fees, refunds, hosting, materials, and the hours of delivery and support it triggers, costed at what you would have to pay somebody else to do them. What remains is your contribution per unit, and it is almost always smaller than the first guess.
  3. Compute breakeven with your own pay inside it. Add your fixed monthly costs to the minimum you must take home — the mortgage, the insurance, the childcare, the number below which this stops being viable regardless of how the business is doing — and divide by contribution per unit. That is how many units a month you need. A breakeven that pays you nothing is not a breakeven; it is a hobby with an accountant.
  4. Hold that number against paid evidence, not compliments. Count only people who have given you money or signed something binding. Enthusiasm, mailing-list signups, and "I would definitely use that" all count as zero. If the honest count is zero, that is not a failure — it is the accurate starting position, and chapters two and three are about improving it.
  5. Write the stopping rule down, with a date and a number. Not "if it is not working by summer" — something you cannot argue with later, like: if fewer than eight customers have paid by 30 September, I do not sign the lease. Do it now, while you are calm and nothing is sunk. Then price the cancellable version of that same commitment: get the actual quote and the actual notice period this week, and see what the option to be wrong costs. Chapter four is entirely about this.

Aftermath: Instacart — the same idea, a decade later

The obvious question about Webvan is whether it was simply wrong about people wanting groceries delivered. It was not.

Instacart, founded in 2012, went after the same appetite and built no warehouses at all. It used the shops that already existed and paid people to walk around them. In 2022 it fulfilled 262.6 million orders across the United States — up from 223.4 million the year before — on gross transaction value of $28.8 billion, and reported its first annual net profit: $428 million, of which $358 million was a tax benefit from releasing a valuation allowance rather than anything that happened in the business.

Two cautions, because it is easy to take too much from that. Those are national figures set against one Oakland facility, so any ratio between them is arithmetic without meaning. And Instacart's decade was not Webvan's: smartphones, retailer integrations and a labour market that made same-hour delivery affordable all arrived in between. This is not the same business built better. It is a different business serving a demand that had, by then, become servable.

What the comparison establishes is narrow and sufficient. The appetite Webvan bet on was real, and it was still real long after Webvan was gone. Whatever killed the company, it was not that nobody wanted the product — which leaves the cost structure holding the bill.

A counter-case is owed here. The block above is an aftermath, not a counter-case. Instacart succeeded, and this book's rule is that every chapter shows its own advice being followed carefully and failing anyway. The honest counter-case for this chapter is a business that stayed deliberately cheap and variable, exactly as recommended above, and lost its market to someone who committed to fixed capacity early and won on scale economics. No such case has yet been found in a source that can be checked, so the chapter is published owing one rather than with a comfortable substitute in its place. The method page explains why these are structurally hard to find.
