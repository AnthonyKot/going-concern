Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 13

Profit Is Not Cash

They are not the same measurement, and the gap between them runs in both directions. A profitable business can run out of money on a Tuesday. A loss-making one can be quietly filling up. Only one of the two can pay anybody.

The decision How long you are willing to fund the gap between doing the work and being paid for it — and what you will change when the answer is longer than you can afford.

Move 1 The situation

The previous chapter named three statements and asked you to look at available cash every week. This one explains why that instruction was separate from the profit figure, and why the two numbers can tell opposite stories about the same month.

Three things get called "cash" and they are not the same, which is where most of the confusion lives:

  • Profit measures performance over a period under accounting rules. It records revenue when you earn it, not when the money lands, and costs when you incur them, not when they leave.
  • Operating cash flow measures the cash your ordinary operations generated or consumed in that period.
  • The change in the bank balance is that, and then everything else — equipment bought, loans drawn or repaid, money you put in or took out.

All three are honest. They answer different questions, and only the third one pays anybody.

The mechanism is timing. You do the work in March, invoice at the end of March, and are paid in May. Meanwhile you pay people in March, the supplier in April, and the VAT you collected on that invoice is due to the tax authority on a schedule of its own. On paper March was a good month. In the bank, March was expensive and May was the relief.

Which produces the corollary that catches people who are doing well: growth magnifies the cash cycle, in whichever direction that cycle already runs. When you pay before you collect, growth consumes cash, and selling more at a profit is exactly what empties the account. When customers pay before you incur the costs — deposits, prepaid subscriptions, retail takings, a supplier who waits — growth can supply cash instead. Neither arrangement tells you whether the underlying work is profitable. It tells you which way the account moves while you find out.

Move 2 What actually happened

The clearest published demonstration is in a document the reader will never file. Instacart's 2023 registration statement gives, for three consecutive periods, the reported profit and the cash the business actually generated. Take them in order.

For the full year 2022, "net cash provided by operating activities was $277 million, which consisted of net income of $428 million, adjusted for certain non-cash items of $275 million" and "net cash inflows from changes in operating assets and liabilities of $124 million". Watch the signs, because they are the lesson: net income of $428m, less $275m of net non-cash adjustments, plus $124m from movements in operating assets and liabilities, gives $277m. The adjustment is a subtraction because "The non-cash items primarily consisted of a deferred tax benefit of $373 million" — a gain that raised profit without raising cash, and so has to come back out.

Now the six months to June 2022, which is the fact worth carrying: "net cash provided by operating activities was $99 million, which consisted of net loss of $74 million, adjusted for certain non-cash items of $38 million". The business reported a $74m loss while its operations generated $99m of cash. Note precisely what that does and does not say: operations produced cash. What the bank balance then did depends on what was bought, borrowed or repaid afterwards, and the filing settles that on a different line.

And the six months to June 2023: "net cash provided by operating activities was $242 million, which consisted of net income of $242 million" — the two figures identical, which is a coincidence and a useful one, because it shows that agreement between them is a circumstance rather than a rule.

Two causes of the gap appear in the filing, and they are the two the reader has as well. The first is non-cash accounting entries: the 2022 profit includes a "$358 million tax benefit from the release of our valuation allowance on our deferred tax assets in the United States" — a benefit that improved the reported result by more than a third of a billion dollars without a cent moving. The second is stated by the company in the plainest possible terms. The cash inflows were "primarily due to timing of collection of accounts receivable"; elsewhere, offset "as a result of timing of vendor payments".

Timing of collection. Timing of payment. That is the whole subject, and a business with eleven customers has it in exactly the form a business with millions does.

Move 3 The reasoning

Two bridges, not one

Getting from profit to the bank balance takes two crossings, and most confusion comes from trying to do it in one.

The first bridge: profit → operating cash. Four kinds of item:

  • Non-cash income and expenses. Depreciation reduces profit without touching cash; Instacart's deferred tax benefit raised it without touching cash. Added back or taken out, depending on direction.
  • Receivables. Revenue earned and recorded, sitting in someone else's bank account. Profit counted it in March; you get it in May.
  • Inventory, prepayments and work in progress. Cash converted into objects and unfinished obligations.
  • Payables, accruals, deferred revenue and tax liabilities. Including the money that was never yours — VAT collected, payroll taxes withheld — which sits in the account flattering the balance until the day it leaves.

The second bridge: operating cash → the change in the bank balance. Equipment and other investment. Borrowing drawn and repaid. What the owner put in or took out. Dividends, acquisitions, the proceeds of selling something. None of these appear on the first bridge, and a business can generate operating cash all year while the balance falls because it bought a van.

The practical distinction: items on the first bridge that are timing — receivables, stock, payables — are fixed by changing terms, sequence or the order in which you take work. Non-cash entries are not problems at all. And everything on the second bridge is a decision you made, which is a different kind of thing to look at.

The arithmetic that decides how much you need

Put dates on it, because a vague version of this teaches nothing. Day 0: work begins and €6,000 of labour and supplier costs are paid. Day 30: the €10,000 invoice is issued. Day 90: the customer pays. You have funded €6,000 for ninety days.

One such job is a nuisance. Six running concurrently — not six completed over a quarter, which may never overlap — is €36,000 of your money sitting in other people's businesses at the same moment. What decides the number is the cash cost of each job, how many run at once, and how long the cycle is. Margin alone will not tell you: a job can be handsomely profitable and still tie up cash for a quarter.

Which is why businesses fail while growing, and why the failure surprises the owner: every indicator he was taught to watch improved right up to the week the payroll did not clear.

Why the good quarter is the dangerous one

Run it forward. Six concurrent jobs tie up €36,000. Now win the year you have been working towards and run twelve at once. The profit doubles, which is the number everyone celebrates, and the funded gap doubles too, to €72,000. That figure is not invisible — its pieces sit in receivables, work in progress and payables on the balance sheet, and in the movements in operating assets and liabilities on the cash flow statement, exactly as they do in Instacart's filing. It is fragmented. An owner can read every statement he has and still never see the operating cycle as one demand on cash, because no line adds it up for him.

That is the whole trap in one movement. The extra €36,000 has to come from somewhere: retained cash you had, an overdraft, a supplier who agrees to wait, or work you turn down. If none of those is arranged in advance, the business meets its own success as a liquidity event, and it arrives on a specific day — the payroll date — with no warning from any indicator the owner was watching.

Which is why growth needs to be financed on purpose rather than absorbed by accident, and why can I afford this customer is a different question from is this customer profitable. Both have answers. Only one of them is in the accounts.

What to actually change

Four levers. Invoice as soon as the contract and the work allow — often on delivery rather than at month end, which costs nothing and is frequently the largest single improvement available. Take money earlier in the sequence: a deposit or staged payments, where the customer is being asked to fund less risk than you are. This is chapter three's ladder used for a second purpose. Use supplier terms deliberately — an early-payment discount may be worth taking, but habitually paying before due while your customers pay late means financing both sides of the transaction. And hold a buffer sized to the gap you have measured, not to a feeling.

Collections belong on the list too, with a caveat: chasing is a real lever and worth doing properly, but it cannot repair a model whose ordinary terms require more cash than the business can fund. Fix the terms, then chase.

Boundaries

The gap does not have a fixed direction. The temptation after reading this is to assume profit always flatters. The same document shows a loss period generating cash. Profit and cash are two measurements, not an optimistic one and a realistic one.

The case is enormous and the reader is not. Instacart negotiates its payment terms from a position no small supplier has. The mechanism transfers; the magnitudes and the bargaining power do not, and nothing here should be read as a claim about how big your own gap will be.

The timing half has no reader-scale case. The chapter's arithmetic is arithmetic, and the statute below is real, but no small business in this book is shown measuring its own cash gap and acting on it. That absence is the same class of debt as the previous chapter's weekly numbers.

This chapter does not tell you what a customer is worth. Whether the gap is worth funding depends on the contribution a customer produces over the relationship — after the costs of acquiring and serving them, not their total payments — and on how long that cash takes to arrive. That is chapter fourteen, and total revenue is the wrong number for it.

On Dutch ground · how long you can be made to wait

Dutch law caps payment terms between businesses. These are ceilings, not defaults — nothing stops you agreeing far shorter, and the cap tells you only what cannot lawfully be imposed on you.

With no term agreed, statutory commercial interest runs automatically from "30 dagen na de aanvang van de dag, volgende op die waarop de schuldenaar de factuur heeft ontvangen" — thirty days after the invoice is received, with alternative starting points in the statute where the receipt date is not established, the invoice arrives before the work, or an acceptance procedure applies. No reminder is needed for interest to start. It does not run at all where the creditor is itself in default: "Geen wettelijke rente is verschuldigd wanneer de schuldeiser zelf in verzuim is."

With a term agreed, art. 6:119a lid 5: "Partijen kunnen een uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van ten hoogste 60 dagen" — at most sixty days — "tenzij zij uitdrukkelijk een langere termijn van betaling in de overeenkomst opnemen en deze termijn niet kennelijk onbillijk is jegens de schuldeiser". A longer term needs to be express, and it must not be manifestly unfair to the creditor.

And the rule most likely to matter to this reader. Where the debtor is a large company and the creditor an SME, art. 6:119a lid 6 caps the term at thirty days: "In afwijking van lid 5 kunnen partijen geen uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van meer dan 30 dagen" — and a clause purporting otherwise is void, "Een beding in een overeenkomst in strijd met de vorige zin is nietig." "Large" is defined by failing at least two of the art. 2:397 tests that the previous chapter set out, which is a rare instance of two chapters of this book meeting inside one statute.

An inspection or acceptance period cannot be used to extend this quietly: it "bedraagt niet meer dan 30 dagen vanaf de datum van ontvangst van de prestatie" unless expressly agreed and again not manifestly unfair. Selling to a public body — "de Staat, een provincie, een gemeente" and other public authorities — is governed by art. 6:119b, on its own thirty-day footing, with a limited route to sixty where the nature of the contract objectively justifies it. And the interest compounds annually: "Telkens na afloop van een jaar wordt het bedrag waarover de wettelijke rente wordt berekend, vermeerderd met de over dat jaar verschuldigde rente."

Checked 2 August 2026 against Burgerlijk Wetboek Boek 6, arts. 6:119a and 6:119b. This box states the statutory ceilings and when interest begins. It does not tell you whether a particular clause in a particular contract is enforceable, what your invoice must contain, or whether suing a customer is a good idea. Those need a Dutch lawyer and a look at the contract.

Monday

  1. Measure both halves of your gap. For the last ten jobs: the first material cash outflow, every later direct outflow, the invoice date and each receipt. That gives you two numbers — how many days cash was tied up, and the largest amount tied up before collection. Then lay the ten on one timeline and find the peak overlap, because that, not the average, is what has to be funded.
  2. Separate the money that is not yours. Take today's bank balance and subtract the VAT and payroll taxes you are holding for someone else. That smaller figure is what you actually have.
  3. Reconcile last month's profit to last month's cash movement. Receivables, payables and stock, money held for the tax authority, then non-cash entries. If the four do not close the gap, something is happening that you have not found yet.
  4. Move one invoice earlier this week. Bill on delivery instead of at month end for one job, and watch what it does. It is the cheapest lever and the one most often left unused because the old habit is invisible.
  5. Write down the terms you actually agreed. Check them against the ceilings in the box, and against the thirty-day cap if your customer is large and you are not. If someone pays you at ninety days without an agreed ninety-day term, the statute may give you a claim to commercial interest from far earlier — which is leverage in a conversation, though it will not make anyone transfer the principal on day thirty.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be an owner who measured the cash gap, funded it deliberately, shortened terms where possible — and was worse off for it: who lost good customers by insisting on deposits, or who held a buffer so large that the business starved of the investment that would have grown it. The second is the more interesting, because every instruction in this chapter has a cost and the chapter does not price it. Cash discipline is presented here as free, and it is not.
