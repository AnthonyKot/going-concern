Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 13

Profit Is Not Cash

Lea's next two jobs would leave more money in the bank after the customers pay. Before they pay, the proposed schedule takes the balance below zero. The decision has to happen before she commits to the work.

The decision Whether the proposed work and payment schedule fit the cash you have, and which agreement or commitment must change before the lowest balance becomes a missed payment.

Start with the money and obligations already there

Chapter twelve ended with €2,350 in Lea's model bank account and a €100 bookkeeping bill still to pay. Its simplified profit was also €2,350, by coincidence. Profit described that completed period; the bank and unpaid bill carry into the next one. A profitable period does not pay the next supplier until the money is available when that supplier needs it.

Lea remains a fictional employed developer testing a small consultancy. The example below is a forecast, not another set of completed jobs. It continues the earlier simplifications: VAT and taxes are excluded from the model, including its opening cash, and there are no loans or equipment purchases. A forecast for your actual business must include its actual tax and other payment dates. No overdraft or further money from Lea is assumed.

Two new offers, with a different cash requirement

Two prospective customers, D and E, need integrations beyond Lea's original €900 single-file report service. She is considering separately scoped €1,800 projects for each. This is a new, hypothetical offer, not evidence that she successfully doubled the price of the old one. Each would require €900 for a specialist and €100 of other job purchases, paid before delivery.

Lea estimates eight hours of her own sales and delivery work for each project. That would use sixteen of her next month's 32 available hours, leaving sixteen for administration, follow-up and uncertainty. These estimates still need checking against the actual scope and specialist availability. A cash forecast cannot establish that the delivery plan works.

The proposed schedule has D's €1,000 outlay on day 3 and E's on day 10. D would be delivered on day 14 and paid on day 28; E delivered on day 21 and paid on day 35. Those receipt dates are assumptions about the proposed terms and acceptance process. They are not deposits already in the bank or guarantees that payment will arrive.

Two existing cash needs sit alongside the jobs: the old €100 bookkeeping bill and €100 for software used in the new period, both paid on day 2. Lea also plans to draw €300 on day 14. The illustration assumes there are no other movements through day 35. In your version, include the whole business rather than stopping at the costs of the attractive new job.

The period total conceals the problem

If both projects complete as proposed, model revenue is €3,600. Subtract €2,000 of direct project costs and €100 of new software expense: the projected profit is €1,500 before tax and any allowance for Lea's own labour. Her sixteen estimated project hours alone represent €800 at the earlier €50 allowance; other owner work still needs counting. The cash test is not a substitute for that economic test.

The old €100 bill is paid now but was already expensed in chapter twelve. Charging it to profit again would count it twice. The €300 drawings affect cash, not the expenses of these projects in the sole-trader model. These distinctions give projected closing cash of €3,450: opening €2,350, plus receipts of €3,600, less €2,000 for the jobs, €200 for the two bills and €300 drawings.

That closing figure looks comfortable. Before reading the timeline, work out whether Lea can make every payment leading up to it. At which point does she first need money she does not have?

Notice what a monthly total could miss. The early payments happen before the receipts. If an important receipt and payment fall on the same day, check their order too; an evening receipt cannot fund a morning debit. Use dates around the tight points rather than allowing a weekly or monthly grouping to cancel them out.

Compare changes without assuming agreement

Lea considers three versions. For comparison, she chooses to keep at least €500 in this simplified forecast. That is an illustrative planning floor, not a recommended reserve for every business. It contains no allowance for the taxes omitted from the model. Her real floor would need to reflect obligations, uncertainty and access to other funds.

  • Keep the proposed schedule. The lowest balance is −€150. Reaching zero at that point requires €150; keeping her chosen €500 floor requires €650. Those are different funding questions.
  • Defer the €300 drawing until after day 35. The lowest balance becomes €150 and day-35 cash becomes €3,750. The payments through that date fit, but the low point is still €350 below her chosen floor. The household also has to be able to wait.
  • Request a €900 deposit from D before starting. If it arrives on day 2, with the remaining €900 on day 28, the lowest balance becomes €750. Keep E's terms and the €300 drawing unchanged. Day-35 cash is still €3,450: D pays the same total earlier.

Check the third version: day 2 closes at €3,050 after the two bills and D's deposit; day 3 at €2,050; day 10 at €1,050; day 14 at €750; day 28 at €1,650; day 35 at €3,450. The deposit changes the timing, not the price or the projected profit from completed work.

Lea has not yet secured that deposit. Her proposed next action is to discuss the staged payment with D, specify the scope and cancellation arrangements, and make the start dependent on receipt before committing to the specialist. If D declines, the original shortfall remains. She must change the schedule, arrange affordable funding or decline the proposed combination of work. An optimistic cell is not an agreement.

A deposit also carries an obligation to do the work and possibly to refund money under the agreement. It is not extra earnings on top of the final invoice. Keep the delivery and refund exposure visible when deciding what cash can be committed elsewhere. For applicable Dutch payment rules, see the dated section at the end of this chapter.

Make one assumption fail

Now suppose D's deposit arrives as agreed but its remaining €900 arrives on day 42 rather than day 28. Keep all other assumptions fixed. The day-28 balance stays at €750; E's day-35 payment takes it to €2,550; D's later payment takes it to €3,450. Under this sparse schedule, that delay alone does not take the balance below the €500 floor.

That is a useful result. A delay is not automatically a crisis; its effect depends on the other payments falling due. But the forecast must now extend through day 42. Any further software payment, drawing or new job cost belongs on that extended timeline. Do not keep the later receipt while silently excluding the later bills.

Test another possibility separately: D's deposit never arrives before work is due to start. The protection in Lea's proposal is the decision not to commit the outlay until receipt. If she starts anyway, she has returned to a different, unfunded schedule. Changing a payment term helps only when the business follows the sequence it negotiated.

Use the forecast without confusing it with the accounts

A real filing shows the distinction

Instacart's 2023 registration statement reports a net loss of $74 million for the six months ended June 2022, alongside $99 million of cash provided by operating activities. For the six months ended June 2023, it reports $242 million of net income and the same amount of operating cash. The first pair differs; the second happens to match.

The filing explains operating cash through adjustments for non-cash items and movements in operating assets and liabilities. It discusses the timing of customer collections and vendor payments. Those disclosures show why reported earnings alone do not explain the movement of cash. They do not tell us what payment terms a small consultancy can obtain.

Operating cash is also not the entire change in a bank balance. Investment, borrowing and owner transactions can change cash separately. Lea's drawing and her old unpaid bill are the small-example reminders: the drawing reduces cash without reducing the projected profit; the bill uses cash now for an expense already counted. Keep each movement in the right period and category rather than trying to make the profit figure do every job.

Give every expected receipt a reason and a date

In your own forecast, record the customer, the work or invoice, the agreed payment basis, the expected receipt date and what supports that date. A contract due date and your best estimate of actual receipt may differ. Keep both visible. If acceptance, missing purchase-order information or a dispute may delay payment, investigate that condition rather than merely moving the forecast date forward every week.

Use proposals and tentative work in a separately labelled scenario. Do not rely on them to cover payments already committed. Likewise, record the amount and expected date of any proposed loan or owner contribution without counting it as available funding before it is arranged. Include its fees, interest or later repayment where applicable.

KVK's cash-forecast guidance recommends setting out expected receipts and payments over time. Here the dated event view makes the shortfall inside a period visible. Choose a horizon long enough to include delivery costs and expected collection for the work being considered, plus the business's other commitments during that time.

Choose a change whose cost you understand

Earlier invoicing may help where the agreement allows it, but sending an invoice earlier does not itself make the customer pay earlier. A deposit or milestone payment shifts part of the funding to the customer. Later supplier payment needs that supplier's agreement; leaving an overdue bill unpaid is not a negotiated financing plan.

Starting E later could reduce overlap, but Lea must check the buyer's deadline and the specialist's availability. Deferring drawings affects the household. Borrowing introduces a cost and a repayment. Turning down a job gives up its possible contribution. Compare those consequences alongside the lowest balance, rather than treating cash improvement as free.

Finally, the business may have enough cash because the owner keeps adding savings. That can finance a deliberate experiment, but it is not proof that customers are funding a sustainable business. Carry the cost and time review from the earlier chapters alongside this forecast. A well-funded unprofitable offer still needs attention.

Monday

Use the worked cash timeline and blank forecast record beside your customer records and accounts. The worked file contains the three alternatives and delayed-payment scenario separately. Both files contain values or headings, not automatic formulas.

  1. Put dates on the next commitments. Start with actual opening cash and list receipts and payments in order, including existing bills, job costs, taxes, financing and owner transfers. Record uncertain receipts as assumptions and include the obligations falling due before and after them. Avoid subtracting a tax reserve at the start and counting the same payment again without explaining the adjustment.
  2. Calculate every running balance. Previous balance plus money in minus money out gives the next balance. Find the lowest point and its date. Compare it with zero and with an explicitly chosen planning floor. Move a material receipt later and extend the schedule to include the payments falling due while you wait.
  3. Resolve the decision before committing. Name the term, start date, drawing or funding arrangement that must change, who must agree and when you will know. Keep the unagreed version visible. At the next review, replace forecast movements with actual amounts and dates and explain the differences.

You have completed this exercise when you can name the first shortfall or threatened floor, the assumptions behind it and a feasible next action. A positive final balance is not enough.

What remains uncertain

The forecast is a set of assumptions to revise, not a guarantee. Lea's projects, prices and dates are invented, and the Instacart filing does not establish that deposits are the right solution for her. No small-business case here demonstrates the effect of this exact procedure. A customer may reject the changed terms, work may cost more, or collection may fail entirely. Keep the next commitment within what the actual agreements and available funds support.

The next interlude considers businesses paid at the counter rather than by invoice. The timing question still applies when stock must be bought before a sale. Chapter fourteen then asks what a customer contributes over the relationship, so you can judge what a funding commitment might be worth as well as whether you can make it.

On Dutch ground · how long you can be made to wait

Dutch law caps payment terms between businesses. These are ceilings, not defaults — nothing stops you agreeing far shorter, and the cap tells you only what cannot lawfully be imposed on you.

With no term agreed, statutory commercial interest runs automatically from "30 dagen na de aanvang van de dag, volgende op die waarop de schuldenaar de factuur heeft ontvangen" — thirty days after the invoice is received, with alternative starting points in the statute where the receipt date is not established, the invoice arrives before the work, or an acceptance procedure applies. No reminder is needed for interest to start. It does not run at all where the creditor is itself in default: "Geen wettelijke rente is verschuldigd wanneer de schuldeiser zelf in verzuim is."

With a term agreed, art. 6:119a lid 5: "Partijen kunnen een uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van ten hoogste 60 dagen" — at most sixty days — "tenzij zij uitdrukkelijk een langere termijn van betaling in de overeenkomst opnemen en deze termijn niet kennelijk onbillijk is jegens de schuldeiser". A longer term needs to be express, and it must not be manifestly unfair to the creditor.

And the rule most likely to matter to this reader. Where the debtor is a large company and the creditor an SME, art. 6:119a lid 6 caps the term at thirty days: "In afwijking van lid 5 kunnen partijen geen uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van meer dan 30 dagen" — and a clause purporting otherwise is void, "Een beding in een overeenkomst in strijd met de vorige zin is nietig." "Large" is defined by failing at least two of the art. 2:397 tests that the previous chapter set out, which is a rare instance of two chapters of this book meeting inside one statute.

An inspection or acceptance period cannot be used to extend this quietly: it "bedraagt niet meer dan 30 dagen vanaf de datum van ontvangst van de prestatie" unless expressly agreed and again not manifestly unfair. Selling to a public body — "de Staat, een provincie, een gemeente" and other public authorities — is governed by art. 6:119b, on its own thirty-day footing, with a limited route to sixty where the nature of the contract objectively justifies it. And the interest compounds annually: "Telkens na afloop van een jaar wordt het bedrag waarover de wettelijke rente wordt berekend, vermeerderd met de over dat jaar verschuldigde rente."
