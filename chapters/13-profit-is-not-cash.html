Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 13

Profit Is Not Cash

Lea has been offered two jobs that would leave €3,450 in the bank once both customers have paid. Before they pay, the same schedule takes her balance to minus €150. A profitable month can still bounce a payment. How do you find the day it happens, before you say yes?

The decision Whether the next work and payments fit the cash you have, and which term, date or payment must change before the lowest balance becomes a missed payment.

Instacart: a loss, and $99 million more cash

When Instacart filed to go public in 2023, its registration statement showed the two numbers moving apart. For the six months to June 2022, it reported a net loss of $74 million, and "net cash provided by operating activities was $99 million". Most of the gap was working capital, which the filing puts "primarily due to timing of collection of accounts receivable": customers' money arriving at a different time from the work it paid for. For the full year 2022 the gap ran the other way: "net cash provided by operating activities was $277 million, which consisted of net income of $428 million", and the largest difference was "a deferred tax benefit of $373 million", an accounting gain that brought in no cash at all.

Profit says what a period earned. Cash says what arrived and left, and when. At Instacart's size the gap is measured in hundreds of millions. At yours it is a few hundred euros on the wrong day, and it is just as real: a supplier paid on day 3 cannot be paid from a customer's money that arrives on day 28.

Lea's two offers, day by day

Lea is invented, continued from chapter twelve, which left her with €2,350 in the bank, a €100 bookkeeping bill still to pay, and a proposed €300 to herself on day 14. She will pay that bill, and the new period's €100 of software and bookkeeping, on day 2. VAT and tax stay out of the model, and she has no overdraft.

Two prospective customers, D and E, need something beyond her €900 single-file job: a second source system each. They are a different offer, so they do not count towards the three single-file jobs chapter twelve set as her test. Following her own rule, she scopes that separately and brings in a specialist, and quotes €1,800 a project. Each needs €900 for the specialist and €100 of other purchases, paid before delivery, and about eight of her own hours, sixteen of the 32 she has in the month. D would start on day 3, be delivered on day 14 and pay on day 28; E would start on day 10, be delivered on day 21 and pay on day 35.

On paper the month is good: €3,600 of revenue, €2,000 of project costs and €100 of software and bookkeeping leave €1,500 of profit before her own time. The final balance on day 35 is €3,450. Before reading the table, work out whether she can make every payment on the way there.

The final €3,450 hides it, and so would any monthly total, because the payments come before the receipts. The number that matters is the lowest one, and its date.

Three ways to move the low point

Lea wants at least €500 in the account at every point, a planning floor she chooses for this example, not a rule for every business. She compares three versions.

  • Keep the schedule. The low point is −€150. Reaching zero needs €150 from somewhere; keeping her floor needs €650.
  • Defer her own €300 until after day 35. The low point becomes €150: the payments fit, but it is still €350 below her floor, and the household has to wait.
  • Ask D for half up front. A €900 deposit on day 2 makes the low point €750, on day 14, with her drawing unchanged. It changes the timing, not the price or the profit.

She chooses to ask, and to make one rule for herself before she does: D's specialist is not booked until the deposit is in the bank. An optimistic cell in a spreadsheet is not an agreement.

What D said

D agrees to a €900 deposit, but its approval process means the money arrives on day 7, not day 2. By her rule, the specialist is booked only once it has cleared, so D's €1,000 of job costs move from day 3 to day 7. The work still takes eleven days and D still pays the rest fourteen days after delivery, so every later date moves by the four days between the old start and the new one. (The deposit itself is five days later than she hoped, day 7 against day 2; D's work had been due to start on day 3.)

  • Specialist and purchases: day 3 → day 7, after the deposit clears
  • Delivery: day 14 → day 18
  • Remaining €900: day 28 → day 32

D accepts the later delivery. Lea checks two things the cash table cannot: that the specialist is free from day 7, and that D's work, now running from day 7 to 18, still fits the sixteen hours she has set aside for the two projects, beside E's, which runs from day 10 to 21. E's dates do not change.

On day 7 she has €2,150, receives €900 and pays €1,000, leaving €2,050. E's costs on day 10 leave €1,050, and the €300 drawing on day 14 leaves €750. That is the low point, above her floor, and the €300 goes to her household on the day she planned. Then she makes one assumption fail: D's remaining €900 arrives on day 46 instead of 32. E's payment takes the balance to €2,550 on day 35 and D's to €3,450 on day 46, so the low point is unchanged, although she holds €900 less on day 35 than she planned. The test was worth running, because it moved her horizon: the forecast now has to run to day 46, and anything falling due before then belongs on it. The deposit is not extra money either. It is D's, held against work she now owes, and possibly refundable under the agreement.

Three rules carry over to your own forecast. Give every expected receipt a reason and a date, and keep the contract date and your honest estimate side by side. Keep proposals and tentative work in a separate scenario from committed ones. And price every fix: a deposit moves funding to the customer and may cost you a start date; a later supplier payment needs the supplier's agreement; deferring your own pay moves the problem to the household; a loan has a cost and a repayment; declining the job gives up its contribution. If the business only has enough cash because you keep adding savings, that is an experiment you are funding, not customers paying for a business.

Cash says whether Lea can take the work. It does not say whether the work is worth taking: her sixteen hours on D and E are not in any of these rows. Chapter fourteen asks which work to seek again, and what to spend finding it.

Monday · the lowest balance and its date

Do this one thing. Start from today's bank balance and list, by date, every payment and receipt you expect in the next five weeks: bills, job costs, tax, loan payments, what you plan to pay yourself, and what customers owe you. Keep a running balance after each line. Circle the lowest one and its date. You now know whether your next commitments fit, and which day would fail first.

Treat the two directions differently. Payments go in on the date they are due under the contract or bill, never later: pushing an outgoing payment back only makes the forecast look safer. A receipt goes in on the date you honestly expect it, marked uncertain unless the money is already agreed and dated, and you then test it later. If you do not know an amount, such as a tax payment, ask your accountant with a date for the answer, and commit to nothing that depends on that week until it comes. Five weeks is only the starting window: if you are deciding on new work, extend the list to the last payment or receipt that work creates.

If you have another hour:

  1. Make one receipt late. Move your largest uncertain receipt two weeks later, keep every payment on its due date, extend the list to the new receipt date with everything that falls due in between, and find the new low point.
  2. If the low point is below what you are comfortable holding, name the one term, date or payment that must change, who must agree, and when you will know. Keep the unagreed version visible.
  3. Use the worked files. The worked cash timeline has Lea's schedule and alternatives; the blank forecast record has the columns. Neither contains formulas.
What this chapter rests on, and what it leaves out

Instacart's figures come from its 2023 Form S-1, checked against the cached filing. The filing explains operating cash through non-cash items and changes in operating assets and liabilities; it does not say what payment terms a small consultancy can get, and operating cash is not the whole change in a bank balance either, since investment, borrowing and owner transactions move cash separately.

Lea's projects, prices, dates and D's answer are invented, and so is her €500 floor, which contains no allowance for the taxes the model leaves out. A real forecast includes tax, financing and every other payment date. Within the model, raising her day-14 drawing to €550 would leave exactly €500 at the low point: a mathematical boundary with no margin for an omitted cost, not a recommended payment, and one feasible transfer does not make a repeatable monthly income. KVK's guidance describes the same kind of forecast of expected receipts and payments; the daily view and scenarios here are this book's.

No small-business case here shows the effect of this procedure. A customer may refuse changed terms, work may cost more, or a payment may never come.

For a business paid at the counter that must buy stock before selling it, the optional stock-and-cash example applies the same timing question.

On Dutch ground · how long you can be made to wait

Dutch law caps payment terms between businesses. These are ceilings, not defaults — nothing stops you agreeing far shorter, and the cap tells you only what cannot lawfully be imposed on you.

With no term agreed, statutory commercial interest runs automatically from "30 dagen na de aanvang van de dag, volgende op die waarop de schuldenaar de factuur heeft ontvangen" — thirty days after the invoice is received, with alternative starting points in the statute where the receipt date is not established, the invoice arrives before the work, or an acceptance procedure applies. No reminder is needed for interest to start. It does not run at all where the creditor is itself in default: "Geen wettelijke rente is verschuldigd wanneer de schuldeiser zelf in verzuim is."

With a term agreed, art. 6:119a lid 5: "Partijen kunnen een uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van ten hoogste 60 dagen" — at most sixty days — "tenzij zij uitdrukkelijk een langere termijn van betaling in de overeenkomst opnemen en deze termijn niet kennelijk onbillijk is jegens de schuldeiser". A longer term needs to be express, and it must not be manifestly unfair to the creditor.

And the rule most likely to matter to this reader. Where the debtor is a large company and the creditor an SME, art. 6:119a lid 6 caps the term at thirty days: "In afwijking van lid 5 kunnen partijen geen uiterste dag van betaling overeenkomen van meer dan 30 dagen" — and a clause purporting otherwise is void, "Een beding in een overeenkomst in strijd met de vorige zin is nietig." "Large" is defined by failing at least two of the art. 2:397 tests that the previous chapter set out, which is a rare instance of two chapters of this book meeting inside one statute.

An inspection or acceptance period cannot be used to extend this quietly: it "bedraagt niet meer dan 30 dagen vanaf de datum van ontvangst van de prestatie" unless expressly agreed and again not manifestly unfair. Selling to a public body — "de Staat, een provincie, een gemeente" and other public authorities — is governed by art. 6:119b, on its own thirty-day footing, with a limited route to sixty where the nature of the contract objectively justifies it. And the interest compounds annually: "Telkens na afloop van een jaar wordt het bedrag waarover de wettelijke rente wordt berekend, vermeerderd met de over dat jaar verschuldigde rente."
