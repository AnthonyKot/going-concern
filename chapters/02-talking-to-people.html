Part I · Is there anything here? · Chapter 2

Talking to People Who Will Lie to You

Nobody is lying on purpose. That is what makes it hard to correct for: the person telling you your idea is good believes it while they are saying it, and has no stake in whether it is true.

The decision What you can learn from a conversation with someone who has every social reason to be kind to you — and what you cannot, at any length or sample size.

Move 1 The situation

You are going to talk to twenty people about your idea, and you are going to come back encouraged. That is not a prediction about your idea. It is a prediction about conversations, and four mechanisms produce it — not one of which requires anybody to be dishonest.

Politeness. Discouraging someone who is visibly excited costs the discourager thirty seconds of discomfort, immediately, with a person sitting right there. Encouraging them costs nothing, and the bill — if there is one — arrives in eighteen months, addressed to you. The asymmetry does not need bad faith. It needs an ordinary person who would rather the next ten minutes went pleasantly.

Hypotheticals. Would you use this? asks about a future self, and everyone's future self is a better person than they are: more organised, less busy, with firmer opinions and more discretionary money. Answering accurately would mean predicting your own behaviour under conditions that do not yet exist, which people are poor at even when trying.

Solution-leading questions. The moment you describe what you are building you have changed the subject. They stop reporting on their own life and start evaluating your description — an easier task, a more enjoyable one, and a different one. You have also told them what the right answer is, and you cannot take it back: the rest of that conversation, and every later one with that person, is now about your idea rather than about them.

The idealised self. People describe the person they would like to be — the one who organises their photos, keeps the books current, reads the documentation, cancels the subscriptions they no longer use. This is not vanity; it is how self-report works. It is also, precisely, the version of them who would buy your product.

To those four, add one that belongs to anyone who has done something like this before for an employer. Taking a brief from a client, a manager or a customer who has already bought works because the person across the table already has a budget, the authority to spend it, and a problem somebody has decided is worth solving — all three established before you walked in. Here none of them has been, and finding out whether they exist is the job. The skill transfers. The preconditions do not.

Move 2 What actually happened

Everpix — 33cube, Inc., 2011–2013

Everpix was a photo company, and by its own description it "was started in 2011 with the goal of solving the Photo Mess, an increasingly real pain point in people's life photo collections." Prototypes began in the late spring of 2011 and it was "angel and VC funded with $2.3M raised over its lifetime." It opened for business in September 2012 at $40 a year or $4.99 a month, moving to a freemium model in March 2013 at $49 a year.

It was a real product, on every platform, and the people who used it loved it. The company's own account describes "a very enthusiastic user base of early adopters combined with strong PR momentum."

On 20 and 21 April 2013 — twenty-three months after the first prototypes, seven months after it started charging, and the same month it took a $525,000 bridge note — Everpix bought three questions on Google Consumer Surveys and put them to a weighted sample of the national adult internet population.

The first was the founding premise. Do you feel that managing your digital photo collection is overwhelming? Of 1,248 weighted responses: yes 14.8%, no 61.3%.

The second took money out of it entirely. Do you want a better way to manage and see all of your digital photos than what you have today? Nothing to pay, nothing to install, no commitment — a question engineered to be easy to agree with. Of 1,288 responses: yes 11.9%, no 67.3%.

The third put the money back. Would you pay for a service if it could automatically help you organize, manage and print your life in photos from your entire digital collection? Of 1,268 responses: yes 12.1%, no 72.0%, with 15.9% answering "I don't take digital photos." Median response time: 7.3 seconds — nobody needed to think about it.

Set the second and third side by side, because that pair is the finding — not either number alone. Twelve per cent of the national adult internet population is a fact about a population, not a verdict on a product; a survey of everybody is not a survey of your customer. Attaching a price moved the yes rate from 11.9% to 12.1% — two-tenths of a point, in the wrong direction, well inside a confidence interval of about two points either way. Two numbers that ought to differ and do not are a fact about demand: price was not what stood between Everpix and its market.

Nothing in the record suggests the answers changed anything: the company kept building and kept pitching, and six and a half months later, on 5 November 2013, announced it was shutting down. Its position then: 50,000 signed-up users, including 7,000 subscribers, and subscription sales of "$40,000 / month during the last 3 months" — which it describes, without flinching, as "enough money to cover variable costs, but not the fixed costs of the business." Lifetime subscription income: $269,974.47, against total expenses of $2,665,192.34.

This case is not at your scale. $2.3m of other people's money, an office, a payroll, and a Series A that did not arrive — the way Everpix died is a venture death rather than the kind this book is mostly about.

Everpix is here because almost nobody publishes the research they ran and did not act on. After the shutdown the company released its whole dataset — metrics, AWS bills, cap table, profit and loss, the anonymised rejections from investors — calling it a "rare and uncensored inside look at the internals of a startup." The surveys are in that folder, dated, so you can see what the company knew and when. And there is one more document in it, which closes the argument.

Between 23 May and 4 June 2013, Everpix spent $11,500 on search and display advertising aimed at "people actively seeking photo solutions & photo enthusiasts" — not the general public, but people already looking. Sign-ups came in at $13.46, which is cheap. The problem was further down: "conversion from sign-up to paid conversion is about 80% lower for paid traffic vs. organic traffic." The deck's own verdict: "ROI is -87% on a 3 year LTV of $147."

That is the enthusiasm, measured. People who arrived under their own steam — who had read the coverage, gone looking for the site, installed an uploader and waited while it ingested their library — converted at roughly five times the rate of people who merely clicked an advertisement about photos. The enthusiastic user base was entirely real. It was also, largely, an artefact of how much work it took to join. Everpix had built an efficient machine for finding the twelve per cent and was reading its output as evidence about everybody else.

Move 3 The reasoning

A conversation cannot measure the future, and stated interest is a claim about the future. What a conversation measures well is the past — because the past has already happened, and the person is not being asked to predict themselves, only to remember.

So the method reduces to a single instruction: ask about things that have already occurred. Nobody at Everpix asked; its surveys and its advertising funnel show that stated interest was weak and the enthusiasm largely a sampling artefact, and say nothing whatever about whether asking would have saved the company. What follows is a proposal about what to do instead, owed better evidence than this chapter can supply, and to be held more loosely than the diagnosis it follows. There are five things to extract, and you want all five — you can survive missing one, and no single one is enough.

1. Past behaviour

Not would you use this but when did you last…. You are looking for a date. If they cannot supply one, there is no behaviour, and everything built on top of it is fiction. "I'm always losing files" is not a date. "Last Thursday, and it took me about an hour" is.

2. The existing workaround

Every real problem already has a bad solution attached, because people do not sit still inside pain. A spreadsheet. A file-naming convention. A junior who does it by hand every Monday. Find it and look closely, because the workaround tells you three things at once: that the problem is real, what they currently accept as good enough, and — since somebody is already paying for it in hours or money — roughly what it is worth. A problem with no workaround is usually not a problem. Usually: past behaviour and existing workarounds can only find demand for something people are already doing badly, and where the behaviour does not exist yet a rigorous and correctly executed set of interviews returns a confident no, and is wrong. Almost everything is a better version of something, and the exception is claimed far more often than it occurs; if you believe you are inside it, the honest position is not that the method failed but that you have no cheap evidence at all and are gambling — permissible, provided you know it and attach a stopping rule, which is chapter four.

3. Urgency

What happened the last time it went wrong, and what did that cost? A problem with no incident attached to it is a preference, and preferences do not get budget. You are looking for a specific bad Tuesday, with consequences somebody remembers.

4. Authority

Who signs. Interviews measure the person in front of you, not what else has to be true, and you can run all five questions perfectly on the wrong people: Cydoc's doctors genuinely liked the product and would have answered honestly about their own behaviour, and what killed the business was the arithmetic of the practices they worked in — a four-thousand-dollar integration against thirty dollars a month. So, in a business: who else has to agree, what that person's last refusal was about, and whether this comes out of a budget that already exists or one that would have to be created. Money that has to be found is a different sale from money already allocated, and usually a much longer one. For a consumer the question is smaller but not absent: whose money, and what does it displace.

5. Actual spending

What do they pay today for anything adjacent — the incumbent tool, the contractor, the hours of the person doing it by hand. Money already moving in the direction of your problem is the strongest evidence a conversation can produce, and the only one of the five that is not a proxy. It is still not a purchase. Chapter three is about that difference.

How to run it

Do not describe your solution: not at the start, not in the middle, and if you can manage it, not at all. If they ask what you are building, say you will tell them at the end — and mean it. Write down only facts: dates, amounts, names of tools, things people did. Never write down a compliment; it is not information about the market, it is information about the room. Ask the question you are afraid of, the one whose answer would be inconvenient, because otherwise you will leave it until the money has been spent. And treat a clear no as a good outcome, which it is — the cheapest thing you will ever buy. The formulation is Rob Fitzpatrick's: ask about their life, not about your idea.

What the ledger looks like, and what you do with it

An invented one, to show the shape — not a case. A bookkeeper wants to sell monthly accounts to market traders at £60 a month, and runs the five questions on five of them. Two supply a date: one lost a Saturday to a VAT letter in March, one paid an accountant £400 in January to reconstruct a year from carrier bags of receipts. Both have a workaround with money in it. Two more say "it's a nightmare" and cannot name a day, a cost or a tool; their workaround is not doing it. The fifth does his own in an hour a month and is content. Five people, and the ledger already says something the conversations did not feel like saying: the market is not "traders," it is traders who have recently been hurt, and there are two of them in five.

The decision rule, stated before the conversations rather than after: continue if at least three of five produce a date, an incident with a cost, and money already moving — the same three, on the same person; narrow if the ones who do share a feature the others lack, and go and find five more like them; stop — or, if you are claiming the thing without precedent, admit the gamble — if none do. The bookkeeper is at two of five with a visible pattern, which is narrow. The numbers are yours to set; what is not optional is setting them before you have five conversations' worth of reasons to move them.

Monday

  1. Write down the five things you believe about your customer — when they last hit the problem, what they do instead, what the last incident cost, who signs, what they pay today. Next to each, the last piece of evidence you got that was not somebody being nice to you. The blank lines are this week's agenda.
  2. Book five conversations, and apply one filter: nobody who would find it awkward to tell you this is a bad idea. Not friends, not the former colleague who likes you, not your partner. Sell to them later if you like — a friend is a fine first customer and a useless first witness. If that empties the list, that is the finding, and the week's work is building a different list.
  3. Write the script in the past tense. Five questions and nothing else: When did you last hit this? What did you do instead? What did that cost you, in hours or in money? Who else would have to agree? What do you pay for anything like this today?
  4. Ban your solution from the first half of the conversation — twenty minutes across a table, or the first three messages on a phone. If they ask what you are building, say "at the end," and return to the question. Notice how much you want to skip this one — that wanting is Move 1, operating on you in real time.
  5. Keep the notes as a ledger, not a narrative. Dates, amounts, tool names, actions taken — then read the file back with nothing else in it. A conversation that produced no line produced no evidence, however good it felt.

What would prove this chapter wrong

It would be an owner who ran the five questions properly on genuine customers, drew the reasonable conclusion from consistent answers, and was wrong — either a no that should have been a yes, or a yes describing a market that did not exist at the required price. No checkable case has been found, so the failure mode is reasoned about rather than demonstrated and the debt remains visible.
