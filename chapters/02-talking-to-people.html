Part I · Is there anything here? · Chapter 2

What They Say, What They Pay

Everpix had users who loved it, press that loved it and $2.3m of other people's money. Twenty-three months in, it asked the country two questions: do you want a better way to manage your photos, and would you pay for one. The yes was 11.9% to the first and 12.1% to the second. What can you learn by asking, what can only a payment tell you, and what do you owe once somebody pays?

The decision What you will ask people about, what you will ask them for, and whether you could deliver what they would then have bought.

Everpix: loved, and twelve per cent

Everpix — 33cube, Inc., 2011–2013

Everpix was a photo company, and by its own description it "was started in 2011 with the goal of solving the Photo Mess". It was "angel and VC funded with $2.3M raised over its lifetime." It opened for business in September 2012 at $40 a year or $4.99 a month. It was a real product, on every platform, and the company's own account describes "a very enthusiastic user base of early adopters combined with strong PR momentum."

On 20 and 21 April 2013, seven months after it started charging and in the month it took a $525,000 bridge note, Everpix bought three questions on Google Consumer Surveys and put them to a weighted sample of the national adult internet population.

The first was the founding premise. Do you feel that managing your digital photo collection is overwhelming? Yes 14.8%, no 61.3%.

The second took money out of it entirely. Do you want a better way to manage and see all of your digital photos than what you have today? Nothing to pay, nothing to install. Yes 11.9%, no 67.3%.

The third put the money back. Would you pay for a service if it could automatically help you organize, manage and print your life in photos from your entire digital collection? Yes 12.1%, no 72.0%. Median response time: 7.3 seconds. Nobody needed to think about it.

The pair is the finding. Attaching a price moved the yes by two-tenths of a point, in the wrong direction, inside a margin of about two points either way. Two numbers that ought to differ and do not: price was not what stood between Everpix and its market. Most people did not have the problem.

A month later it tested the other reading, that the right people simply had not heard of it. It spent $11,500 on advertising aimed at "people actively seeking photo solutions & photo enthusiasts". Sign-ups were cheap, at $13.46. But "conversion from sign-up to paid conversion is about 80% lower for paid traffic vs. organic traffic", and the deck's own verdict was "ROI is -87% on a 3 year LTV of $147."

Nothing in the record suggests either result changed anything. On 5 November 2013 the company announced it was shutting down, with 50,000 users, 7,000 of them subscribers, and subscription sales it describes as "enough money to cover variable costs, but not the fixed costs of the business." Lifetime subscription income was $269,974.47 against expenses of $2,665,192.34.

The enthusiasm was real. It was also a sample. The people who loved Everpix had read the coverage, gone looking for the site, installed an uploader and waited while it swallowed their library. That much work selects for the twelve per cent, and the company was reading its output as evidence about everybody else. You will do the same thing on a smaller scale, and for a simpler reason: everyone you ask will be kind.

Ask about what already happened

Nobody will be lying. Discouraging an excited person costs thirty seconds of discomfort now; encouraging them costs nothing, and the bill arrives in eighteen months, addressed to you. Would you use this? asks about a future self, who is better organised and has more spare money than the real one. And once you describe what you are building, they stop reporting on their life and start reviewing your description, which is easier and tells you nothing. If you have taken briefs from clients at work, note the difference: those people already had a budget, the authority to spend it and a problem someone had decided to solve. Here none of that is established, and finding out is the job.

A conversation cannot measure the future. It measures the past well, because the person only has to remember. So ask for five things, all of which have already happened:

  1. A date. Not would you but when did you last. "I'm always losing files" is not a date. "Last Thursday, and it took me an hour" is.
  2. The workaround. A spreadsheet, a naming convention, a junior who does it by hand every Monday. It shows the problem is real, what counts as good enough, and roughly what it is worth. A problem with no workaround is usually not a problem.
  3. The last incident and what it cost. A problem with no bad Tuesday attached is a preference, and preferences do not get budget.
  4. Who signs. Who else has to agree, and whether the money comes from a budget that exists or one that would have to be created. At Cydoc, a medical-software company whose story chapter four tells, the doctors liked the product and answered honestly; the practices they worked in would not pay for the integration.
  5. What they pay today for anything adjacent: the tool, the contractor, the hours. Money already moving towards your problem is the strongest thing a conversation can produce.

Do not describe your solution until the end; if they ask, say "at the end" and mean it. Write down only facts: dates, amounts, tool names, things people did. A compliment is information about the room, not the market. The formulation is Rob Fitzpatrick's: ask about their life, not about your idea. Talk to people who would not find it awkward to tell you it is a bad idea. A friend is a fine first customer and a useless first witness.

Set the rule before the conversations, not after. For example: continue if at least three of five people give you a date, an incident with a cost and money already moving; narrow if the ones who do share something the others lack, and find five more like them; stop if none do. Three of five is an illustration, not a tested threshold. Pick your own numbers, but pick them first, because five pleasant conversations will supply five reasons to move them.

Here is what that looks like with invented answers. A bookkeeper wants to sell monthly accounts to market traders at £60 a month and asks five of them. Two supply a date: one lost a Saturday to a VAT letter in March, one paid an accountant £400 in January to rebuild a year from carrier bags of receipts. Both have a workaround with money in it. Two more say "it's a nightmare" and cannot name a day, a cost or a tool. The fifth does his own in an hour a month and is content. Two of five is below her continue line, but the two share something: both were recently hurt by the tax office. So she narrows. Her next five are traders who had a tax letter or an accountant's bill this year, and she asks the same questions again.

When the method can be wrong

Where the behaviour does not exist yet, this method returns a confident no and can be wrong. Almost everything is a better version of something, so the exception is claimed far more often than it occurs. If you believe you are inside it, you have no cheap evidence and are gambling, which is allowed if you know it and attach a stopping rule.

The Glif: the question that costs them something

Money already moving is still not a purchase from you. Everything in that conversation was free for the person you were talking to. The next question is the first whose answer you can bank: will you pay for this now, before it exists? Asking turns a pleasant conversation into a transaction. That discomfort is the price of the information, and it is cheap.

Studio Neat — Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost, 2010–

Two people. Their first product was the Glif, a small moulded lump that clamps an iPhone to a tripod. In October 2010 they put it on Kickstarter with a working prototype and a goal of $10,000. Backers pledge; nobody is charged unless the goal is reached; then everyone is charged at once. The buyer hands over a non-refundable payment for a thing that does not exist, and the maker receives cash and a list of people they now owe.

The campaign opened on 3 October with nothing pledged. That day they emailed John Gruber of Daring Fireball and offered him a prototype. He linked to them the next morning. In their words: "1 hour and 25 minutes after the post went live, we surpassed our funding goal of $10,000, and finished the day with over $25,000." At the close the page recorded "5,273 Backers · $137,417 pledged of $10,000 goal". They started shipping "in early December, 2010", about a month later.

They did it four more times, raising "nearly half a million dollars in total." All five were delivered.

Their rule for when a campaign may launch at all: "a napkin sketch is not enough. If you are making a physical piece of hardware, a working prototype is a must." And it is "important to start conversations with manufacturers beforehand to make sure your design is not impossible (or extremely difficult) to manufacture."

Fourteen times the goal is the audience of one link, not a fact about tripod clamps. The number to carry is that five thousand strangers paid before a product existed. Their 2013 campaign for an iPhone app had the same $10,000 goal and closed at "$12,442 pledged · 1,211 backers": same makers, same instrument, an answer ten times quieter. A presale does not return yes or no. It returns a size, from the people who saw it.

What to ask for

Rank what you might ask for by what the buyer gives up, among buyers who are independent of you and are actually your customers.

  1. Verbal enthusiasm. Free to give. It tells you the pitch is intelligible.
  2. Signed intent. Costs a signature. Worth more if it went through procurement and a budget holder than if one friendly director signed it alone.
  3. A refundable deposit. The first rung with money on it. The buyer can still unwind it, so it measures interest on one day.
  4. A non-refundable payment. Hard to fake, and where your obligation becomes real. The Glif sat here. For a consultancy it is paid discovery: a small fixed piece of real work, invoiced first. For a café it is a standing order: twenty sandwiches every Tuesday for a month, the first week paid now.
  5. A full-price repeat purchase. The only rung that tests the product and not the promise, and the slowest.

Climb until it hurts to ask, then ask. The rung you are avoiding is the one carrying the information.

Coolest Cooler: the yes that became a debt

There is one more question, and it is not a rung, because it does not measure the buyer. It measures you. Can you deliver what they just bought, at that price, in that time?

Coolest Cooler — the demand test passed 265 times over

In the summer of 2014 Ryan Grepper put a cooler on Kickstarter, with a built-in blender, a speaker and a USB charger. Goal: $50,000. The page records the close: "62,642 Backers · $13,285,226 pledged of $50,000 goal". That is two hundred and sixty-five times the target, which is a small maker's forty unexpected yeses with more zeros. Just over fifty thousand took the main tier: "Pledge $185 or more · 50215 backers · COOLEST COOLER." The page gave a date: "Estimated delivery: Feb 2015."

In February 2015 there were no coolers. According to Matthew Hughes in The Register, the company had "spruiked over 60,000 units costing $185 apiece" and, two years after the campaign, "asked backers for a further $97 in order for them to receive their product before the July 4 holidays of 2016." A settlement with the Oregon Department of Justice followed. It shut down in December 2019, five years past its delivery date, "with 20,000 orders unfulfilled," owing those backers money that "cannot be discharged in bankruptcy."

The pledge total was the best demand evidence anyone in this book obtained. It was also a contract to manufacture sixty thousand complex objects at a price fixed by someone who had never made one. Both arrived in the same instant, from the same act, and only one of them felt like winning.

Success on the first question makes the second harder. Studio Neat's warning against stretch goals names it: "a slippery slope that can lead to biting off more than you can chew." So before you ask anyone for money, answer three checks:

  • Can it be made at all? Studio Neat's version, a working prototype and a conversation with whoever would have to make it, is the cheapest form of the question.
  • At that price? The price you are about to take money at, including the thing, the shipping, the payment fees, the returns and your hours, on the day you have to buy the materials.
  • By that date? Their advice is one sentence: "However long you think the project will take to complete, you may do well to simply double that estimate."

On Dutch ground, whether "non-refundable" holds depends on whether you sell to a business or to a consumer, and on whether the thing is made to order; the section at the end of this chapter quotes the rule and the exceptions.

Everpix asked too late and did not act on the answer. Studio Neat asked for money and could deliver. Coolest Cooler asked and could not. There is a fourth outcome, which nobody posts about: the answers come back thin, or the deliverability check comes back no, and you have to decide whether to go on. Chapter four is about writing that decision down before you need it.

Monday · one conversation, booked

Do this one thing. Pick one person who has the problem and would not find it awkward to tell you no, ask them for twenty minutes this week, and copy the five questions into your notes under their name: When did you last hit this? What did you do instead? What did it cost you? Who else would have to agree? What do you pay for anything like it today? You now hold one booked conversation and the script for it. In the conversation, write down only dates, amounts, tools and actions.

Nobody on your list who could comfortably say no? That is the finding, and building a different list is this week's work: one name found this way counts as finishing.

If you have another hour:

  1. Write your continue, narrow and stop numbers, then book four more.
  2. Once the answers include a date, a cost and money already moving, write the ask: “It will be X, it will be ready by [date], and I need [amount] up front to start.” Answer the three checks under it in one line each. If you cannot fill in the date or the price, that blank is the next thing to work out, and nobody is asked for money until it is filled.
  3. Write down what happens if it works too well. If forty say yes instead of five: who makes them, with what money, by when.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Everpix is not at your scale: $2.3m of other people's money, an office, a payroll and a funding round that did not arrive. It is here because almost nobody publishes the research they ran and did not act on. After the shutdown the company released its whole dataset, calling it a "rare and uncensored inside look at the internals of a startup", and the surveys are in it, dated. The record shows stated interest was weak; it does not show that asking earlier would have saved the company. The five questions are a proposal, held more loosely than the diagnosis.

Studio Neat's figures are their own account and the archived Kickstarter pages. They ran one instrument five times, so the case shows a non-refundable payment before existence is obtainable and survivable; the order of the other rungs is an argument from what each costs the buyer, not a result. Coolest Cooler's figures are its archived page and The Register's reporting. Grepper attributed the end to tariffs on Chinese imports; why it collapsed is disputed and not settled here.

Still owed: an owner who ran the five questions properly on genuine customers and was misled by consistent answers; someone who cleared both the payment and the three checks, delivered, and still had no business; or someone whose presale returned a clear no, who stopped and was wrong. None has been found in a checkable record, so the debt stands.

On Dutch ground · whether "non-refundable" holds

Whether "non-refundable" holds depends on who is selling to whom, and how — and for most readers of this book the answer is reassuring. If you sell to a business, none of what follows applies; that is a different regime. If you make things to order — goods "vervaardigde zaken, die niet geprefabriceerd zijn", made to the consumer's specification, not prefabricated — art. 6:230p BW puts you outside the rule too. Bespoke work, custom commissions, a service designed for the client: rung four is what it looks like.

The rule itself catches one situation: a trader selling a stock product to a consumer at a distance or away from business premises. There, art. 6:230o BW gives the consumer fourteen days to withdraw "zonder opgave van redenen" — without giving reasons. For goods the clock starts the day the consumer receives them, not the day they pay; for services, the day the contract is concluded. So in an ordinary online consumer preorder, rung four moves back toward rung three, and the wording of what you sell matters.

Whether a crowdfunding pledge is a contract of this kind depends on whether you are acting as a trader, the backer as a consumer, and the arrangement as a distance sale — a question for someone qualified, not for a book. If you are selling stock goods to consumers online, ask a lawyer who does consumer contracts before you write the terms, not after somebody asks for their money back.
