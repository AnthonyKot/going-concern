Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 8

Asking for Money

Every closed tab is a no with no reason attached. The only instrument that returns the reason is a person who has been asked directly and now has to answer you.

The decision Whether you are going to ask people for money yourself, out loud, to their face — and what you will do with the reasons they give you when they say no.

Move 1 The situation

This is the chapter you were going to skip, and the skipping has a respectable vocabulary. Nathan Barry lists the phrases he hears from bootstrappers: "As the founder my time is too valuable for selling $49/month accounts", that it does not scale, that there is no money for a sales team. Every one is true in some circumstance. All of them are usually being said by someone who has no traction, which is the circumstance in which none of them holds.

But the excuses are not the interesting part. The interesting part is what you lose by accepting them, and it is not revenue. It is information.

Consider what Part I and the last two chapters have actually built. Chapter two got you conversations. Chapter three got you a commitment. Chapter six defined a buyer. Chapter seven put you where they already are. Every one of those produces a stream of people arriving, looking, and leaving — and every departure is silent. You have a conversion rate, which tells you how many said no, and nothing whatever about why. Barry's version is blunter: "When you sell online you are getting rejected dozens of times without hearing a reason. If someone comes to your blog post or sales page and closes the tab without buying, they rejected you."

Your first objection is not one of Barry's three. It is that this should not be necessary — that a good product reaches people through word of mouth. Barry has an answer, and it is the best paragraph in his article. In 2013 he read a post by the founder of Customer.io about how "they grew from $2,400 MRR in December 2012 to $50,000 MRR a year later", met him at a conference, and asked what drove it. The answer was word of mouth. Barry's reaction, as a founder with thirty customers, was that this is useless: word of mouth is not a "magic lever that you can just pull to get growth". It is what having customers produces, not a way of getting them.

Which is worth generalising past this case, because the pattern recurs everywhere in this genre. Ask a successful founder what worked and you will often be told the name of an output of success, described as its cause. Word of mouth, brand, community, inbound. All real, all downstream, none of them available at thirty customers. The thing that is available at thirty customers is asking people one at a time, which is why this chapter exists and why it sits after the two that promised leverage.

Set the closed tab against a person you have asked: "The basic rules of social interactions require them to give you a reason" as to why they will not buy. That asymmetry is the whole argument. Asking is not primarily a way to make this month's number. It is the only apparatus you own that converts a no into a sentence.

Move 2 What actually happened

In late 2012 Nathan Barry set himself a public goal: "to start a brand new SaaS company and grow it to $5,000 in monthly recurring revenue within 6 months", with the constraint that "I could only make an initial investment of $5,000." He built an email marketing tool, ConvertKit, and he missed. "July 1st, 2013 arrived and ConvertKit was only at $2,480/month". "$2,480 was the peak month."

Then eighteen months of decline, which he documents without softening. He went back to selling books, which were earning well. He tried teaching beginners how to build a list, and it worked briefly and attracted the wrong people: "these beginners complained the most about price", and "We were actually driving away our best customers and focusing on those least likely to succeed."

"In October 2014 monthly recurring revenue had slid all the way down to $1,207." The decision he then faced was chapter four's, arriving on schedule: "Either give ConvertKit the attention it deserved or shut it down". Shutting it down was the defensible choice — the books were paying and the tool was not, and had not been for eighteen months. He doubled down anyway, and did two things at once.

The first was positioning, and he is blunt about the diagnosis: a market definition with the word anyone in it will probably fail. He chose email marketing for authors. "By the end of October MRR had grown 23% to $1,646", then another 27% in November, to $2,100.

The second was asking. He began "tracking every conversation in a ConvertKit Sales Trello board" — the whole system, at first, being a Trello board — after a friend watched him do it for the first time and said "been trying to get you to do that for over a year." Then the outreach: "we contacted all the top sellers on LeanPub and Udemy." And the yield:

"Out of about a hundred personal emails, we picked up 5-7 customers. Not great, but also not bad."

February "closed the month growing 17% to $4,561", and in March 2015 he passed the goal he had set for six months and reached in twenty-six.

Move 3 The reasoning

Ask about the frustration, do not pitch

The email he says he has sent over a thousand times opens like this: "Is anything frustrating you with MailChimp?" — then a sentence naming what he runs and who else uses it, and nothing else. No offer, no price, no call to action. His own gloss: "The point is to start a conversation, not to make a sale."

Readers of chapter two should recognise that exactly. It is the same instrument — ask about the present and the past, never about the hypothetical future — pointed at a stranger instead of an acquaintance, with money now plausibly on the table. Chapter two said a conversation cannot measure the future. This chapter adds that a conversation is nonetheless the only thing that will tell you what is standing between you and the money, and that you have to be the one to open it.

Whom to ask, and why the list should be small enough to finish

An instruction to send emails is worthless without a list, and Barry's method for building one is the part that connects this chapter backwards to the two before it. He prospects by niche, and the instruction is to keep narrowing past the point of comfort: not bloggers, not recipe blogs, but "Paleo recipe blogs run by women". The test is whether you could sit down and enumerate the whole population by hand. If you can, it is small enough.

The reason is not tidiness. It is that "The smaller the circle you target the more likely your prospects know each other" — so the first customer is evidence to the second, and being unknown everywhere is replaced by being conspicuous in one small place. Then, once you are the obvious choice in that circle, you move to an adjacent one rather than to the general market.

Notice what this does to chapter six. There, narrowing was justified because it gives you a tiebreaker for decisions. Here it earns its keep a second way, and a more immediate one: a definition narrow enough to be a list is a definition you can act on Monday morning. If your buyer definition cannot be turned into a hundred names, it is still too wide, whatever else it is doing for you.

And the discipline on the other side, which is the mistake he says he is asked about weekly: leaving a niche too early. "only 5% of professional bloggers even know we exist", he writes, at a point where the company is large and the market feels exhausted. The feeling that you have outgrown a segment arrives long before you have.

The objection that turned out to be the product

Here is what asking bought him, and it is worth more than the accounts. The conversations kept ending the same way: the prospect liked it, and "switching email providers is so much work." Out of what he calls desperation he offered to do the switch himself — "and do it all for you. For free." They agreed. And then the realisation: "The prospect placed all their possible objections on a single thing: the cost of switching."

That is the shape to look for. Not a list of complaints but a single load-bearing one, which everything else is resting on, and which no analytics package will ever show you because the people who have it leave without typing anything. Removing it became a standing service, and it came with a number attached: "the churn on accounts that go through a migration is around 1.5% rather than the more typical 5.5%."

So the sequence runs: ask → get a reason → discover the reason is one thing → build the removal of that thing → find the customers who came through the removal are the ones who stay. None of that is available to someone reading a funnel report. It is available to someone who asked a hundred people and listened to the ones who said no.

What the asking told him about chapter six

The positioning was wrong twice, and the instrument that caught it both times was a conversation. Email marketing for authors pulled in people who "tended to be on the smallest accounts, needed the most help, and were the most likely to cancel". He knew because he was talking to them. So they moved again — his mastermind group's decisions: rebrand as email marketing for professional bloggers, and "Focus even more on direct sales."

This is the honest relationship between chapters six and eight, and it runs the opposite way from the order they appear in. Positioning is not settled and then executed. It is proposed, then corrected by the only people who can correct it, who will not volunteer the correction unless asked. If you have defined a buyer and have never once had someone tell you to your face that they are not it, you do not yet know whether the definition is right.

Boundaries

Two things changed in the same fortnight, again. He repositioned and started selling at once, so the recovery from $1,207 cannot be assigned to either alone. That is the second time in three chapters — chapter six's case has the identical confound. Two instances do not fix it, but they do suggest something about how these decisions arrive: together, under pressure, when the alternative is shutting down.

He was not doing this on nothing. The books were earning well, he put $50,000 in, and he hired. A reader whose floor depends on this month's invoices is running a materially different experiment, and the numbers in the figures strip should be read with that in front of them.

This is a prosumer sale. Individual buyer, own money, decision in a single call, price in tens of dollars a month. Neither a €40 impulse purchase nor a €40,000 procurement with four people and a legal review. The asking generalises; the cadence does not.

Five to seven percent is the realistic figure, and it is his. "Not great, but also not bad." If you send a hundred honest emails and get five customers, that is the advertised result and not a sign that you are bad at this.

One last relation, to the previous chapter, since they can look like opposites. Chapter seven said to practise channels that accumulate. This one says to spend your evenings on something that accumulates nothing and stops the moment you stop. Both are right, in that order, and Barry states the join: do the unscalable thing "because that enables the channels that do scale." The hundred emails are not a distribution strategy. They are how you survive long enough to have one, and how you find out what the accumulating channel should say.

Monday

  1. Write the list before the first email. A hundred names, by hand, from chapter six's definition — if you cannot finish it, the definition is still too wide. Then send twenty of them one question before Friday: what is frustrating you about [the thing they use now]. No pitch, no price, no link.
  2. Open a Trello board today. Three columns: talked to, waiting on, dead. Barry ran a business on exactly this. You are not too sophisticated for it; you are avoiding it.
  3. Write down the last five reasons anyone gave you for not buying. If you cannot fill five lines, that is the finding: you have been collecting silent nos and calling it market research.
  4. Find your single objection. Of those reasons, which one is load-bearing — the one that, removed, makes the rest stop mattering? Then cost out removing it for one customer, by hand, for free.
  5. Book the follow-up now. Every conversation ends with a date in your calendar or it ends. Barry names follow-up as the mistake he made repeatedly: "You must follow-up."

Two weeks in Thailand — the part that did not scale was him

Barry's article is emphatic on this point: "Direct sales absolutely scales and anyone who says otherwise is just too lazy to do the work." His evidence is that ConvertKit later hired a sales team, which establishes that selling scales for a company with product-market fit and a payroll. It does not establish that the founder's own selling scales, and his contemporaneous account two years earlier says the opposite.

In January 2015, mid-recovery, he went away for a fortnight. The first half of the month was the best stretch the company had had: "From December 15th to January 15th we grew 60% to $4,067." Then he left. "We still hit 20% growth in January" — but only because of those first fifteen days, and "We ended the month with lower MRR than we had on the 15th." His own heading for the episode: "Growth stalls out when I go on vacation."

What makes this sharp rather than merely a slow fortnight is what did keep working. He is unambiguous that the team handled everything else without him — support, outages, customers — and calls it a founder's dream. The single function that stopped was the one this chapter is telling you to do personally. Not the company: him.

A limit, then, and not a counter-case. Nothing here says do not sell; the selling plainly worked, and it is why the numbers moved at all. What it says is that the chapter's instrument has the founder built into it, and an instrument you have to hold cannot be left running. That is a cost to price in from the first email, not a discovery to make in year three, and it is the subject the book reaches at chapter seventeen, You Are the Bottleneck.

He is candid about the same weakness elsewhere in the article. Recommending that founders fly to a prospect's city rather than asking for another call, he adds that he is "better at giving this advice than I am at taking it", doing it once or twice a year where he thinks it should be monthly. The unscalable acts work and are difficult to sustain, and the person recommending them was not sustaining them either.

Note the boundary on the boundary: one fortnight, one company, self-attributed. He does not isolate the vacation from the season, and January follows December. The claim that survives is modest and still worth having — the growth that direct selling produced did not continue in his absence, on the one occasion the record allows a look.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

The shape: someone who did the asking properly — a real list, honest questions about frustrations rather than pitches, follow-up tracked, several hundred conversations — and learned nothing that changed the business, or learned things that were actively misleading. A false positive would do as well as a false negative: a founder who took the stated objection at face value, built the removal of it, and found the objection was a polite substitute for a different and fatal one.

The second shape is the more likely to be real and is the harder to find, because it requires someone to publish an account in which they listened carefully to customers and were led astray by it. The genre has no room for that story. Every published sales account is written by someone for whom the asking worked, which means the base rate on asking is unknown to this book and probably to everyone.
