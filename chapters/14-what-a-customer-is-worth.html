What a Customer Is Worth

Four customers paid Cydoc. The sales were real, the product worked, and the arithmetic underneath them was not survivable. A paying customer and a worthwhile customer are not the same thing, and the gap between them is measurable long before anybody has a lifetime to measure.

The decision Which customers are worth acquiring again — judged on what has actually remained after acquiring and serving them, and how long that money took to arrive.

Move 1 The situation

Somewhere you have already multiplied a monthly payment by a number of months and written the result down as what a customer is worth. The previous chapter asked whether you can afford to finance the gap between the work and the payment; this one asks whether what eventually arrives was worth financing at all.

That is lifetime value, and in a business this age it is worse than useless. It asks you to multiply a monthly payment by an expected number of months, subtract an acquisition cost, and treat the product as a fact. Two of those three inputs are guesses, and the guess sits in the position with the most leverage: a customer who stays two years is worth four times one who stays six months, so the number you know least determines the answer almost entirely.

Three different things get called what a customer is worth, as three different things got called cash last chapter, with the same consequence: you end up comparing figures that were never answering the same question.

  • Total revenue. Everything the customer has paid or is projected to pay. This is the number every business has, and it is the least informative of the three.
  • Contribution. What remains after the costs of acquiring that customer and serving that customer — not a share of the rent, not a share of your accountant, but the costs that exist because that customer exists.
  • Contribution to date. The same figure, computed only over the period you have actually observed, with everything after today marked unknown rather than projected.

This chapter is about the third one. It is the least flattering and the only one that is evidence.

Move 2 What actually happened

Rachel Draelos spent seven years building Cydoc, an AI documentation tool for physicians, "for seven years (April 2018 to August 2025)". Her post-mortem, published after the shutdown, is unusually exact about the numbers that matter here.

By the conventional signals the business was working. She reached what she calls "what many consider to be validation milestones: paying customers, patents, and demonstrated clinical impact". The clinical impact was real and measured — the tool "saved 11 minutes per visit". Two US patents were granted. And the sales were genuine: "We acquired four paying customers", producing "four-figure recurring revenue and five-figure custom development revenue". Chapter four asked what those milestones did to her stopping rule. This chapter asks what was underneath them.

The doctors who used it got eleven minutes back on every visit, and they "wanted to pay fewer than $100 per month" per doctor. That was the ceiling, and it was set by the customer. Underneath it, a number she did not set was moving: "the hosting and AI costs for our platform had gone up to about $70 per doctor". What had once been a margin was now something under thirty dollars a month per doctor, and every practice still had to be connected to its records system first — "a single EHR integration costs around $4,000 to $6,000". Thirty dollars a month against four thousand up front. Her own arithmetic: "it would take 11 years per practice just to break even".

The subtraction is worth doing slowly. Under $100 a month against about $70 of cost specific to that doctor leaves something under $30 — before support, before her own time, before anything else. Against the cheaper integration, $4,000, that comes back in a little over eleven years. Her estimate and the arithmetic agree.

One more detail, which will matter in a moment. Her four customers arrived by four different routes: "one customer through a cold call, one through a cold email, one through a very warm intro", and one via a consulting network. Four customers, four channels, no channel used twice. Chapter seven asked whether a channel repeats; here none was tried a second time — which shows no repeatable mechanism, not that none of them could have repeated. It also means that even perfect records would have given her four prices for four acquisitions, and no price for any channel.

Move 3 The reasoning

Four subtractions stand between revenue and value

Everything a customer pays you is gross. Four things come out before what is left is worth calling value, and each of them is routinely missed in a different way.

  • Direct delivery cost. What it costs to serve that customer, per period, because they are there. Cydoc's $70 per doctor is the cleanest possible example: a variable cost, per customer, that consumed most of the price.
  • Setup cost, charged to the customer who caused it. Integration, onboarding, migration, the custom work agreed in the last week of the sale. It is spent once, so it feels like a project cost. It belongs to that customer and is recovered from that customer or not at all.
  • Acquisition, including your own time at replacement cost. Chapter five established the rule: your labour has a price even when no money moves. A free channel that consumes two days of founder time per customer is not free, and this is where the businesses that look best on paper hide their costs.
  • Refunds, credits and concessions. The discount given to close, the month written off after a bad delivery, the invoice quietly never sent. These are reductions in what the customer was worth, and they tend to live in nobody's calculation at all.

Positive is not the same as sufficient

Something under $30 a month is a positive contribution. A business could run on it if everything else about the business were free, and nothing about a business is free. The threshold that matters is not zero — it is whether the contribution, across the customers you can actually get, covers everything that is not attributable to any one customer, and pays the owner. Cydoc's per-doctor economics were positive on the monthly line and could not carry the business, and both of those things were true at the same time for years.

This is why "are we profitable on each customer?" is the wrong first question. It admits too much. The useful question is how much is left, against how much has to be covered.

The date the money came back

The second half of the calculation is not a quantity at all. It is a date: the day on which cumulative contribution from a customer finally exceeded what was spent acquiring and starting them.

Eleven years is that date, for Cydoc, and it disqualifies the customer even though the contribution is positive and even though the product worked. A customer who repays the cost of their own setup after eleven years cannot finance the business that has to serve them for the eleven years. The previous chapter showed that a profitable customer can still be a cash problem; this is the same fact arriving from the other side, and it is why total contribution and the time it takes are not two views of one number. Two customers who each eventually contribute €2,000 are not equally valuable when one pays before delivery and the other repays their setup after a decade.

There is no average customer

Computing one value for all your customers destroys the information you needed. WP Curve, from chapter ten, shows why with a single price. Every customer paid the same $69, and the ones cancelling inside the first month had something in common: "The common thread is that all of these customers have been running blogs, rather than businesses", for whom "$69 feels like a big cost". Same price, same service, two entirely different relationships. Acquisition was not cheap for either — "no virality involved. We have to work for every single customer" — and a human had to perform the work each time.

Averaging across those two groups produces a number describing nobody, and it hides the only finding worth having: one segment was worth acquiring again and one was not. So the calculation is done per segment, per channel, or per cohort, and never once for the business.

WP Curve is a contrast, not a calculation: nothing it published can produce a customer value figure.

When you do not need a lifetime estimate

The obvious objection to everything above is that Cydoc's numbers ignore the future. Perhaps those practices would have expanded, referred others, or bought more. That objection is correct, and it does not rescue the case:

When the observed economics are already bad, you do not need a lifetime estimate. When they look good, "lifetime" is the part you know least.

An eleven-year payback does not become acceptable under a more generous forecast; it requires one, which is the tell. And a business whose observed numbers look healthy is precisely where the projection is doing invisible work, because that is where nobody checks it. The asymmetry means the honest calculation is the same in both cases: compute what has actually happened, mark the rest unknown, and notice which conclusions depend on the unknown part.

Boundaries

Cydoc cannot tell you what acquisition costs. No cost per customer is published, and four customers arriving through four routes give nothing from which a channel cost could be estimated. The narrower true statement is that Cydoc had no acquisition process to measure, not that its acquisitions had no cost. The acquisition line in the record below is real and this case does not demonstrate it.

Four customers cannot establish a lifetime, a churn rate, or a cohort. No retention conclusion is available here, nothing about expansion or referral, and the chapter does not offer one. The eleven-year figure is Draelos's own estimate from her own costs, not audited unit economics, and integration costs in medical software are unusually high — the mechanism transfers, the magnitude does not.

Future expansion is unknown, not zero — and it is also not evidence. This chapter's instrument systematically undervalues relationships whose returns genuinely arrive late, and a business that only ever counts what has already happened will refuse some customers it should have kept. That is a real cost of the method, priced nowhere in it.

And one cost has been left out of every calculation in this chapter. The delivery cost, the setup cost, the support burden — all of them assume someone is paid to do the work. You have now charged the customer for everyone's labour except one person's, and the owner is usually the most expensive omission in the record. That is the next chapter.

Monday

  1. Add the columns to the record you already have. The customer rows from chapter ten and chapter eleven gain these: cash actually collected; acquisition spend; your own acquisition time valued at replacement cost; onboarding or setup cost; direct delivery and support cost; refunds, credits and concessions. Carry the outcome status from chapter eleven across as well — renewed, lapsed, still here — because a customer whose payback date is distant and who has already left is a different finding from one who is distant and staying. Extend the document; do not start a new one.
  2. Compute contribution to date, per customer. Collected, minus everything in the list above. Only what has happened. Anything you are tempted to project goes in a separate column labelled unknown, and it stays out of the total.
  3. Find the payback date for each customer. The month in which cumulative contribution first exceeded what you spent acquiring and starting them. For customers where that date has not arrived, write "not yet" rather than a forecast — that is the honest entry and it is also the alarming one.
  4. Aggregate by segment and by channel, never once for the business. If two segments average the same and one of them contains both your best customer and your worst, you have two businesses, not one. If a channel produces customers whose payback dates are consistently later, you have found something that no revenue report would have shown you.
  5. Then ask the actual question: which of these would you acquire again? Write the answer down for each segment, with the number that justifies it. That sentence is the output of this chapter, and it is the input to every pricing and channel decision you make next year.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be a business that computed exactly this — observed contribution, payback date, by segment — refused a segment because both looked poor, and discovered later that the segment's expansion, its referrals, or the fall in its service cost as the work was learned and standardised would have made it the most valuable one. Note the tense: a refused segment is not observed afterwards, so the case has to show what the numbers would have become, which is why it is hard to find rather than merely absent.

That case would not overturn the method. It would price it, and the price is not in the arithmetic but in the observation window. Value to date protects you from inventing a future. It can also make a real but delayed future invisible. This chapter recommends the conservative error without demonstrating what it costs.
