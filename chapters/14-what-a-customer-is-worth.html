What a Customer Is Worth

Three customers each paid Lea €900. After her time, one left €350, one €100, and one cost her €300. Before she spends anything finding a fourth, she has to decide which of the three she is trying to repeat, and how much a new one is allowed to cost.

The decision Which kind of work you will seek again, on what terms, and how much money and time you will risk finding it before you review the result.

Same price, three results

Lea is invented, continued from chapter ten. D and E from chapter thirteen are a different, larger offer and are not in this comparison. Each job paid €900 and cost €50 in tools. What differs is her time. Price every hour, selling and delivery, at the same €50 she used in chapter twelve, count each hour once, and the three jobs stop looking alike.

A leaves €900 − €50 − (10 × €50) = €350. Together the three leave €150. Then add, once, the six hours of approaches that sold nothing: another €300, and the three customers together are €150 short. Leave out the failed selling and the result reverses sign.

Now the tempting conclusion, and why it is wrong. Former colleagues good, cold approaches bad reads three rows as three markets. A was one person, not a stream of buyers. B's lower result came from Lea's unclear preparation instructions, not from being an introduction. C's came from a second source system she agreed to in a call without re-quoting. What she can repeat is not a kind of person but a kind of job: a buyer who can supply one agreed input file, needs the specified output and accepts the handover and correction scope, with any second source quoted separately.

Cydoc: the setup a monthly price hid

Rachel Draelos, whose company closed in chapter four, reports that the small practices she sold to wanted to pay under $100 a month, that hosting and AI cost about $70 per doctor, and that connecting each practice's record system cost $4,000 to $6,000. Her own estimate: eleven years per practice to break even on the connection alone. Every customer paid. Each one began with a cost the monthly price would take a decade to recover.

That is the other way a customer can look profitable and not be: a setup the price never names. Lea's version is small, C's unpriced second source, but the rule is the same. Price the setup, name who funds it, and check how long the receipts take to recover it. Cash recovery is a separate test from the remainder: on invented dates, C paid on day 42 and recovered its €50 outlay despite leaving −€300 after Lea's time. A customer who has paid you back has not necessarily been worth serving.

A spending limit from the work, not from a lifetime

Start with what winning a customer has actually cost. Lea spent ten hours selling to the three who bought and six on people who did not: sixteen hours for three customers, about 5.3 hours or €267 each. Count only the buyers and it looks like 3.3 hours, €167. The failed approaches are part of the price of every customer she won, so they stay in.

Now what the next job can afford. Of the repeatable kind: €900 − €50 of tools − (8 delivery hours × €50 = €400) = €450 before selling and shared costs. Lea chooses to keep at least €200 of that towards shared costs and surprises, an invented figure for this example. €450 − €200 = €250 is the most that winning the job may cost, failed approaches included: five of her hours with no cash spend (5 × €50), or €100 of cash and three hours (€100 + 3 × €50). Not both. Her record so far, €267, is already just over it. Selling has to get cheaper, or delivery has to, before this offer pays for finding its own customers.

Then she tests the assumption the allowance rests on. At twelve delivery hours, B's figure, the job leaves €900 − €50 − (12 × €50 = €600) = €250 before selling, and keeping €200 leaves €50: one hour to win it. The allowance depends on delivery staying near eight hours, so the clearer instructions from chapter ten are not a nicety. They are what pays for the next customer.

Her first campaign gets a cap, set before it starts: €100 and three hours, which is one job's €250 allowance, or two weeks, whichever comes first. If it wins one customer, that customer cost €250, exactly the limit. If it wins none, she has spent €250 and acquired nobody. That campaign goes in the record too, even though it has no cost per customer to report. An open proposal stays pending, not a sale. At the review she records wins, losses and pending approaches and decides whether more effort is justified. The cap controls what she can lose; it does not prove the route can deliver a customer in two weeks. A slow sale can be cut off too early, and continuing has a cost too. Which routes deserve her hours over six months is the question of the optional chapter on channels.

Forecasts of later purchases, referrals and cheaper delivery may change all of this. Keep them in a separate scenario with a review date, and if the scenario is the only reason to take the work, name the most money and time you will risk finding out.

Monday · one line per customer

Do this one thing. For each customer so far, write one line: price, direct cost, your hours to win and deliver them multiplied by your rate, and what is left. Add, once at the bottom, the hours you spent on people who did not buy, and divide all your selling hours, theirs included, by the number of customers won. Circle the job you would repeat, and write in one sentence what made it that job rather than who the customer was. You now know which work to seek, and roughly what it can afford to cost you to find.

Never tracked your hours? Estimate them per job and mark each estimated; then add an hours column to your customer record so the next job is counted. That counts as finishing.

If you have another hour:

  1. Set the allowance. Price minus direct cost minus delivery hours at your rate, less what you want to keep. Then redo it with delivery 50% over, and see what is left.
  2. Cap the next campaign in money, hours and weeks, whichever comes first, and write the review date.
  3. Trace cash recovery for one customer with a setup cost: the date their payments first covered what you paid out, or not yet. Use the shared customer record (no formulas).
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Lea's jobs, hours, the €50 rate, the payment dates and her €200 and campaign cap are invented. The economic remainder is not net profit: shared software, administration and failed selling belong at period level, which is why chapter twelve reconciles them. A projected lifetime is neither an observed fact nor worthless; the question is how much the decision depends on it.

Draelos's figures are her own retrospective estimates, not audited accounts, and their units differ: the price is not stated per doctor or per practice, the hosting cost is per doctor and the recovery estimate per practice, so they cannot be combined without assumptions. Her account describes four successful sales routes, not four total attempts, so no channel average can be reconstructed from it.

When comparing groups of customers later, keep the ones who left, asked for refunds or needed corrections in their original group, and compare equal lengths of time.

Still owed: a documented business that rejected early work that looked uneconomic and later found it would have repaid its costs. Short observation windows can undervalue slow returns; separating a forecast and capping the test makes that choice visible, it does not remove it. The debt stands.

Back on the main path, chapter sixteen asks what all of this must provide for your household, and whether you want to keep doing the work it takes.
