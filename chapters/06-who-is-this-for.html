Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 6

Who Is This For

A positioning statement that costs you nothing is not a positioning statement. The test is not whether it sounds true, but whether you can name the customers it turns away — and say what refusing them buys you.

The decision Which customers you are going to stop trying to serve, and what that refusal is supposed to purchase.

Move 1 The situation

You come out of Part I holding one person who paid. Perhaps five. The whole apparatus of those five chapters — the conversations, the ladder of commitment, the stopping rule — existed to establish that a single instance of demand was real rather than polite. It established nothing about the second instance, and it gave you no way to find one except by repeating the first exactly, which is not a business but a coincidence you are trying to have twice.

The instinct at this point is to widen. If one accountant paid, then accountants; if accountants, then bookkeepers too, they have the same problem; if bookkeepers, then really any small business with invoices. Three steps of that and the sentence on your homepage is true of software in general.

This is not stupidity, it is arithmetic run backwards. Widening appears free because nothing is subtracted — the accountant is still welcome, you have merely also welcomed everyone else — and the market is now larger, which in a spreadsheet is unambiguously better. The cost is absent from the spreadsheet because it is not a cost in money. It is the loss of every tiebreaker you had.

That is what positioning actually is, and why it belongs in a book about decisions rather than one about marketing. It is not a description and not a slogan. It is the rule you consult on ordinary days, when two things both look worth doing and there is time for one. Which bug first. Which feature next. Which customer's complaint is a defect and which is a person who should be using something else. A business with a defined buyer settles those in seconds; a business without one settles them by whoever asked most recently and most loudly, which over a year produces a product shaped like its own support queue.

So the decision is not "who is this for" in the sense of who might conceivably benefit — almost anyone might. It is who you are going to disappoint on purpose.

Move 2 What actually happened

In December 2018 a developer called Uku wrote the first line of an analytics tool. "The marketing head at the company he worked for asked him to integrate Google Analytics into their landing page." He did not want to. That is the whole origin story, and it begins with a refusal rather than an opportunity.

He launched a public beta on Indie Hackers in January 2019 and paid subscriptions that May, finishing the month at $64 MRR. Then, in his co-founder's later summary of the period: "It took us 324 days to reach the first $400 monthly recurring revenue (MRR) after we launched the paid subscriptions SaaS business in May 2019." The published ladder for that year is short — "July: First traffic spike ($118 MRR)", "September: Embracing the open source world ($178 MRR)", and by February 2020, $403.

In that month he went looking for help. "After a few months of relatively stagnant growth," he announced that he had been looking for a partner since the beginning. The partner was Marko Saric, who wrote the account this chapter is built on: "This other person was me" — and, he adds, "Uku cold-emailed me after reading a blog post I published about how to degoogle a website." A marketer, found because of something he had written about the exact subject the product was about.

"I started working with Uku on March 16th, 2020 … we had 49 visitors in total, and 0 from Google search the day before I joined." Forty-nine visitors, ten months after launch, at $433 a month. What happened in the first few weeks is listed plainly, and the list matters more than any single item on it:

  • "Product relaunch (one example being that the early version of Plausible used one first-party cookie)"
  • "New brand positioning (simple, lightweight, open source and privacy-first Google Analytics alternative)"
  • "Streamlining the communication to send a consistent message everywhere"
  • "Improving the site structure"
  • "Adding a lot of new product-related content"

Then: "On April 8th, I published my first Plausible blog post. Our new positioning was based on how different we are compared to Google Analytics, so we decided to pick a fight." The post was "Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website". It reached the top of Hacker News. "More than 25,000 people visited our site on the day we published the post." And: "We broke all our records in April: most traffic, trials and the biggest MRR increase."

At the time of writing they were a "completely independent, self-funded and bootstrapped team of four", describing themselves as "intentionally small, profitable and sustainable", with "More than 7,000 paying subscribers" and stats on "more than 50,000 websites with more than a billion monthly page views". They add that they have "never paid to advertise for Plausible": "Our growth comes organically from word of mouth."

Move 3 The reasoning

Read the positioning statement as a list of things given up

Take the sentence they wrote in March 2020 and take it apart: "simple, lightweight, open source and privacy-first Google Analytics alternative".

The noun phrase does the first work. Google Analytics alternative does not describe the product, it describes the buyer: someone who uses a specific named thing and is unhappy about it. That person can be found, because they are already looking and already have a word for what they want.

Then four adjectives, and this is the part worth being slow about. Each one is a refusal, and each refusal has a bill attached.

  • Simple, in 2020, meant refusing the advanced reporting and configuration larger marketing teams expect — funnels, cohorts, segments, multi-touch attribution. Not a fringe: most of the people who care enough about analytics to pay for it. Read the date, because the refusal did not hold forever — their documentation now lists funnel analysis, custom properties and ecommerce tracking. That is a second lesson inside the chapter, not a contradiction of it; see below.
  • Lightweight gives up feature depth as a matter of physics. A small script is small because it does less. You cannot later add the heavy thing without breaking the claim.
  • Open source gives up the licensing moat and actively invites the customer not to pay: anyone may run it themselves for nothing. They had already made this one in September 2019 — "Embracing the open source world" — at $178 MRR, which is the point at which giving revenue away is least affordable and easiest to do.
  • Privacy-first gives up the way the incumbent is funded. Their reading of why the incumbent costs nothing, and it is theirs rather than this book's: "Google's products are free to use because Google has built its wealth by collecting huge amounts of personal information and using these personal and behavioral insights to sell targeted advertising." Refusing that does not logically leave only one way to make money — grants, sponsorship, services and cross-subsidy all remain. What it left them was revenue from users rather than from their users' visitors — the model they chose and still describe: "Funded entirely by our subscribers." You must charge, so you must be worth paying for.

Now the chronology, because it is easy to get backwards and this chapter got it backwards in draft. The positioning statement did not create these choices. Every one of them already existed in March 2020: paid subscriptions since May 2019, the open-source licence since September, the privacy argument in the founder's head since the day he was asked to install the incumbent. What the sentence did was gather four scattered decisions into one line and make them binding. Before it, each was a preference that could quietly have been traded away on a bad month. After it, trading any one of them away meant breaking a public promise.

That is the general lesson, and it is more useful than the version where positioning invents the business. The model arrives in fragments; the positioning turns the fragments into constraints. A statement that binds that hard is a decision. A statement you could delete without changing a single downstream choice is a slogan.

Constraints can be renegotiated later, at a price, which is what the funnels are. Refusing advanced reporting at $433 a month is cheap — you have no such customers to lose. Adding it at $83,637 trades some of the distinctness that got you there for buyers you had previously sent away. The refusal bought the growth; relaxing it is what the growth then paid for. The second decision is only available to businesses that made the first.

The test

Name three kinds of customer who should not buy from you. Not ones you cannot serve yet — ones you have decided against, who have money and a real problem, and whom you are sending elsewhere on purpose.

If the three come easily, you have a position. If the honest answer is that anyone with the problem is welcome, you have a description, and it will not break a single tie for you next Tuesday. Plausible's three write themselves: anyone running paid acquisition who needs cross-channel attribution; anyone who wants session recordings; anyone whose employer has standardised on the incumbent and does not get a vote. Between them, the large majority of the analytics market — given away deliberately, at $433 a month, by two people drawing no salary.

Picking a fight is a technique, not a principle

The April post worked because there was a named incumbent to be against, and that part does not generalise. An incumbent hands you three things at once: a search term people already type, a comparison a reader can settle in ten seconds, and a reason for someone else to find the argument worth linking to. Where no dominant thing exists that people are quietly unhappy with, the move is simply unavailable — enthusiasm will not conjure an opponent. Positioning by refusal still applies; positioning by opposition does not. Do not read the Hacker News result as evidence for the general case when it is evidence for the special one.

How much of this was positioning?

Less than the curve suggests, and the chapter is obliged to say so before the reader gets there first. Five things changed in March 2020 and positioning was one; the others were a product relaunch, message consistency, site structure and a volume of new content. A sixth is not on the list: the company went from one person to two, and the second was a specialist in the function that had been missing. Any of those could carry part of the result. There is no version of this case in which positioning is isolated, because nobody isolated it.

The arithmetic is gentler than the story, too. On their own figures, $64 in May 2019 to $403 in February 2020 is about 23% a month, compounding; the four months after are about 45%, and the year to February 2021 settles near 37%. A real and large change — roughly a sustained doubling of the rate — but not a flat line becoming a vertical one. It is compounding that was already happening, reaching numbers big enough to notice.

Which makes their phrase "a few months of relatively stagnant growth" the most useful sentence in the source. Twenty-three percent a month is not stagnation, but at $178 MRR it adds forty-one dollars, and forty-one dollars feels like nothing — so a founder compounding healthily can read his own dashboard and conclude he is stuck. Some of what positioning bought here was arithmetic and some was the ability to see it. Only the first is what this chapter is selling.

Boundaries

Repositioning followed by acceleration is not an isolated cause. Five simultaneous changes and a new co-founder. Anyone claiming this proves the return on positioning claims more than the document supports, including me.

This is narrowing by value, not by industry. Plausible narrowed on what the product refuses to do. Most readers here will narrow on who the customer is — one trade, one size of company, one country. Different manoeuvre, different failure mode, not tested here.

The mechanism was content and search, which is the next chapter's subject. Positioning told them what to say and to whom, not where to stand. Forty-nine visitors became twenty-five thousand because someone wrote a post and put it in front of the right crowd.

One survivor, self-reported, two years after the fact. Nobody keeps a register of businesses that repositioned sharply and died, and a retrospective written at $1M ARR is a story told by the winner about why he won.

Monday

  1. Write the sentence. "[Product] is a [category] for [who] who [what they refuse to accept]." One line. Then strike out every adjective in it that costs you nothing — the ones no competitor would claim the opposite of. What survives is your actual position, and it may be nothing yet.
  2. Name the three you turn away. Real kinds of customer, with money, whose problem you could technically solve. If you cannot get to three, go back to the sentence; it is not doing any work.
  3. Find your incumbent. What is the buyer using today — a competitor, a spreadsheet, an agency, nothing at all? Write the honest comparison against it, including the column where they win. No incumbent: skip the fight, keep the refusals.
  4. Trace the sentence into the accounts. Does your positioning force a pricing model, a support cost, a market you can no longer sell to? If the answer is no in every direction, it is decoration.
  5. Do your own compound arithmetic. Take your last nine months of revenue and work out the monthly rate. You may be growing steadily and calling it stagnant because the numbers are small. Decide from the rate, not the feeling.

Plausible on Product Hunt — the right message, and a channel that only worked once

By August 2020 the repositioning was five months old and working: "August: Product Hunt launch ($4,062 MRR)." The message was settled, the comparison content existed. Then they did what every product of that size is told to do. "We launched on Product Hunt."

The result, in their own numbers: "We got more than 1,000 visitors and 15 trial signups from Product Hunt on the launch day", and then, "only a few days later, we got fewer than 20 visitors from there." Their description of the shape is the best line in the source: "It brings that famous spike of hope after which follows the flat line of nope."

Their own verdict is more generous than that line, and the chapter has to carry both halves rather than quote the one that suits: "Product Hunt was worthwhile," they write, and also "not a sustainable channel to acquire customers in the long term." Worth the day; not worth building on.

This is a limit rather than a counter-case, and the difference is the one the method page insists on. Nothing here shows the chapter's advice to be wrong. They had done the positioning work, done it well, and the channel still returned a spike and a flat line. What it shows is that the advice is incomplete: a sharp position tells you what to say and to whom, and says nothing whatever about where to stand when you say it, or how often you get to stand there. And the defect is not that Product Hunt held the wrong people — fifteen trials in a day is not the wrong people. It is that a launch happens once, so whatever it found could not be found again the following Tuesday. Insufficient rather than misdirected.

Nor does one launch, one company and one category establish anything about launches in general. What survives is narrower and more useful: a message can be right and still have nowhere durable to be said, so the question of where, repeatably is a separate decision taken on separate evidence. That decision is chapter seven.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

Two shapes would do it. The first: a business that narrowed hard, gave up the customers, executed properly on what remained, and found the remainder too small to live on. That must happen constantly, and is unfindable for the reason chapters two and three both hit — the people it happens to stop, and stopping is not written up.

The second is more damaging and ought to be easier. A business with published monthly revenue that deliberately declined to narrow, stayed general on purpose, and grew anyway. Plenty of general-purpose tools succeed; what is missing is one whose founder both says so explicitly and publishes the numbers, so that the claim can be checked rather than asserted. Until that case is found, this chapter rests on one company that repositioned and won, told by the person who did the repositioning. The debt stands, and it is larger than chapter three's.
