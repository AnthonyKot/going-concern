Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 6

Who Is This For

A positioning statement that costs you nothing is not a positioning statement. The test is not whether it sounds true. It is whether you can name, out loud, the customers it turns away — and say what refusing them buys you.

The decision Which customers you are going to stop trying to serve, and what that refusal is supposed to purchase.

Move 1 The situation

You come out of Part I holding one person who paid. Perhaps five. The whole apparatus of those five chapters — the conversations, the ladder of commitment, the stopping rule — existed to establish that a single instance of demand was real rather than polite. It established nothing about the second instance, and it gave you no way to find one except by repeating the first exactly, which is not a business but a coincidence you are trying to have twice.

The instinct at this point is to widen. If one accountant paid, then accountants; if accountants, then bookkeepers too, they have the same problem; if bookkeepers, then really any small business with invoices. Three steps of that and the sentence on your homepage is true of software in general.

This is not stupidity. It is arithmetic run in the wrong direction. Widening appears free because nothing is subtracted: the accountant is still welcome, you have merely also welcomed everyone else. And the market you are addressing is now larger, which in a spreadsheet is unambiguously better. The cost does not appear in the spreadsheet because it is not a cost in money. It is the loss of every tiebreaker you had.

That is what positioning actually is, and why it belongs in a book about decisions rather than a book about marketing. It is not a description of your product and it is not a slogan. It is the rule you consult on the ordinary days, when two things both look worth doing and there is time for one. Which bug first. Which feature next. Which of two words in a sentence. Which customer's complaint is a defect and which is a person who should be using something else. A business with a defined buyer resolves those in seconds. A business without one resolves them by whoever asked most recently and most loudly, which over a year produces a product shaped like its own support queue.

So the decision is not "who is this for" in the sense of who might conceivably benefit. Almost everyone might conceivably benefit from almost anything. The decision is who you are going to disappoint on purpose.

Move 2 What actually happened

In December 2018 a developer called Uku wrote the first line of an analytics tool. "Uku is a developer. The marketing head at the company he worked for asked him to integrate Google Analytics into their landing page." He did not want to. That is the entire origin story, and it is worth noticing that it begins with a refusal rather than an opportunity.

He launched a public beta on Indie Hackers in January 2019 and paid subscriptions that May, finishing the month at $64 MRR. Then, in his co-founder's later summary of the period: "It took us 324 days to reach the first $400 monthly recurring revenue (MRR) after we launched the paid subscriptions SaaS business in May 2019." The published ladder for that year is short — "July: First traffic spike ($118 MRR)", "September: Embracing the open source world ($178 MRR)", and by February 2020, $403.

In that month he went looking for help. "After a few months of relatively stagnant growth," he announced that he had been looking for a partner since the beginning. The partner was Marko Saric, who wrote the account this chapter is built on: "This other person was me" — and, he adds, "Uku cold-emailed me after reading a blog post I published about how to degoogle a website." A marketer, found because of something he had written about the exact subject the product was about.

"I started working with Uku on March 16th, 2020. I remember looking at our stats in the Plausible dashboard; we had 49 visitors in total, and 0 from Google search the day before I joined." Forty-nine visitors, ten months after launch, at $433 a month.

What happened in the first few weeks is listed plainly, and the list matters more than any single item on it:

  • "Product relaunch (one example being that the early version of Plausible used one first-party cookie)"
  • "New brand positioning (simple, lightweight, open source and privacy-first Google Analytics alternative)"
  • "Streamlining the communication to send a consistent message everywhere"
  • "Improving the site structure"
  • "Adding a lot of new product-related content"

Then: "On April 8th, I published my first Plausible blog post. Our new positioning was based on how different we are compared to Google Analytics, so we decided to pick a fight." The post was "Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website". It reached the top of Hacker News. "More than 25,000 people visited our site on the day we published the post." And: "We broke all our records in April: most traffic, trials and the biggest MRR increase."

At the time of writing they were a "completely independent, self-funded and bootstrapped team of four", describing themselves as "intentionally small, profitable and sustainable", with "More than 7,000 paying subscribers" and stats on "more than 50,000 websites with more than a billion monthly page views". They add that they have "never paid to advertise for Plausible": "Our growth comes organically from word of mouth."

Move 3 The reasoning

Read the positioning statement as a list of things given up

Take the sentence they wrote in March 2020 and take it apart: "simple, lightweight, open source and privacy-first Google Analytics alternative".

The noun phrase does the first piece of work. Google Analytics alternative does not describe the product; it describes the buyer. It says: you are someone who currently uses a specific named thing and is unhappy about it. That is a person who can be found, because they are already looking, and already have a word for what they want.

Then four adjectives, and this is the part worth being slow about. Each one is a refusal, and each refusal has a bill attached.

  • Simple gives up everyone who needs funnels, cohorts, segments and multi-touch attribution. That is not a fringe — it is most of the people who care enough about analytics to pay for it.
  • Lightweight gives up feature depth as a matter of physics. A small script is small because it does less. You cannot later add the heavy thing without breaking the claim.
  • Open source gives up the licensing moat and actively invites the customer not to pay: anyone may run it themselves for nothing. They had already made this one in September 2019 — "Embracing the open source world" — at $178 MRR, which is the point at which giving revenue away is least affordable and easiest to do.
  • Privacy-first gives up the incumbent's entire business model. Google Analytics is free because the data is the product. Choosing privacy-first is choosing to have no revenue except money from users, which means you must charge, which means you must be worth paying for.

That last one is the general lesson, and it runs the opposite way from how positioning is usually taught. The positioning did not follow from the business model. It determined it. Once the sentence said privacy-first, the free-with-advertising path was closed, the price had to be a real price, and the product had to justify one. A statement that reaches that far into the accounts is a decision. A statement that could be deleted without changing a single downstream choice is a slogan.

The test

Name three kinds of customer who should not buy from you. Not customers you cannot serve yet — customers you have decided against, who have money, whose problem is real, and whom you are sending elsewhere on purpose.

If the three names come easily, you have a position. If the honest answer is that anyone with the problem is welcome, you have a description, and it will not break a single tie for you next Tuesday. Plausible's three are easy to write: anyone running paid acquisition who needs cross-channel attribution; anyone who wants session recordings and user-level trails; anyone whose employer has standardised on the incumbent and does not get a vote. Between them that is the large majority of the analytics market, given away deliberately, at $433 a month, by two people with no salary.

Picking a fight is a technique, not a principle

The April post worked because there was a named incumbent to be against. That is worth separating from the rest of the chapter, because it is the part that does not generalise. An incumbent gives you three things at once: a search term people already type, a comparison a reader can evaluate in ten seconds, and a reason for a third party to find the argument interesting enough to link to.

If your market has no dominant thing that people are quietly unhappy with, the move is unavailable, and no amount of enthusiasm will conjure an opponent. Positioning by refusal still applies; positioning by opposition does not. Do not read the Hacker News result as evidence for the general case when it is evidence for the special one.

How much of this was positioning?

Less than the shape of the curve suggests, and the chapter is obliged to say so before the reader gets there first.

Five things changed in March 2020, of which positioning was one. The other four were a product relaunch, message consistency, site structure and a volume of new content. And a sixth thing changed that is not on the list: the company went from one person to two, and the second was a specialist in exactly the function that had been missing. Any of those alone could carry part of the result. There is no version of this case in which positioning is isolated, because nobody isolated it.

The arithmetic is also gentler than the story. Taking their own published figures: $64 in May 2019 to $403 in February 2020 is about 23% a month, compounding. The four months after are about 45% a month, and the year to February 2021 settles near 37%. That is a real and large change — roughly a doubling of the growth rate, sustained — but it is not a flat line becoming a vertical one. It is compounding that was already happening, doubling in rate and finally reaching numbers big enough to notice.

Which makes their own phrase, "a few months of relatively stagnant growth", the most useful sentence in the source. Twenty-three percent a month is not stagnation. At $178 MRR it adds forty-one dollars, and forty-one dollars feels like nothing, so a founder who is compounding healthily can look at his own dashboard and conclude he is stuck. Some of what positioning bought here was arithmetic; some of it was the ability to see the arithmetic. Both are worth having, but only the first is what this chapter is selling.

Boundaries

The case shows a repositioning followed by acceleration; it does not isolate the cause. Five simultaneous changes and a new co-founder. Anyone claiming this proves the return on positioning is claiming more than the document supports, including me.

This is narrowing by value, not by industry. Plausible narrowed on what the product refuses to do. Most readers of this book will be narrowing on who the customer is — one trade, one size of company, one country. That is a different manoeuvre with a different failure mode, and it is not tested here.

The mechanism was content and search, which is the next chapter's subject. Positioning told them what to say and whom to say it to. It did not tell them where to stand. The forty-nine visitors became twenty-five thousand because someone wrote a post and put it in front of the right crowd.

One survivor, self-reported, two years after the fact. Nobody keeps a register of businesses that repositioned sharply and died, and a retrospective written at $1M ARR is a story told by the winner about why he won.

Monday

  1. Write the sentence. "[Product] is a [category] for [who] who [what they refuse to accept]." One line. Then strike out every adjective in it that costs you nothing — the ones no competitor would claim the opposite of. What survives is your actual position, and it may be nothing yet.
  2. Name the three you turn away. Real kinds of customer, with money, whose problem you could technically solve. If you cannot get to three, go back to the sentence; it is not doing any work.
  3. Find your incumbent. What is the buyer using today — a competitor, a spreadsheet, an agency, an intern, nothing at all? Write the honest comparison page against it, including the column where they win. If there is no incumbent, skip the fight and keep the refusals.
  4. Trace the sentence into the accounts. Does your positioning force a pricing model, a support cost, a market you can no longer sell to? If the answer is no in every direction, it is decoration.
  5. Do your own compound arithmetic. Take your last nine months of revenue and work out the monthly rate. You may be growing steadily and calling it stagnant because the absolute numbers are small. Decide what to change from the rate, not from the feeling.

Plausible on Product Hunt — the positioning was right and the channel still paid nothing

By August 2020 the repositioning was five months old and working: "August: Product Hunt launch ($4,062 MRR)." The message was settled, the fight had been picked, the comparison content existed. Then they did the thing that every product of that size is told to do. "We launched on Product Hunt."

The result, in their own numbers: "We got more than 1,000 visitors and 15 trial signups from Product Hunt on the launch day", and then, "only a few days later, we got fewer than 20 visitors from there." Their verdict is that it is "not a sustainable channel to acquire customers in the long term", and their description of the shape is the best line in the source: "It brings that famous spike of hope after which follows the flat line of nope."

This is a limit rather than a counter-case, and the difference is the one the method page insists on. Nothing here shows the chapter's advice to be wrong. They had done the positioning work, done it well, and the channel still returned a spike and a flat line. What it shows is that the advice is incomplete: a sharp position tells you what to say and to whom, and says nothing whatever about where to stand when you say it. The same message, four months earlier, put in front of Hacker News, produced twenty-five thousand visitors and a record month. Put in front of Product Hunt it produced fifteen trials.

Note also what it does not establish — that Product Hunt is a bad channel, or that launches never work. One launch, one company, one category. What survives is narrower and more useful: the same positioning can be worth a record month in one place and nothing in another, which means the place is a separate decision, taken on separate evidence. That decision is chapter seven.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

Two shapes would do it. The first: a business that narrowed hard, gave up the customers, executed properly on the remainder — and found the remainder too small to live on. That must happen constantly. It is unfindable for the reason chapters two and three both ran into: the people it happens to stop, and stopping is not written up.

The second is more damaging and ought to be easier. A business with published monthly revenue that deliberately declined to narrow, stayed general on purpose, and grew anyway. Plenty of general-purpose tools succeed; what is missing is one whose founder both says so explicitly and publishes the numbers, so that the claim can be checked rather than asserted. Until that case is found, this chapter rests on one company that repositioned and won, told by the person who did the repositioning. The debt stands, and it is larger than chapter three's.
