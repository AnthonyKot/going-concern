Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 6

A Buyer You Can Name

In October 2014 Nathan Barry's email tool was earning $1,207 a month, half its peak, after more than a year of decline. His books were paying; the tool was not. He wrote down the choice: "Either give ConvertKit the attention it deserved or shut it down". What he did next was not a launch or an advertising budget. He picked one kind of buyer and started writing to them one at a time. Who exactly are you selling to, and what will you ask them?

The decision Which buyers you will pursue and which you will send elsewhere, how you will reach the first few you can name, and what you will ask them.

ConvertKit: from $1,207, one email at a time

ConvertKit — Nathan Barry, 2013–2015

In late 2012 Barry set himself a public goal: "to start a brand new SaaS company and grow it to $5,000 in monthly recurring revenue within 6 months", and "I could only make an initial investment of $5,000." He built an email marketing tool. He missed: "July 1st, 2013 arrived and ConvertKit was only at $2,480/month", and "$2,480 was the peak month."

Then the slide. He went back to selling his books, which earned well. He tried teaching beginners to build an email list, and it attracted the wrong people: "these beginners complained the most about price", and "We were actually driving away our best customers and focusing on those least likely to succeed." By October 2014 the tool was at $1,207 a month.

He did two things at once. The first was a buyer. "The segment we chose was email marketing for authors", because his most successful customers wrote and sold books or courses, as he did. It was a focus, not a wall: "We wouldn’t turn away customers from outside the market, but this would give us a focused audience to pursue."

The second was asking. He began "tracking every conversation in a ConvertKit Sales Trello board", after a friend watched him do it for the first time and said he had "been trying to get you to do that for over a year." Then: "we contacted all the top sellers on LeanPub and Udemy." The yield: "Out of about a hundred personal emails, we picked up 5-7 customers."

The conversations kept ending the same way. The prospect liked it, but "switching email providers is so much work." Out of desperation he offered to move them himself, "and do it all for you. For free." They agreed. Looking back, he saw what had happened: "The prospect placed all their possible objections on a single thing: the cost of switching." Removing it became a standing service, and the accounts that came through it stayed: "the churn on accounts that go through a migration is around 1.5% rather than the more typical 5.5%."

The asking also corrected the buyer. Authors turned out to be the customers who "tended to be on the smallest accounts, needed the most help, and were the most likely to cancel". He knew because he was talking to them, so the focus moved again, to professional bloggers, with the instruction "Focus even more on direct sales." In January 2015 he put $50,000 of his own money in and hired a developer. In March 2015 the tool reached $5,020 a month: the goal he had set for six months, reached in twenty-six.

Five to seven customers from a hundred emails is the realistic figure, and it is his. A hundred honest emails and five customers is the advertised result, not a sign you are bad at this. The rest of the chapter takes the two things he did at once, the buyer and the asking, one at a time.

Plausible: a position is a list of refusals

A buyer definition is only useful if it breaks ties. Which bug first, which feature next, which complaint is a defect and which comes from someone who should be using something else. A business with a defined buyer settles those in seconds. One without settles them by whoever asked most recently and loudest, and within a year the product is shaped like its support queue. So the real question is not who might benefit, since almost anyone might. It is whom you will disappoint on purpose.

Plausible Analytics — Uku and Marko Saric, 2019–2022

Plausible began with a refusal. "The marketing head at the company he worked for asked him to integrate Google Analytics into their landing page", and the developer, Uku, did not want to. He built an alternative. "It took us 324 days to reach the first $400 monthly recurring revenue (MRR)". When the marketer Marko Saric joined in March 2020, the product earned $433 a month and "we had 49 visitors in total, and 0 from Google search the day before I joined."

They wrote one line: "simple, lightweight, open source and privacy-first Google Analytics alternative". On 8 April Saric published "Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website". "More than 25,000 people visited our site on the day we published the post." Two years later: "June 2nd: $1M ARR ($83,637 MRR)", from a "completely independent, self-funded and bootstrapped team of four" that had "never paid to advertise for Plausible".

Read the line as things given up. Google Analytics alternative describes the buyer, not the product: someone who uses a named thing and is unhappy with it, and who can be found because they already have a word for what they want. Then each adjective refuses somebody. Simple refused the funnels, cohorts and attribution that larger marketing teams expect. Lightweight refused feature depth. Open source invited anyone to run it for nothing, a choice made in September 2019 at $178 a month. Privacy-first refused the way the incumbent is paid for, leaving a product "Funded entirely by our subscribers". Two people drawing no salary gave most of the analytics market away, and that was the point: every choice was now a public promise that could not quietly be traded away on a bad month.

The two cases do different things, and you need not do both. Focus is choosing whom you approach first, whose problems shape the next feature, and whose words go on the page. It refuses nobody: ConvertKit still sold to whoever came. Refusal is deciding what you will not build or promise, which sends some buyers elsewhere whether you mean it to or not. Plausible's refusals send away anyone who needs cross-channel attribution, anyone who wants session recordings, anyone whose employer has standardised on the incumbent. A café's, to invent them: the seven o'clock full English, the three-hour laptop on one flat white, the hen party, so the room turns its tables at lunch.

Every business needs a focus; most readers will set it on who the buyer is, one trade, one size, one street, as Barry did. A refusal is worth writing down when it protects something: a price you can deliver at, a promise you can keep, the hours you have. If you cannot name whom to approach first, you have a description rather than a buyer, and it will not break a single tie next Tuesday. Attacking a named incumbent, as Plausible's April post did, is a narrower tool: it needs an incumbent people are quietly unhappy with. Where there is none, keep the focus and skip the fight.

A few people you can name, and how to reach each

Barry's prospecting rule is to keep narrowing past the point of comfort: not bloggers, not recipe blogs, but "Paleo recipe blogs run by women". The reason is not tidiness. "The smaller the circle you target the more likely your prospects know each other", so the first customer is evidence to the second, and being unknown everywhere becomes being conspicuous in one small place.

You do not need a hundred names to start. You need a few people you could identify today, and for each, one plausible way to reach them: the email address of a former colleague's new firm, the forum or association where they ask questions, the newsletter they read, the counter they stand at. If you already have a customer, write down exactly how they arrived, not "word of mouth" but whose word, where. Failing to list many names does not by itself mean your buyer is too broad. It may mean you have not yet learned where they are, which is the next thing to find out. Later, when you have a few customers and a record of how each arrived, the optional chapter on channels asks which routes deserve your hours for the next six months.

Lea, the invented developer from chapter one, writes her line: automated weekly reports for small consultancies that rebuild the same client report by hand every week. It turns away the firm that wants a live dashboard, the one that wants its source systems changed, and the one that wants someone to maintain its data indefinitely: each has money and a real problem, and each would pull her away from a job she can price. Then she names five people: two former colleagues now at small consultancies, a contact one of them has mentioned, and two firms whose partners write publicly about their reporting, reachable by a short email. Chapter ten picks her up after three of these routes have become paid jobs.

Ask about the frustration, and listen for the one objection

Every closed tab is a no with no reason attached. Barry puts it bluntly: "When you sell online you are getting rejected dozens of times without hearing a reason." A person you have asked directly is different: "The basic rules of social interactions require them to give you a reason". Asking is not mainly a way to make this month's number. It is the only instrument you own that turns a no into a sentence.

The email he says he has sent over a thousand times opens: "Is anything frustrating you with MailChimp?", then one sentence on what he runs and who else uses it, and nothing else. No offer, no price, no link. "The point is to start a conversation, not to make a sale." It is chapter two's instrument pointed at a stranger, with money now plausibly on the table. Across a counter it becomes: where do you get your coffee now, and what annoys you about it? To someone who asked the price and went quiet: what stopped you? And follow up; Barry names the mistake he made repeatedly: "You must follow-up."

What you are listening for is not a list of complaints but one load-bearing objection, the one everything else rests on, like the cost of switching. No analytics package will show it to you, because the people who have it leave without typing anything. When you think you have found it, test it: remove it for one customer, by hand, and see whether they buy. A stated objection can be a polite stand-in for a different one.

Word of mouth will not do this for you. Barry once asked a founder whose company had grown from $2,400 to $50,000 a month what drove it, and was told word of mouth, which is not a "magic lever that you can just pull to get growth". It is what having customers produces, not a way of getting them. What is available at thirty customers is asking people one at a time.

It has one cost worth knowing now. When Barry went abroad for two weeks in January 2015, the team handled support and customers without him, and the selling stopped: "Growth stalls out when I go on vacation." Asking has you built into it; chapter seventeen comes back to that much later.

For now you have a buyer you can name and a way to reach them. The people who answer your question will soon ask one of their own: what exactly are you offering, and what does it cost? Chapter nine is about the answer.

Monday · one line, three names

Do this one thing. Finish the line: “[Product] is for [who] who [what they refuse to accept].” Then write the names of three people it fits, each with one way you could reach them this week. You now hold a buyer you can name and three first conversations.

Cannot name three? Name one, and next to it the place where people like them ask questions or buy today: a forum, an association, a supplier, a shop. Finding out where they are is the week's work, and one name with one place counts as finishing.

If you have another hour:

  1. Send each of the three one question: what is frustrating you about [what they use now]? No pitch, no price, no link. Keep a record, talked to, waiting on, dead, with a follow-up date beside each.
  2. Name what you will not build or promise, and one kind of customer with money and the problem that it sends elsewhere. Skip this if nothing needs protecting yet.
  3. Build a longer list from the same line: as many names as you can find by hand, up to a hundred, for the weeks after this one.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

ConvertKit's history comes from Barry's March 2015 post, written before the outcome was known, which reports the missed goal and the decline as fully as the recovery. The method, the email, the switching objection and the churn figures come from his 2017 retrospective, written from the far side of success. Narrowing and asking arrived together, with a later investment and a hire, so the recovery cannot be assigned to either alone. Nor was he doing this on nothing: the books were paying. The post says MRR "had grown 23% to $1,646" by the end of October; from the $1,207 bottom that rise is 36%, so the 23% presumably starts from an unstated September figure. It dates the $50,000 to 1 January 2014, which must be 2015. The fortnight abroad is one occasion, self-attributed, and not separated from the season.

Plausible's figures come almost entirely from one account by one of its two founders, written two years later. Five things changed in March 2020, positioning among them, alongside a product relaunch, consistent messaging, a new site structure and new content, and the company went from one person to two. Positioning is not isolated in this case. On their own ladder, growth was about 23% a month before the change and about 34% in the year after it: a real change, not a flat line becoming vertical. The 2020 refusal of advanced reporting was later relaxed; their documentation now lists funnels, custom properties and ecommerce tracking.

Still owed: a business that narrowed deliberately and found the remainder too small to live on, or one that stayed general on purpose and grew because of it; and someone who asked properly, hundreds of times, and was misled, for example by building the removal of a stated objection that turned out to stand in for a fatal one. Every published sales account is written by someone for whom asking worked, so the base rate is unknown. The debt stands.
