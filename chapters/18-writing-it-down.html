Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 18

Writing It Down

Chapter seventeen ranked what stops when you are unavailable and found operational knowledge the slowest of the four to fix, because knowledge that lives only in your head cannot be delegated by deciding to delegate it. Writing it down is the obvious remedy. Whether it actually transfers anything is a separate question, and the act of writing does not answer it — only someone else using the document without you in the room does.

The decision Which pieces of what only you currently know are procedure — checklists, verification steps, debugging flows a competent stranger could follow — worth writing down this week, and which pieces are judgment a document will not carry, so you stop budgeting for a transfer that will not happen.

Move 1 The situation

You have people around you now — an assistant, a contractor, a first hire, however you got there — and the day still runs through you. At some point this week, more than one of them needed something out of your head: how to handle a specific kind of refund, which supplier will actually bend, why the deploy script has that one flag nobody remembers adding. You answered, they carried on, and tomorrow someone will ask a version of the same question, because the answer lived nowhere except the exchange that just happened.

The instinct is to write it down, and the instinct is not wrong. It is also, left unexamined, the single most comfortable form of avoidance available to an owner who does not actually want to hand the decision over. A document takes an afternoon, produces something to point at, and asks nothing of anyone else. Handing a task over for real means watching someone else do it worse than you would, live, on a case that might notice — which costs something a checklist written alone in your own office never does.

So the question here is not should you write things down — you should, and most of what follows assumes you will. It is narrower and less comfortable: which of the things only you know is actually procedure, transferable by a document, and which is judgment a document will not carry no matter how well it is written — and how you find out which is which before you have spent a quarter documenting the half that was never going to transfer.

Move 2 What actually happened

Eran Galperin is the founder this book has already used twice for a different question — chapter four took his stopping rule, chapter five took his transition to full time. Gymdesk, his gym-management software, is self-funded B2B SaaS at close to the reader's scale, and by his own account he ran it alone at the start: "for the first 5 years of the company's existence, I was doing everything. From product, to development, to customer service, to operations." The company eventually outgrew that shape entirely. In a 2023 post about what came after, he describes "the process I went through growing Gymdesk from a one-person operation to a company with over 10 FTE at that point in time."

What he expected hiring to do was straightforward: take the smaller, lower-level tasks off him so he could focus elsewhere. What happened instead, by his own account, is the paradox this chapter exists to explain. More employees produced more work reaching him, not less, because the questions that were trivial for him were not trivial for anyone he hired to take them. The gap showed up first as a timing problem, on tasks he had underestimated: "Tasks that would take 15-20 minutes of my day as a side activity, required hours for a dedicated person to complete." Three to four times the duration, on the same task, in the hands of someone hired specifically to take it off him.

By his own account, the reason was not that the people he hired were incapable. It was that the knowledge required to do the job at his speed had never existed anywhere except inside him, built up over years rather than written anywhere: "What I failed to take into consideration was that I can transfer knowledge, but not the intuition and cross functional experience with all aspects of the business that came with years of performing all roles in the company." Knowledge is what a document can hold. The years of doing every job — the fast pattern-matching that lets a founder resolve an ambiguous customer complaint in two minutes because he has already seen forty variants of it — is not, and no format he tried made it so.

He built the structure anyway, for the part that was actually procedure. By his own account: "I started also documenting all of the knowledge I accumulated for each function of the business," in a Google Drive with a folder per department, an internal employee handbook, and recorded weekly engineering walkthroughs of how parts of the system had evolved. The one piece described precisely enough to judge as a mechanism rather than an intention is the customer-service knowledge base: "Creating an internal customer service knowledge-base / SOPs, containing verification and debugging flows that are repeatable and can be used immediately by new reps. Reps input a new entry every time they encounter a ticket and we collaboratively document the steps to resolve it." The document is not written once by the founder and handed down. It is written by the people using it, on the case in front of them, and it grows only where it is actually needed.

Read the two findings against each other rather than separately, because together they are the whole chapter in miniature. A documented rep could do the task at all — that is real transfer, and it is not nothing. She also took three to four times as long as the founder who had built the product, on his own telling, in a post with every incentive to report only the win. That is the honest measure of what a document moved and what it left behind.

Move 3 The reasoning

Sort before you write

Chapter seventeen split what depends on you into four kinds and assigned two of them a remedy that has nothing to do with documentation: credentials fail mechanically and are fixed with authorised redundancy and a tested recovery procedure; a relationship fails socially and is fixed only by another person spending months in the room. This chapter is about the other two — recurring work that turns out to be procedure, and the operational knowledge behind it — and the first move is separating what is actually procedure from what only looks like it because you have never had to explain it out loud.

Procedure is a sequence a competent stranger could follow to the same result, in a written order, every time. Verification steps, a debugging flow, a checklist for a task that has the same shape each time it recurs — Galperin's customer-service knowledge base is procedure, which is exactly why it partly worked: a rep with no history at the company could pick it up and produce a correct outcome. Judgment is what changes the sequence depending on the case — reading what a customer actually means underneath an ambiguous complaint, guessing which of six possible causes is the live one before checking any of them, knowing which rule to break. This is what Galperin names directly as the thing that did not transfer: the intuition and cross-functional experience built by years of doing every job, which a document can describe but not install.

The dividing line is not skill level and it is not importance — some judgment calls are trivial and some procedures are the most important thing the business does. The dividing line is whether the task is the same shape twice. If it is, write it down, and expect a competent stranger to be slower than you at first — three to four times slower, on Galperin's own evidence, is not a failure of the document, it is the cost of the transfer actually happening — and expect that gap to close with repetition rather than with more documentation. If the task changes shape with context, a document will describe your instincts without transplanting them, and the honest move is to say so rather than keep rewriting the document until it feels complete.

Documentation is the comfortable kind of avoidance

This chapter's whole subject is dangerous in a specific way: it has no deadline, no confrontation, and produces a real artefact at the end of it, which lets it read as progress while substituting for the harder thing you are actually avoiding. I'll delegate once it's properly documented is not a plan — it is a stopping condition with no way to fail it, since a document can always be made more complete and completeness was never the thing that mattered. Writing at your own desk, alone, costs you nothing socially and asks nothing of anyone else; watching someone else use what you wrote, on a live case, where they might get it visibly wrong, costs both.

The mechanical defence: a document does not count as written down until a named other person has used it, unsupervised, on a live instance of the task, and you have recorded whether it worked and how long it took. Not reviewed it, not nodded at it in a meeting — used it, with you out of the room. Until that has happened, what you have is a comforting draft, and the only way to find out which is to run the test rather than admire the document.

Boundaries

This chapter does not reopen chapter seventeen's diagnosis of the other two categories, and it does not claim documentation repairs credentials or relationships — those fail differently and already have a different remedy. It also does not decide whether or when to hire, which is chapter nineteen's question and carries its own statutory threshold; this chapter's case begins after hiring is already under way, and documentation is not contingent on headcount — it is a prerequisite to headcount paying off, not a consequence of having it. Managing the person who receives the documentation — trials, feedback, the difficult conversation — belongs to chapter twenty; this chapter stops at the document and does not follow you into evaluating a hire. And building margin ahead of the immediate need, the subject of chapter twenty-three, is a different question asked of a business that already has enough hands and still cannot move without its owner — documentation is what makes hands usable, not what gives you spare ones.

The evidence here is one founder's felt account, not a measurement. There is no controlled comparison of documented against undocumented handoffs, no figure for how much Galperin's own bottleneck actually eased, and none of the employees' side of the story. The growth to more than ten FTE and the documentation programme happened in the same stretch of years, by his own account, and that is all the source establishes — not that the second produced the first. Score your own attempt at the handoff test above; a founder's own report of what worked is the weakest evidence class this book uses, and it is what a business that files with nobody has to offer.

Monday

  1. Split what only you know into two columns. Procedure — same shape twice, a stranger could follow it — on one side; judgment — changes with context, needs the feel of having done every job — on the other. Ten minutes, and everything that follows this week applies only to the first column.
  2. Pick the top procedure item and write it as steps, not as an explanation. Verification order, decision points, what to do when a step fails. Store it where a new person would actually look, not in a document only you have ever opened.
  3. Hand it to a named person this week, and do not sit with them. Give it to someone on the next live instance of the task and leave the room. Wrong turns are the point — they are what tells you whether the document or your presence was doing the work.
  4. Record the gap the same day, in two numbers. Time taken against your own baseline, and whether the outcome was correct. The document is not finished until this pair of numbers exists; before that it is a draft, however complete it reads.
  5. Put a date on the next handoff. The second item on your procedure list, tested the same way, before the first one's numbers are a month old and you have quietly stopped trusting them.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

What is owed is an owner who wrote the procedure down properly, handed it to someone else, and the business was measurably worse for it — not slower, which Galperin's own case already shows and freely admits, but wrong: a documented flow followed confidently past the edge of what it covered, producing an error, a lost customer, or a decision that should have escalated and did not. That is the real risk this chapter's advice creates and does not measure. Galperin's account is not that case: the three-to-four-times slowdown is the cost of a document doing exactly what documents do, and he reports it as a limit on transfer, not as a failure of the advice. The sharper case is hard to find for the reason this whole part of the book keeps running into: an owner who documented well and then watched a competent-looking rep follow the rule off the edge of it is describing their own hiring mistake or their own gap in the document, and neither makes a flattering post. Until it is found, note the asymmetry honestly: this chapter recommends a test — a named handoff, a recorded gap — whose failure is cheap and visible, and a document sitting untested on a drive costs nothing today and everything on the one day it is actually needed.
