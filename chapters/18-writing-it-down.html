Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 18

Writing It Down

A task that took the founder fifteen minutes took the person hired to do it an hour, and still needed him at every decision. Writing it down is the obvious remedy. Whether the document transfers anything is a separate question, and only someone else using it without you in the room can answer it.

The decision Which pieces of what only you currently know are procedure — checklists, verification steps, debugging flows a competent stranger could follow — worth writing down this week, and which pieces are judgment a document will not carry, so you stop budgeting for a transfer that will not happen.

Move 1 The situation

You have people around you now — an assistant, a contractor, a first hire, however you got there — and the day still runs through you. At some point this week, more than one of them needed something out of your head: how to handle a specific kind of refund, which supplier will actually bend, why the deploy script has that one flag nobody remembers adding. You answered, they carried on, and tomorrow someone will ask a version of the same question, because the answer lived nowhere except the exchange that just happened.

The instinct is to write it down, and the instinct is not wrong. It is also, left unexamined, the single most comfortable form of avoidance available to an owner who does not actually want to hand the decision over. A document takes an afternoon, produces something to point at, and asks nothing of anyone else. Handing a task over for real means watching someone else do it worse than you would, live, in front of a customer who might notice — which costs something a checklist written alone in your own office never does.

So the question here is not should you write things down — you should, and most of what follows assumes you will. It is narrower and less comfortable: which of the things only you know is actually procedure, transferable by a document, and which is judgment a document will not carry no matter how well it is written — and how you find out which is which before you have spent a quarter documenting the half that was never going to transfer.

Move 2 What actually happened

Eran Galperin has been in this book twice: chapter four for his stopping rule, chapter five for his move to full time. This is the part of his story those chapters had no use for — what happened once Gymdesk outgrew him. Gymdesk, his gym-management software, is self-funded B2B SaaS at close to the reader's scale, and by his own account he ran it alone at the start: "for the first 5 years of the company's existence, I was doing everything. From product, to development, to customer service, to operations." The company eventually outgrew that shape entirely. In a 2023 post about what came after, he describes "the process I went through growing Gymdesk from a one-person operation to a company with over 10 FTE at that point in time."

What he expected hiring to do was straightforward: take the smaller, lower-level tasks off him so he could focus elsewhere. What happened instead: more employees produced more work reaching him, not less, because the questions that were trivial for him were not trivial for anyone he hired to take them. The gap showed up first as time, on tasks he had underestimated: "Tasks that would take 15-20 minutes of my day as a side activity, required hours for a dedicated person to complete." He gives one of them its exact shape. A support process that would "take me a couple of minutes to go through and respond" can, for a rep who is not a developer and has no view of the development queue, "take close to an hour, and require my participation at each one of those decision points." Read the second clause twice. The work moved and he did not.

The reason was not that the people he hired were incapable. It was that the knowledge required to do the job at his speed had never existed anywhere except inside him, built up over years rather than written anywhere: "What I failed to take into consideration was that I can transfer knowledge, but not the intuition and cross functional experience with all aspects of the business that came with years of performing all roles in the company." Knowledge is what a document can hold. The years of doing every job — the fast pattern-matching that lets a founder resolve an ambiguous customer complaint in two minutes because he has already seen forty variants of it — is not, and no format he tried made it so.

He built the structure anyway, for the part that was actually procedure: "I started also documenting all of the knowledge I accumulated for each function of the business," in a Google Drive with a folder per department, an internal employee handbook, and recorded weekly engineering walkthroughs of how parts of the system had evolved. The one piece described precisely enough to judge as a mechanism rather than an intention is the customer-service knowledge base: "Creating an internal customer service knowledge-base / SOPs, containing verification and debugging flows that are repeatable and can be used immediately by new reps. Reps input a new entry every time they encounter a ticket and we collaboratively document the steps to resolve it." The document is not written once by the founder and handed down. It is written by the people using it, on the case in front of them, and it grows only where it is actually needed.

The programme came after — his response to that finding, not a measurement of it. He describes what he built and how it is meant to work. He does not report a rep working from those SOPs, or what it took them when they did. On his own assessment the whole effort is "still a work in progress, and I still feel too embedded in many of the processes" at the company. That is the honest state of this case, and the chapter can only stand on it: a diagnosis with numbers attached, and a remedy whose result is not yet in.

Move 3 The reasoning

Sort before you write

The first move is separating what is actually procedure from what only looks like it because you have never had to explain it out loud.

Procedure is a sequence a competent stranger could follow to the same result, in a written order, every time. Verification steps, a debugging flow, a checklist for a task that has the same shape each time it recurs — Galperin's customer-service knowledge base is built for exactly this shape, flows "that are repeatable and can be used immediately by new reps." Judgment is what changes the sequence depending on the case — reading what a customer actually means underneath an ambiguous complaint, guessing which of six possible causes is the live one before checking any of them, knowing which rule to break. This is what Galperin names directly as the thing that did not transfer: the intuition and cross-functional experience built by years of doing every job, which a document can describe but not install.

The dividing line is not skill level and it is not importance — some judgment calls are trivial and some procedures are the most important thing the business does. The dividing line is whether the task is the same shape twice. If it is, write it down, and expect a competent stranger to be slower than you at first, by a margin that will look alarming — that gap is not a failure of the document, it is the cost of the transfer actually happening — and expect it to close with repetition rather than with more documentation. If the task changes shape with context, a document will describe your instincts without transplanting them, and the honest move is to say so rather than keep rewriting the document until it feels complete.

Documentation is the comfortable kind of avoidance

I'll delegate once it's properly documented is not a plan. It is a stopping condition with no way to fail it: a document can always be made more complete, and completeness was never the thing that mattered.

The mechanical defence: a document does not count as written down until a named other person has used it, unsupervised, on a live instance of the task, and you have recorded whether it worked and how long it took. Not reviewed it, not nodded at it in a meeting — used it, with you out of the room. Until that has happened, what you have is a comforting draft, and the only way to find out which is to run the test rather than admire the document.

Boundaries

This chapter does not reopen chapter seventeen's diagnosis of the other two categories, and it does not claim documentation repairs credentials or relationships — those fail differently and already have a different remedy. It also does not decide whether or when to hire, which is chapter nineteen's question and carries its own statutory threshold; this chapter's case begins after hiring is already under way, and documentation is not contingent on headcount — it is a prerequisite to headcount paying off, not a consequence of having it. Managing the person who receives the documentation — trials, feedback, the difficult conversation — belongs to chapter twenty; this chapter stops at the document and does not follow you into evaluating a hire. And building margin ahead of the immediate need, the subject of chapter twenty-three, is a different question asked of a business that already has enough hands and still cannot move without its owner — documentation is what makes hands usable, not what gives you spare ones.

Everything above is one founder's felt account: no comparison against undocumented handoffs, no figure for how much his own load eased, none of the employees' side. The growth to more than ten FTE and the documentation programme happened in the same stretch of years, by his own account, and that is all the source establishes — not that the second produced the first. It is what a business that files with nobody has to offer. Run the handoff test yourself and the two numbers it produces will be better evidence than anything in this chapter.

Monday

  1. Split what only you know into two columns. Procedure — same shape twice, a stranger could follow it — on one side; judgment — changes with context, needs the feel of having done every job — on the other. Ten minutes, and everything that follows this week applies only to the first column.
  2. Pick the top procedure item and write it as steps, not as an explanation. Verification order, decision points, what to do when a step fails. Store it where a new person would actually look, not in a document only you have ever opened.
  3. Hand it to a named person this week, and do not sit with them. Give it to someone on the next live instance of the task and leave the room. Wrong turns are the point — they are what tells you whether the document or your presence was doing the work.
  4. Record the gap the same day, in two numbers. Time taken against your own baseline, and whether the outcome was correct. Then have the person who used it write the corrections in — the step they got wrong, the question they had to ask you — before you touch it. The document is not finished until the numbers exist and someone other than you has edited it.
  5. Put a date on the next handoff. The second item on your procedure list, tested the same way, before the first one's numbers are a month old and you have quietly stopped trusting them.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is an owner who wrote the procedure down properly, handed it to someone else, and the business was measurably worse for it — not slower, which Galperin's own case already shows and freely admits, but wrong: a documented flow followed confidently past the edge of what it covered, producing an error, a lost customer, or a decision that should have escalated and did not. That is the real risk this chapter's advice creates and does not measure. Galperin's account is not that case: the slowdown he reports is the cost of a document doing exactly what documents do, and he reports it as a limit on transfer, not as a failure of the advice. The sharper case is hard to find for the reason this whole part of the book keeps running into: an owner who documented well and then watched a competent-looking rep follow the rule off the edge of it is describing their own hiring mistake or their own gap in the document, and neither makes a flattering post. Until it is found, note the asymmetry honestly: this chapter recommends a test — a named handoff, a recorded gap — whose failure is cheap and visible, and a document sitting untested on a drive costs nothing today and everything on the one day it is actually needed.
