Knowing When to Stop

The last decision is the one the book began with. Chapter four asked you to write a stopping rule before you had anything to lose; this chapter is about the version of that rule nobody writes — the one for a business that has run for years, employed people, taken money from a bank or a family or investors, and has quietly run out of moves. Stopping it is a decision with a date and a procedure. Almost nobody is taught either.

The decision Whether the business has exhausted its options or only your ideas for the month; when to have the conversation that turns what more can we do? into it is time; and how to stop in the order that leaves the least behind — what can be sold, what is owed, what is filed, and to whom you account — rather than drifting until the stop is made for you.

Move 1 The situation

It has not failed. That is what makes it hard. Customers still pay, the people still come in, the bank still clears the wages most months. What has happened is quieter: the last big move did not move anything, the one before it did not either, and the weekly conversation with yourself or your partner has had the same single item on it for longer than you would admit — what more can we do? Every answer costs another quarter, and every quarter spends something you will want later: the reserve from chapter twenty-three, the goodwill of the people you would have to let go, the price the assets would fetch while they are still assets.

The stopping rule chapter four gave you was for the start, when the number was small and every consequence on its menu was cheap — a salary to go back to, a domain to keep. This is the rule for the end, and it is harder for the same reason it is more important: there is more to lose by stopping, and more to lose by not. The question is not whether the business can go on. It usually can, for a while. It is whether going on is a decision or the absence of one — and if you cannot say what would have to happen, by when, for the answer to be stop, it is the absence.

Move 2 What actually happened

Before Gymdesk, Eran Galperin had another company, and this is its ending. Binpress, a marketplace for developers' code libraries, was conceived in late 2010 by Galperin and his business partner, Adam, out of a web-development shop they had run for four years; it soft-launched at the end of that year, and "Over the course of 2 months, we had exactly 10 new users." A contest with $40,000 in sponsored prizes seeded the marketplace with around two hundred products; sales then ran at "around $500 in sales" a month for "the first several months". Mobile components changed that — "Our revenue stabilized around $3k / month. Then $5k / month. Then $10k / month" — and in February 2013, after a month that had reached $20,000, an application made "on a whim" put them in an accelerator, where "we raised our seed round of almost $1M." The peak, by his account, was "getting to $30k in monthly revenue."

The money was for marketing and sales, which the two founders — "product guys first", by his account — knew they lacked. Five of the hires he lists — an enterprise marketer, a cold-calling salesperson, a developer evangelist, a former editor, an SEO and content-marketing company — "None of those panned out, for various reasons." The good hires they did make "were not at the positions we needed to really make a difference." They ran the experiments a business runs when the growth will not come: affiliates, discounts, purchase orders, custom work, retargeting, landing pages, lifecycle emails, live chat. "Most of the stuff we did had a positive effect, but none had the breakout potential we had hoped. We were stuck in a rot."

Then the position, in his words. "The timeline now is Sept. 2014, a little over a year since we raised our seed round." "We had spent a little over half the money we raised, which was a healthy rate, but only when you are growing." They chose one more big move: a full pivot, to become "the "Shopify for digital products"", built in around three months, Galperin "programming for 10-12 hours a day", and launched in January 2015 with press and a campaign. "But as time went by, nothing changed."

Then the months, in one paragraph: "As the months went by, it was becoming clear we had exhausted every option. We had no more ideas on how to grow the company. We had lost. It took us a while to come to terms with this reality. We didn't bring it up when we talked – instead, it was always ‘what more can we do?’. But eventually, we had that talk and faced the music. We had given 5 years of our lives to the company, we had some successes, but we had taken it as far as we could. It was time to move on."

And then the stop itself. "Our immediate goals were to return as much as we could to our investors. We tried shopping the company for a while, and while there was significant interest at one point, no one was able to commit." "Eventually we settled for selling the assets only (site and code-base), while dissolving the company. Definitely not the outcome we were looking for." What the account gives is the order: the decision, then the attempt to sell the whole, then the sale of what could be sold, then the formal dissolution — and, at the end, the investors thanked, several by name. Five years, ended by the people who owned it, and late by their own reckoning: the pivot, he writes afterwards, "was brought on too late to have a chance to really succeed."

Move 3 The reasoning

Exhausted options are a date, not a feeling

A business has exhausted its options when the last big move has had its time and not moved the figures — and the date on which that is judged has to be written before the move is made, because afterwards every month is "what more can we do?" Binpress had the date in the record and did not use it: September 2014, half the money gone at a rate that was healthy only for a growing company, and the pivot chosen instead of the talk. The pivot was not wrong. Choosing it without a date was — because the months after January 2015 were spent not bringing it up, and the account says so.

This is chapter twenty's forcing function pointed at the business instead of at a person, and it is not a new rule. The sentence you wrote in chapter sixteen already ends in four consequences — raise prices, cut the offer, take contract work, stop — and the first three are the big moves. What this chapter adds is that each of them gets a date of its own: if [the move] has not moved [the figure] by [the date], the next consequence on the list is the one you take — and when the list is used up, the one left is stop, and the procedure in the next section is how it is carried out. The figure is sixteen's throw-off or one of chapter twenty-two's three, chosen before the move. The date is when the move has had a fair run — a quarter, two — and not longer, because the thing being spent while you wait is the reserve, and it is what pays for stopping well.

The boundary is the one every chapter in this part has drawn: a business can stop growing and be fine. Chapter sixteen's Pinboard did not grow for years and paid its owner; a business whose sentence is still being met is not in this chapter, and a single miss is not an instruction to open it. This chapter is for the business whose figures are falling or whose owner is funding it from a reserve that is running out — the case where going on has a price and the price is being paid without a decision. Chapter twenty-six's shock that does not lift arrives here the same way, through sixteen's sentence, and only when its last consequence is the one left.

Stop in order

Binpress's order is the one this book takes as general. First the decision, made out loud, between the people who own it. Then the attempt to sell the whole — a going concern is usually worth more than its parts, and finding out whether anyone will pay for the whole cost Binpress "a while" and nothing the account counts. Then, if that fails, the parts: what can be sold is sold, before it stops being worth anything. Then the people who are owed — investors in Binpress's case; for most readers a bank, a family, a landlord, suppliers, and the staff chapter twenty's box covers — accounted to, in an order that is not yours to choose and, on Dutch ground, is in the box below. Then the formal end: dissolved, deregistered, filed, so that the business is over rather than merely stopped. The cost of stopping badly is not the loss. It is the loss plus everything that was still worth something on the day the decision was avoided, plus what an owner who skipped the formal end can be asked to answer for afterwards, which the box quotes.

On Dutch ground · winding up cleanly

If you are not in the Netherlands, skip to Monday. If you are, the formal end has two routes, and which one is yours turns on legal form — the fork chapter twelve and chapter fifteen met. Read the fork first; the steps below are meaningless attached to the wrong side of it. A partnership is on neither side — "De turboliquidatie kun je niet gebruiken voor het beëindigen van een eenmanszaak of vennootschap onder firma (vof)" — and a vof's end is the jurist's question from the start.

Trading in your own name — an eenmanszaak — the end is a deregistration from the Handelsregister, done online: "Stop je met je eenmanszaak? Dan moet je deze uitschrijven bij KVK. Dit regel je online met je DigiD. Het uitschrijven is gratis." It is final — "Een uitschrijving is definitief en heeft gevolgen" — and there is no pause: "Tijdelijk uitschrijven kan niet." You choose the date, and by it you must have stopped trading. The Chamber tells the tax authority for you — "Wij geven je uitschrijving door aan de Belastingdienst." — but the returns do not stop with the business: "Blijf je aangiftes doen zolang deze in Mijn Belastingdienst Zakelijk staan." The Belastingdienst writes to say when the last VAT return is due.

A bv or other legal person ends by dissolution, and the fast route is the turboliquidatie — available on one condition: "Er mogen geen baten (bezittingen) meer zijn op het moment van ontbinding." "Als er geen bezittingen zijn op het moment van ontbinding, dan stop je direct met je bedrijf." The condition is narrower than it sounds, and the Chamber says so: "Het begrip baten omvat meer dan je misschien zou denken." Money the business is still owed counts — "Daarnaast zijn vorderingen ook baten" — including amounts you could collect if you pursued them. Where there are assets at the moment of dissolution the fast route is closed — "Dan is een turboliquidatie niet mogelijk"; "Je moet dan de wettelijke vereffeningsprocedure volgen" — and the Chamber describes one way to reach it: sell everything and pay the creditors first, "zoveel mogelijk", and "Hierbij houd je rekening met de belangen en rangorde van schuldeisers", so that no baten remain when the resolution is passed. Where the debts are many, its guidance is to consider bankruptcy instead, with a curator: "Zijn er veel schulden? Dan kun je overwegen om geen turboliquidatie te doen, en in plaats daarvan een faillissement aan te vragen." Running contracts — a lease, an employment — are a reason not to use the route at all: "Doe je dit wel, dan loop je het risico dat je als bestuurder persoonlijk aansprakelijk wordt." And the route carries a filing duty since the temporary transparency law in force from 15 November 2023 — "Binnen veertien dagen na het ontbindingsbesluit moet het bestuur verschillende (financiële) documenten indienen bij ons." — after which the creditors are told: "Nadat alles is ingediend, stelt het bestuur schuldeisers schriftelijk op de hoogte van de turboliquidatie." Misuse is the Chamber's own warning: a creditor can ask the court to re-liquidate, a director who paid some creditors ahead of others or hid baten can be held personally liable, and a bestuursverbod is possible.

None of this is advice, and the Chamber's own instruction is the one this box repeats: "Vraag advies van een (bedrijfs)jurist en accountant voordat er een ontbindingsbesluit volgt." For a sole trader, the accountant, before the date is chosen. For a bv with anything still owed to it or by it, the accountant and a jurist before the resolution to dissolve, and a curator if the debts say so. This box quotes the procedure; it does not say what your baten are, what your creditors' order is, or what you personally answer for if the route is misused.

Checked 28 August 2026 against KVK's pages "Eenmanszaak uitschrijven" and "Een rechtspersoon beëindigen via de turboliquidatie" (the latter updated 4 August 2026), both consulted 6 August 2026. The underlying statute — Book 2 of the Civil Code on dissolution and liquidation, and the tijdelijke Wet transparantie turboliquidatie — is not quoted here; the Chamber's guidance is, and the jurist is for the rest.

Monday

  1. Take the sentence from chapter sixteen and give its next consequence a date. One line: if [the move] has not moved [sixteen's throw-off or one of chapter twenty-two's figures] to [a number] by [a date], we take the next consequence on the list that month. Date it, sign it with whoever owns the business with you, and put it where the weekly numbers are read. When the list has one item left, this chapter is the month.
  2. Read the reserve against the decision, not the month. Take chapter thirteen's cash — the bank balance less what is held for the tax authority — and divide it by the gap between a month's outgoings, counted as chapter sixteen counted them, and this month's takings. The answer is months. If the date in item 1 is later than that, the date is wrong, not the reserve: bring it forward, because stopping well costs money and it has to be there on the day.
  3. Price the parts now, while they are worth something. One page: the customer list, the equipment, the lease, the name, the code, the stock — what each would fetch this quarter, and from whom. You are not selling. You are finding out what the decision would cost to delay.
  4. List who is owed, and in what order. The bank, the family, the landlord, the suppliers, the staff, the tax authority — with the amounts and, on Dutch ground, the order the jurist gives you. This is the list Binpress's founders called their immediate goal, and it is written before the talk, not after.
  5. On the date, have the talk, and say one of two things. The rule held, and here is the next move and its date, or the rule did not hold, and here is the order: whole, then parts, then the list in item 4, then the filing. A third thing — let's give it another quarter — is only allowed with a new number and a new date written down, and once.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is the business that wrote the end-rule, hit the date, had the talk, stopped in order — and was wrong: the one that would have turned in the quarter after it closed, on the move it had already made, for a reason that was visible in hindsight and not on the day. Binpress is not that case; its founders stopped late by their own account, and nothing they report suggests another quarter would have found the growth that four years had not. The inverse is the common one and it is not a counter-case: businesses that went on past every option until the stop was made for them, by a bank or a court, and lost the parts as well as the whole. The true counter-case is nearly unwritable, because an owner who stopped on a rule and later saw what they might have had does not publish the rule. Until it appears, the asymmetry stands where the whole book has left it: a rule with a date costs one conversation and can be wrong once; the absence of one costs the reserve, the parts, and the people, and is wrong every month it is not made.

What the book asked

Five questions, in an order that could not be changed: is there anything here; will someone pay; does the money work; can it run without you; will it last. Each one was answered from businesses that published enough to be checked — three times from a filing or a statute, where no business had — and each answer came with the case that would have proved it wrong: found, or owed and said so. Most of those businesses sold software, because those are the owners who write their numbers down; the method page says what that leaves out, and it is not small. The last question has the same shape as the first. Chapter four asked you to write, before you had anything, what would make you stop. This one asks the same of a business that has become most of your life, and the answer is the same instrument: a number, a date, and a consequence you chose while you could still choose. Everything between the two was about earning the right to need it late.
