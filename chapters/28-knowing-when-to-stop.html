Knowing When to Stop

Eran Galperin spent five years on Binpress before its founders admitted it was over. Looking back, he wrote: "We didn’t bring it up when we talked – instead, it was always “what more can we do?”." The business had not failed. It paid, it had customers, and there was always one more thing to try. How do you have the conversation while you still have choices about how it ends?

The decision Whether to fund another attempt, change what you expect from the business, or begin closing it while you still have choices about how to leave.

Binpress: always one more thing to try

Binpress — Eran Galperin, 2011–2016

Before Gymdesk, Galperin co-founded Binpress, a marketplace for developers' code. It was a bigger venture than most readers of this book will run: "we raised our seed round of almost $1M", and revenue reached "$30k in monthly revenue" at its peak. It did not grow the way the founders needed. Marketing hires and sales experiments followed, and "None of those panned out, for various reasons". By September 2014 they had "spent a little over half the money we raised, which was a healthy rate, but only when you are growing."

So they tried again, with reasons. A feature letting publishers sell through their own checkout had shown some success, and the bigger publishers were enthusiastic. They spent about three months building branded stores, "the “Shopify for digital products”", and launched in January 2015. "But as time went by, nothing changed." Some users signed up and made a few sales; the scale was small next to the marketplace; more marketing did not move it.

Then the sentence in the opening: the talk they did not have. When they did, they tried to sell the company, and "no one was able to commit". "Eventually we settled for selling the assets only (site and code-base), while dissolving the company."

What the account establishes is narrow and useful: the new attempt had real reasons behind it, and still the hardest part was saying the question out loud. It does not show that an earlier date would have saved more; Galperin does not claim to know exactly why it failed. Your business is probably smaller, with no investors and no staff. The conversation is the same size.

Three choices, with your rule on the table

In chapter four you wrote a rule while nothing was sunk: a number, a date, and what failing it would mean. This chapter is that rule's last use, made when a great deal is sunk. There are three choices: another attempt, a smaller business, or an exit. How you weigh them depends on what you have built. A practice gives its owner good work and a good living for as long as they do the work; measure it against the household number and the working life you want. An asset can be run and owned by someone else; measure it against whether someone else could take it over. Both are legitimate. The mistake is not knowing which you have.

Another attempt needs what the last one lacked. Name the result you need, not the work: a finished launch is in your control, paying customers are not, and Binpress finished its product. Write the evidence for trying, a paid pilot, a customer's commitment, a sales route you have actually tested, and keep it apart from encouragement and from what you have already spent. Give the attempt a budget and a review date early enough to act on. If you extend it, say what changed; moving the old target into another quarter answers nothing.

A smaller business can be the right answer, not a consolation. If it meets the household number and its obligations, stalled growth is not a reason to close. It needs the agreement of anyone whose money or work sustains it.

An exit is worth investigating even if you do not take it, because it makes the comparison honest. What would you owe, and to whom? What have customers paid for that you have not yet delivered, and what notice does each contract require? Someone has to return customers' data, account for deposits and answer enquiries after trading ends, and that takes time and money. If the exit is a sale or a handover, a successor gets only what they can run from what is written and read from what is filed, and making that true takes months; the optional chapter Built to Leave covers it in detail. A plan that puts every remaining euro into the next attempt has left nothing for carrying out the other decision.

Lea's rule, the second time

Lea is invented, and this is where the book leaves her. Her first rule, in chapter one, was three paid customers by the end of March at about ten hours a job. She got the customers; the jobs took ten, fifteen and twenty-three hours, so the four-day week waited. Chapter sixteen gave the step its price: €900 a month from the business, two single-file jobs, against about €300 a month so far. She is not at the stopping point, and nothing on these pages has told her the four-day week will come. So she writes the rule again, the way chapter four taught, with what she now knows.

After the next three single-file jobs or on 31 August, whichever comes first, as chapter twelve set: I ask for the four-day week only if all four hold. Those jobs averaged twelve hours or fewer. I am winning two a month. The accountant's estimate says two a month leaves at least €800 a month for the household after tax. And a Monday-morning failure is covered, because my free day is Monday or someone I pay can answer it. I ask on condition that I can return to five days, which I asked my employer about before I started. If a job or a tax figure is missing on the date, the review happens anyway and the missing condition counts as not met. If the hours are within twelve but the jobs are not coming, I keep five days and test one route for finding customers until 30 November. If the hours average more than twelve, I change the offer before I sell it again. And if two reviews in a row show neither moving, I stop selling, keep the job, and finish what I owe.

What would justify the four-day week is now written down, and it is everything the book has shown her, not just the part that is going well: jobs back near ten hours, two of them a month, enough left for the household after tax, and someone for the Monday failure chapter sixteen saw coming. What prevents it is written down too. The review comes after three new jobs or on 31 August, whichever is first, and the three jobs are new ones, not the three from chapter ten. That is the whole of what this book can give her, and it is enough to decide with.

Monday · your rule, written again

Do this one thing. Take out the rule you wrote in chapter four, and write one line beside it: what actually happened by its date. Then, underneath, write the rule again, as Lea did: a number you will judge by, a date, and which of the three choices failing it will mean: another attempt on a smaller budget, a smaller business, or an exit. You now hold your next number, your next commitment and your next review date on one page.

Never wrote the rule? Skip the first line and write the new rule directly. Do not know what an exit would involve? Choose it anyway if it is the honest consequence, and add one line to the page: from your own records, what you owe, what customers have paid for and not yet received, and the notice each contract requires, to be listed by a date. Your business register publishes the formal steps, and the Dutch section below quotes them for the Netherlands.

If you have another hour:

  1. Talk it through with one person who will challenge you, or with the other owners if there are any, with your page in hand.
  2. Cost the next attempt: the result you need, the evidence for trying, the budget, and a review date early enough to act on.
  3. Write what a smaller version would have to provide, using chapter sixteen's number.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

Binpress's account is its co-founder's own postmortem, written in 2016. It is a funded company with investors, which changes the pressures and the obligations. September 2014 dates a financial position in his account, not a stopping deadline; he gives no recovery amount from the asset sale, and his explanations of the failure are his retrospective judgment. The account shows that selling the whole company took time and did not succeed; it does not say what another month's delay would have cost or recovered. Lea and her second rule are invented.

Still owed: an owner who used a review like this, closed, and had good reason afterwards to believe a little more time would have changed the outcome. A deadline can end a business just before work already done begins to pay. None has been found. That is why the review must look at new evidence as well as the target; you can make the decision explicit without making it certain. The debt stands.

The end of the book

The book asked four questions, in order. Is there anything here: a problem people pay to have solved, at a price you can deliver. Does the money work: a period you can read, cash that arrives before it is needed, work worth repeating, and enough reaching the household. Can it run without you: one task handed over, help you can afford, a decision made in time. Will it last: cover for the morning you are gone, permission for what you rely on, a reading of the shock that is not yours.

None of those is answered once. What stays with you is a habit this chapter has just used: write the number, the date and what failing it means before the decision is due, and read what happened against it when the date comes. Your number, your next commitment and your review date are on the page this chapter's Monday asked for. That page is where the book ends.

On Dutch ground · winding up cleanly

This section is for readers in the Netherlands, where the formal end has two routes, and which one is yours turns on legal form — the fork chapter twelve's owner-pay section met. Read the fork first; the steps below are meaningless attached to the wrong side of it. A partnership is on neither side — "De turboliquidatie kun je niet gebruiken voor het beëindigen van een eenmanszaak of vennootschap onder firma (vof)" — and a vof's end is the jurist's question from the start.

Trading in your own name — an eenmanszaak — the end is a deregistration from the Handelsregister, done online: "Stop je met je eenmanszaak? Dan moet je deze uitschrijven bij KVK. Dit regel je online met je DigiD. Het uitschrijven is gratis." It is final — "Een uitschrijving is definitief en heeft gevolgen" — and there is no pause: "Tijdelijk uitschrijven kan niet." You choose the date, and by it you must have stopped trading. The Chamber tells the tax authority for you — "Wij geven je uitschrijving door aan de Belastingdienst." — but the returns do not stop with the business: "Blijf je aangiftes doen zolang deze in Mijn Belastingdienst Zakelijk staan." The Belastingdienst writes to say when the last VAT return is due.

A bv or other legal person ends by dissolution, and the fast route is the turboliquidatie — available on one condition: "Er mogen geen baten (bezittingen) meer zijn op het moment van ontbinding." "Als er geen bezittingen zijn op het moment van ontbinding, dan stop je direct met je bedrijf." The condition is narrower than it sounds, and the Chamber says so: "Het begrip baten omvat meer dan je misschien zou denken." Money the business is still owed counts — "Daarnaast zijn vorderingen ook baten" — including amounts you could collect if you pursued them. Where there are assets at the moment of dissolution the fast route is closed — "Dan is een turboliquidatie niet mogelijk"; "Je moet dan de wettelijke vereffeningsprocedure volgen" — and the Chamber describes one way to reach it: sell everything and pay the creditors first, "zoveel mogelijk", and "Hierbij houd je rekening met de belangen en rangorde van schuldeisers", so that no baten remain when the resolution is passed. Where the debts are many, its guidance is to consider bankruptcy instead, with a curator: "Zijn er veel schulden? Dan kun je overwegen om geen turboliquidatie te doen, en in plaats daarvan een faillissement aan te vragen." Running contracts — a lease, an employment — are a reason not to use the route at all: "Doe je dit wel, dan loop je het risico dat je als bestuurder persoonlijk aansprakelijk wordt." And the route carries a filing duty since the temporary transparency law in force from 15 November 2023 — "Binnen veertien dagen na het ontbindingsbesluit moet het bestuur verschillende (financiële) documenten indienen bij ons." — after which the creditors are told: "Nadat alles is ingediend, stelt het bestuur schuldeisers schriftelijk op de hoogte van de turboliquidatie."

Misuse and later discoveries. KVK says that if an asset turns up later or the procedure was wrongly applied, creditors can ask the court to reopen liquidation. It describes possible personal liability for a former director who consciously acted unlawfully, for example by paying creditors contrary to their proper ranking or deliberately concealing assets. In specified cases, the Public Prosecution Service can ask the court for a bestuursverbod, a prohibition on acting as a director.

None of this is advice, and the Chamber's own instruction is the one this section repeats: "Vraag advies van een (bedrijfs)jurist en accountant voordat er een ontbindingsbesluit volgt." For a sole trader, the accountant, before the date is chosen. For a bv with anything still owed to it or by it, the accountant and a jurist before the resolution to dissolve, and a curator if the debts say so. This box quotes the procedure; it does not say what your baten are, what your creditors' order is, or what you personally answer for if the route is misused.
