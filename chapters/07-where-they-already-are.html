Where the Next Ones Come From

This chapter is optional. Read it after chapter fourteen, once you have a few customers and a record of how each arrived. Until then, chapter six's few names and one way to reach each are enough. The main path continues to chapter sixteen.

Patrick McKenzie worked out that a new trial user from advertising cost him about 24 cents, and a reminder email to someone who had already signed up cost 2. Both brought people to the same product. One had to be paid for again every month; the other came from a list he owned. Which ways of reaching buyers deserve your hours, and which will still be producing customers after you stop?

The decision Which two ways of reaching your buyer you will practise for the next six months, and, for each, whether you are building something that keeps working or paying for attention that stops when you do.

Bingo Card Creator: three channels, and the one that kept paying

Bingo Card Creator — Patrick McKenzie, 2008–2010

McKenzie sold software that printed bingo cards for primary-school teachers, at $24.95 and later $29.95. He called it "Hello World attached to a random number generator", and reckoned that "90% of the results of your business, and somewhere around 90% of the effort, are caused by non-coding activities."

The first channel started as a small bet: in 2008 he paid freelancers to write pages on topics teachers searched for, with no way to know any of it would rank. It became "the freelancer-produced content -> SEO engine that made my year in 2008 and continues to produce dividends". His summary, when asked to explain the method: "turn $3,000 of freelancer writing into $30,000 of sales last year." Search brought just under half of his visits in both years: 546,000 in 2009 and 777,000 in 2010.

The second was email to people who had signed up for a trial. "It costs me about 24 cents to get a trial download or trial signup through AdWords at the margin, but only 2 cents to remind someone that their account still exists." His holiday emails "made well over a thousand dollars in sales for me, for about $30 worth of virtual postage stamps and perhaps twenty minutes of writing."

The third was paid advertising, a fifth of his traffic in both years, and he plainly disliked it. "AdWords has made me very unhappy at several points this year", he wrote in 2009, citing "their inability to approve my new ad copy within a month of submission." By 2010: "AdWords has been on auto-pilot for virtually the entire year", and left alone "it seems to optimize for Google".

The pages written in 2008 were still selling in 2010. The advertising bought visitors that month and nothing after. Stop writing in March and you still have March's pages; stop paying in March and you have March's silence. He kept all three, but only two got his attention. The advertising ran on autopilot: a real return that did not take hours from the two he was getting better at.

Appointment Reminder: the same hands, a different market

Appointment Reminder — the playbook that did not transfer

In December 2010, eight months after going full time, he launched a second product: phone, text and email reminders for professional practices whose clients fail to turn up. The plan, stated in advance: "The plan is, unsurprisingly if you know me, heavily reliant on organic SEO."

Four months later: "AR has had about fifty people sign up for the trial", against "these days, BCC routinely gets 250 signups a day." Everything else worked. It was "cash flow positive". On the $29 plan the worst-case cost was "about $20 a month paid to Twilio" and in practice "most customers on that plan are below $3 apiece." One customer had "seen his no-show rate decline to virtually nothing". His conclusion: "if I could just scale customer acquisition, I would be in a very happy place."

He had not run the playbook either. After leaving his job, burnout and consulting meant "I accomplished almost nothing on AR between May and late October of last year"; after launch he took December off, and the first months of 2011 went on consulting, side projects and an earthquake. In his December review he had already named the failure himself: "Insufficient pre-launch marketing."

Two things survive, and both matter to you. The search asset that made Bingo Card Creator took years to build, and a new product needs customers long before a slow channel matures. And the market was different in a way effort could not fix: teachers searching for bingo cards are a long tail of people typing, while practices that have not yet decided their no-shows are a software problem are not searching for anything. His skill was never "distribution". It was one channel in one market.

Pick two, and ask what each leaves behind

Most advice says test many channels and double down on what works. That is sound when channels are cheap to test and quick to read. The ones worth practising are neither: they take months, and early results look like noise. You have attempts enough for about two. Two is a budget of attention, not a ban: anything that runs unattended and still returns something can keep running, as McKenzie's advertising did.

Ask four things of each route, in order, because failing an early one cannot be fixed by working harder at a later one:

  1. Are the right people there? Hundreds of thousands of teachers typed bingo questions into Google. A firm selling to four hundred logistics companies has no such crowd; its equivalent is a trade association, a list, the six people everyone in the sector reads. At a café it is the school gate across the road at half past eight.
  2. Can you reach them without someone's permission? His ad account was switched off and his copy went unapproved. Search is gatekept too, only more politely: he owned the pages, Google owned their position. Of his three channels only the email list, which he could take elsewhere, passed outright, and it was the smallest.
  3. Does it work again next Tuesday? A page that ranks does. A launch does not. Plausible's Product Hunt day brought "more than 1,000 visitors and 15 trial signups from Product Hunt on the launch day", and "only a few days later, we got fewer than 20 visitors from there." In their words: "It brings that famous spike of hope after which follows the flat line of nope."
  4. Does it produce buyers cheaply enough? Answer this one roughly, from chapter fourteen's allowance, and use it to rule out the obviously ruinous, not to rank the rest.

Then the question that decides where your hours go: if I stop this month, what do I still have in six months? Pages, a list, customers who send the next customer: these keep producing with neglect only slowly eroding them. Advertising, launches and cold approaches have to be re-earned every month for the same result, which spends the one thing a person working alone cannot spare. Neither kind is wrong; they buy different things. And expect a growing channel to make your ratios worse. McKenzie's trial-to-purchase rate fell from 2.33% to 1.7% as search brought in less motivated visitors, and sales still rose: "1% of a very large number is still worthwhile." Manage the number of buyers, not the ratio.

Lea's two routes

Lea is invented, continued from chapter fourteen. Her record already shows her routes: A, a former colleague, took two sales hours; B, whom A introduced, took three; C, a direct approach, took five; and six more hours went on approaches that produced nothing. Chapter fourteen gave her a limit of about five owner hours to win each €900 job at eight hours of delivery, and a first campaign cap of €100 and three hours, or two weeks.

She asks what each route leaves behind. Introductions from customers who got a result accumulate: every finished job can produce the next name, at two or three hours each. Direct approaches do not: each costs five hours again, unless something she makes for one can be reused for the next. So she picks two to practise until 30 September. First, at every handover she asks the customer for one name, by name. Second, she writes one page showing a real weekly report before and after, with the customer's permission, and sends it with every direct approach instead of writing each email from scratch.

She also names the number that would show she is getting better, before any of it pays: selling hours per won job with the failed approaches included, now about 5.3 (sixteen hours for three customers), and replies per direct approach. If by 30 September introductions have produced no new name and direct approaches still take five hours a win, she drops the weaker route and tries one other. This is a decision with a date, not a result; the book does not invent one for her.

Monday · how each customer arrived

Do this one thing. For each customer you have, write one line: how they arrived (not "word of mouth" but whose word, where) and roughly how many hours it took to win them. Mark each route keeps working or has to be redone. You now hold your channels as they actually are, and which of them leave something behind.

Cannot reconstruct a route? Write "unknown" and add one field to your customer record so the next one is captured. No customers yet? This chapter can wait; chapter six's three names are the work.

If you have another hour:

  1. Pick the two you will practise for six months. Write the date, and the one number that would show you are getting better before it pays: pages that rank, replies per message, introductions per finished job. If it has not moved by the date, the channel goes, whatever it feels like.
  2. Do the two-cent arithmetic. What does it cost to reach one stranger, and what does it cost to reach someone who has already given you an address or their name at the counter? If you do not know the second number, you are probably not doing it.
  3. Put the rest on autopilot or stop it. For each other channel: does it earn the hours it takes, not just the money?
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

McKenzie's figures come from his own year-end reviews and a six-month update, published at the time. The specific trade, commissioning cheap content on searched topics, worked in 2008 and does not work now, after years of search-engine countermeasures and machines that write such pages for nothing; nothing here recommends it. His posts disagree about 2009 sales: $31,156.18 in the 2009 review, $31,855.08 as the comparison base in 2010. The earthquake he lists among the distractions of early 2011 is not named in the post; he lived in Japan and the date fits the Tōhoku earthquake of March 2011, which is this book's inference. The Appointment Reminder record ends rather than resolves: in 2012 he declined to publish its statistics and reported profit explicitly excluding it, so what happened after the six-month window is unknown here. Plausible's Product Hunt figures are its co-founder's own account. Lea and her routes are invented.

Still owed: someone who practised two channels properly for a year or more and found the compounding never arrived, while a competitor that bought attention took the market. Appointment Reminder is the nearest record, and its owner says he did not do the work. The debt stands.

Back on the main path, chapter sixteen asks what all of this has to provide for your household, and whether you want to keep doing the work it takes.
