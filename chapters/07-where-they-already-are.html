Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 7

Where They Already Are

There are two kinds of channel and the difference is not how well they work. It is what you are left holding when you stop. One accumulates into something you own; the other stops the moment the payment stops.

The decision Which two ways of reaching your buyer you are going to get good at — and, for each, whether you are building an asset or renting traffic.

Move 1 The situation

Chapter six left you able to say who the buyer is and what you refuse. That sentence is worth nothing until the buyer encounters it, and they are not going to encounter it by accident.

The instinct divides in two, and both halves are wrong in the same way. One is to build the thing well and announce it — a launch, a post, a link somewhere busy — which treats distribution as an event. The other is to buy attention, which treats it as a line item. Both convert something you already have, a moment or a budget, into customers, without acquiring anything you did not have before.

The alternative does not feel like progress for months. Pick a small number of ways of reaching people and become genuinely good at them: what your buyers search for, which twelve people they listen to, what makes one forward something to a colleague. That is a skill, and it behaves like one. Bad at first, better with repetition, and it does not transfer as widely as you expect.

Which sets up the question this chapter is actually about. Most advice tells you to test many channels and double down on what works. That is sound if the channels are cheap to test and instant to read. Practised channels are neither: they take months to produce anything and the early signal is indistinguishable from noise. You do not have enough attempts to test five things. You have enough for two, and the choice of which two is the decision.

Two is a budget of attention, not a cap on channels. A channel that runs unattended and still returns something may keep running; what it may not do is take hours from the two you are practising. The case below ran three on precisely that arrangement.

Move 2 What actually happened

Patrick McKenzie appeared in chapter five as a man resigning on evidence. The evidence itself is the subject here. Bingo Card Creator sold software that printed bingo cards for primary-school teachers, at $24.95 rising to $29.95, and he has described it as "Hello World attached to a random number generator." His own estimate of where the work went: "90% of the results of your business, and somewhere around 90% of the effort, are caused by non-coding activities."

He published the numbers each December. For 2009: "Visits: 546,000 … Unique Visitors: 470,000 … Page Views: 1.6 million" and "Traffic sources of note: Google (48%), AdWords (20%)". For 2010: "Visits: 777,000 (up from 546k) … Unique visitors: 655,000 (up from 470k) … Page views: 2.7 million (up from 1.6 million)", with "Traffic sources of note: Google (44%), AdWords (20%), Binghoo (11%)".

Where the organic traffic came from is not mysterious, because he says. There was "the freelancer-produced content -> SEO engine that made my year in 2008 and continues to produce dividends", and on top of it "a stable of mini-sites, currently mostly centered on holiday bingo from Valentines through Halloween", which "has resulted in greater than a 10x ROI." The headline version, given when he was asked to explain the method: "turn $3,000 of freelancer writing into $30,000 of sales last year." A year later the same approach was still the plan: "Meat and potatoes SEO continues to be my bread and butter."

The second channel was email, collected from people signing up for the trial. His comparison of the two costs is the sharpest line in the whole record: "It costs me about 24 cents to get a trial download or trial signup through AdWords at the margin, but only 2 cents to remind someone that their account still exists." And the return on the reminding: "My Halloween, Thanksgiving, and Christmas mail blasts made well over a thousand dollars in sales for me, for about $30 worth of virtual postage stamps and perhaps twenty minutes of writing."

The third channel supplied a fifth of his traffic in both years and he plainly detested it. "AdWords has made me very unhappy at several points this year," he wrote in 2009, citing an account switched off and "their inability to approve my new ad copy within a month of submission." By the end of 2010: "AdWords has been on auto-pilot for virtually the entire year," and when it is on autopilot "it seems to optimize for Google" rather than for him.

Move 3 The reasoning

Four questions, in order

A channel has to clear four things, and the order matters: failing an early one cannot be fixed by working harder at a later one.

  1. Are the right people there? Hundreds of thousands of teachers were typing bingo-related things into Google. That is not a channel he built but a fact about the world he found. The block below failed this question and could not work its way out.
  2. Can you reach them without a gatekeeper's permission? His account was switched off; his ad copy went unapproved "within a month of submission"; the account was flagged for gambling content. On autopilot it "seems to optimize for Google". He was renting access from a party with other interests, who could revoke it.
  3. Can the contact be repeated, or does it happen once? A page that ranks works again next Tuesday; a launch does not, which is chapter six's Product Hunt block.
  4. Does it produce customers cheaply enough to survive? "24 cents" against "2 cents" — cost per person who might plausibly buy.

The uncomfortable part is question two applied to his good channel. Organic search is not ungatekept; it is gatekept more politely. Nobody switched him off, but Google decided what ranked, and the algorithm changes that later closed this tactic did to thousands of businesses what the suspended account did to him, without anyone being notified. Of his three channels only the email list — the one he could export and carry elsewhere — passed question two outright, and it was the smallest.

Question four is answered shallowly on purpose. Cost per credible opportunity is a number you can have in month one; what a customer is worth — how long they stay, what they buy next, what servicing them costs over that life — is chapter fourteen's subject, and a channel that looks expensive on acquisition cost can be the right one once that arithmetic exists. Do not settle question four now. Use it to disqualify the obviously ruinous.

What accumulates and what does not

He states the distinction himself, in a pitch explaining why the method was worth teaching. Content ranking in search "lets small businesses achieve top rankings for relevant search terms on Google for minimal costs", and — the important half — "lets small businesses develop an asset which continues to grow in value over time, rather than leasing traffic via e.g. AdWords ads, where you have to continue paying or the spigot turns off."

His words, not this book's, and the ownership language needs correcting. Owning an article is not owning its rank: he owned the words, Google owned the position, and that is the same intermediary the ads ran through. The distinction that holds is narrower — whether effort spent this month still produces customers in six months without further spending. Accumulating against contemporaneous. Both can be taken away; only one keeps paying while you look elsewhere.

Not that paid channels are bad — a fifth of his traffic was paid in both years and he kept paying. That the two kinds of spending buy different things. The $3,000 of writing bought pages still earning three years later, the 2008 engine still producing "dividends" in 2009; the AdWords budget bought visitors that month. Spend nothing on writing in March and you still have March's pages; spend nothing on AdWords and you have March's silence.

The consequence is about where the attention goes, not the money. What accumulates rewards compounding effort and punishes only neglect; what is contemporaneous must be re-earned every month for the same result, spending the one resource a person alone cannot spare.

Two practised, one on autopilot

He ran content and search for years, email closely enough to know the numbers to the cent, and AdWords on autopilot while it delivered a fifth of his traffic. The third was not killed but left at a level requiring no attention, producing a real if unoptimised return, while the attention went where it compounded. Better than cutting what you are not committed to: the question is not whether a channel earns its money but whether it earns the hours you must keep feeding it.

Note what he had to become good at, because it was not marketing in general. One narrow trade — commissioning cheap content on topics teachers searched for, and getting it to rank — repeated until it worked without him. His own description, offered when he started consulting: "My general field of expertise is in engineering marketing outcomes: A/B testing, SEO, and the like."

The rate that fell for the right reason

Between 2009 and 2010 his trial-to-purchase conversion rate went from "2.33%" to "1.7%". On any dashboard that is a red number and a reason to call a meeting. He explains it instead:

"Sales go up, since converting 1% of a big number is still a good thing, but it dilutes my aggregate conversion rate." The cause was the channel working: as organic search brought in more people looking for holiday bingo activities and fewer teachers with a lesson on Tuesday, the average visitor wanted it less. "1% of a very large number is still worthwhile."

The lesson generalises past analytics and is the most portable thing here. A channel that grows usually makes your ratios worse: the first people it brings are the most motivated and everyone after is less so. Manage the ratio and you throttle the channel to protect a number that pays nobody. Manage the product of ratio and volume. His online trials went from seventeen thousand to seventy-two thousand while the rate fell by a quarter, and sales rose — by his own account, "up 33%".

Boundaries

The tactic is closed; the distinction is not. Commissioning cheap freelance articles to rank for long-tail search worked in 2008 and does not work now, for reasons ranging from fifteen years of search-engine countermeasures to the arrival of machines that write that content for nothing. Nothing in this chapter recommends it. What survives is owned versus rented, which is a property of channels rather than of that particular channel.

This is a consumer long tail with enormous search volume. Thousands of teachers a day were typing things into Google. A business selling to four hundred logistics firms in the Benelux has no long tail to harvest, and the equivalent asset is something else entirely — a mailing list, a reputation in one trade association, a relationship with the six people everyone in the sector reads.

Low price, impulse purchase, no sales conversation. At $29.95 a stranger can decide alone in four minutes. The chapter's arithmetic does not survive a €12,000 sale where the channel's job is to produce a meeting rather than a payment.

Monday

  1. Write down where your last ten customers actually came from. Not the category — the specific mechanism, one line each. If you cannot reconstruct it for six of the ten, that is Monday's finding, and instrumenting it is the week's work.
  2. Sort your current channels into owned and rented. For each, answer one question: if I stop this month, what do I still have in March? Anything where the answer is "nothing" is rented, which is fine, as long as you know.
  3. Pick the two you will practise for six months. Write the date six months out. Practised channels cannot be evaluated at week three, and deciding the review date now is what stops you quitting in week three.
  4. Find the twelve places your buyer already is. The forum, the association, the newsletter, the conference, the one competitor's comparison page. Name them. Chapter six's positioning tells you what to say in them.
  5. Do the two-cent arithmetic. What does it cost you to reach one stranger, and what does it cost to reach one person who has already given you an email address? If you do not know the second number it is because you are not doing it.

Appointment Reminder — the same playbook, the same hands, a different market

In December 2010, three months after going full time, he launched a second product. "Early in December I launched my second software business, Appointment Reminder" — phone, text and email reminders for professional-services businesses whose clients fail to turn up. The distribution plan was stated in advance, in the same post that reported Bingo Card Creator's best year: "The plan is, unsurprisingly if you know me, heavily reliant on organic SEO."

Four months later he reported the result. "AR has had about fifty people sign up for the trial" — against a comparison he supplies himself: "these days, BCC routinely gets 250 signups a day." Of the fifty, "about 10 of them have already paid me money and about 10 more look likely to", and "The revenue run rate is still inconsequential (south of $500 a month)."

Everything except the distribution worked. It was "cash flow positive". The unit economics beat his forecast: on the $29 plan the worst case was "about $20 a month paid to Twilio" and in practice "most customers on that plan are below $3 apiece." Customers were emphatic — "my biggest fan has seen his no-show rate decline to virtually nothing", which he adds singlehandedly pays that man's mortgage. His own conclusion: "if I could just scale customer acquisition, I would be in a very happy place."

He did not run the playbook. "I accomplished almost nothing on AR between May and late October of last year," he writes, listing burnout, consulting work and an earthquake, and he names the failure himself as "Insufficient pre-launch marketing." A channel never built cannot be assessed for gatekeepers, repetition or cost.

What survives is smaller and still worth the space. The asset that made Bingo Card Creator was accumulated across 2008 and was still paying in 2010; there is no version of it that exists four months after launch, which means the strategy has a minimum duration, and the second product needed customers before that duration had elapsed. And the market was different in a way that matters more than effort: teachers searching for bingo cards are a long tail, and clinics that have not yet decided their no-show problem is a software problem are not searching for anything. The skill did not transfer, in the hands of the person who had it, because the skill was never "distribution" — it was one channel in one market.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be someone who practised two channels properly for a year or more and found that the compounding never arrived while a competitor that bought attention took the market. Appointment Reminder is the nearest record, but its owner says he did not do the work. No qualifying case has been found; the debt remains.
