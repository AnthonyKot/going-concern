Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 27

Built to Leave

You are not selling. You may never sell. But one day you will hand this business to someone — a buyer, a manager, your own children, or whoever is standing there when you are ill — and on that day the business will be worth exactly what a stranger can run and read. Not what you know about it. What is written down, priced, and legible without you in the room.

The decision Whether the business you are building is one a stranger could take over next month — run from what is written, read from what is filed, priced from what the numbers say — and, if it is not, whether that is a choice you are making on purpose or a discovery waiting for the day you want to leave.

Move 1 The situation

Part IV asked whether the business runs without you for a week. This chapter asks the version of that question with no return date: whether it runs without you at all, under someone who did not build it, who does not carry its history in their head, and who will read it from the outside — from the accounts, the price list, the contracts, the record. That reader may be a buyer. It is more often a manager you hire when the thing has outgrown you, or a family member, or a bank, or the person who takes the call on the morning you cannot.

This book has said since its second page that there are two legitimate things to build: a practice that gives you good work and a good living, and an asset that runs and can be owned by others. Neither is wrong. The failure is building the first while believing you are building the second, and finding out on the day you want to leave. Sellability is the name for not having to find out — the state in which what you would hand over is the same size as what you think you have — and it is worth having whether or not anyone ever buys, because every reason you might leave arrives unannounced.

Move 2 What actually happened

Dan Norris has been in this book three times as WP Curve's founder — the launch in chapter ten, the two kinds of customer in chapter fourteen, and the month in chapter twenty-three when he and his partner handed their tasks to an admin and found they did not have a lot to do. WP Curve was sold in 2016. His own site puts the sequence in its title: "WP Curve → GoDaddy, Black Hops Brewing." Black Hops is the second business, a craft brewery on Queensland's Gold Coast, founded with two friends, Michael McGovern and Eddie Oldfield, and sold under the line "Beer, made by 3 Blokes on the Gold Coast." It is the one this chapter is about, and it is a different company in every respect that matters here — different trade, different scale, different sources, reported by a named trade journalist rather than told by the founder.

In January 2022 the company "raised more than $2.2 million from 996 investors in a day at a valuation of more than $67 million", through an equity crowd-funding platform. The offer documents, as Brews News later reported them, showed "a before-tax profit of $129,000" for the previous six months and told prospective shareholders the company had "moved from losing money every year, navigated the brewery valley of death and into profitability", with an EBITDA the year before of $1.76 million. Nine hundred and ninety-six people read that and priced the business at sixty-seven million dollars in a day.

Thirteen months later, on 14 February 2023, the same outlet reported that Norris had stepped down as chief executive and director and was leaving the company, replaced by an outside manager, Nathan Hyde, previously of a dairy business and before that a general manager at a large brewer. McGovern, the founding head brewer, had already gone the previous May. Norris's account of why, given to the reporter by email, is the cleanest statement of the Part IV question this book has: "in hindsight, Black Hops had outgrown him and staying would create confusion." In his own words: "Black Hops started as a fun idea at the pub between a few friends, at no point did we think it would get to be what it is today." "I led the company because we needed someone to do it but I've never done anything like this before and to be honest often felt out of my depth."

What he did about it is the part worth copying. The original plan had been to bring in a managing director and keep a role for himself; he asked people who had done it, and "The advice was always the same – don't do it unless you are fully prepared to do it. If you want to bring in new leadership, you can't hang around in the background, it's confusing to the team and difficult for the new management." So he left: "we couldn't really find a role for me inside Black Hops that made sense." The new manager's reading of the founders is generous and exact: "it's probably just crept up, and then all of a sudden it's like a lot of founders, 'now what? I've got this quite reasonable size business, and how do we run it?'"

Seven weeks after that, on 4 April 2023, Brews News reported the company's annual report for the 2021–22 financial year, filed with the corporate regulator: it "revealed a before-tax loss of $1.8 million", a $2.6 million swing from the prior year's "profit of $867,223 for the 2021 financial year". Revenue had grown almost 19 per cent to $16 million; raw materials and consumables had grown 38 per cent, wages 44 per cent. The report blamed "a significant increase in operating costs across the board and an erosion of profit margins"; the new manager, in a letter to shareholders, added the mechanism: "Black Hops as a business did not pass on any increases to our trading partners" — "The business in six years just had one price increase." And on the books themselves, with a new head of finance and new external accountants: "I don't know if the guys had complete oversight through that [prior] period. The business had grown pretty enormously." The company, the article notes, had not filed the annual reports for the two years before the raise.

Nothing on that record says the departure caused the loss, and the chapter does not: the prior year was a pandemic year of subsidies and people drinking at home, the costs that rose rose for every brewer, and the loss was in the books before Norris left, whoever was reading them. What the record does show is the thing this chapter is about. A business was priced by 996 people at sixty-seven million dollars, handed by its founder to a manager in the way the experienced people said to do it, and the manager's first act was to find out what he had been handed: costs absorbed for years without a price change, and accounts nobody had complete oversight of. The transfer was clean. What transferred was smaller than what had been priced.

Move 3 The reasoning

A stranger has to be able to run it and read it

A business is built to leave when someone who did not build it can run it from what is written and read it from what is filed — and the second half is the one founders skip, because they have never needed to read their own business from the outside. Norris did the running half as well as it can be done: he saw that the business had outgrown him, hired the person, took the advice not to hang around, and left. The reading half was what the person he hired then had to do, and the answer was in figures nobody inside had been looking at as an outsider would — one price change in six years, margins eroding, two annual reports unfiled. Everything chapter twenty-two asked for is this half: three figures a stranger can read, defined in writing, with the definitions dated.

The valuation is the warning. Sixty-seven million dollars was a number the company proposed and a market of 996 people accepted on the strength of six months of profit and a story about three blokes — and the story was the part that could not be transferred. The new manager said at once that the tagline would change; Norris himself said "our story has been about making great beer" and that the customers "keep drinking the beer because it's great quality." Both are right, and between them they mark the line: the beer transfers, the founders' story does not, and a price built on the second is a price for something the buyer does not receive. At the reader's size the same line runs through the relationship with the customers — the part of chapter seventeen's table that was you, not the business — and a buyer, a manager or a successor gets only what was moved off it.

Two things to build, and only one mistake

None of this says you must build the asset. The practice is a real thing to own, and most of this book's readers will own one: a business that is good work and a good living for as long as its owner does the work, and that ends when they stop. The mistake is not knowing which you have. Black Hops looked, to its founders and to 996 people, like the asset, and it may yet be one; what its transfer revealed was that the asset's books had been kept as a practice's are — by people who already knew what the numbers meant, and did not need them to say it. Decide which you are building and write it down, because the decision changes what you do this year: the practice needs chapter sixteen's number and chapter twenty-eight's exit; the asset needs the stranger's read, now, while the stranger is imaginary.

The limits of the case are its size and its silence. Black Hops at sixteen million dollars of revenue, with a board and a regulator and a crowd of shareholders, is not the reader's business, and the mechanics of its raise and its reporting are Australian corporate practice this chapter does not describe. The two articles are one journalist's account of a company's filings and letters, not the filings themselves; what the founders knew and when is not on the record; and what became of the company after April 2023 is not sourced here and is not used. What transfers from the case to yours is one test, and it is the one the new manager ran in his first weeks: hand the books to someone who does not know the business, and see what they find.

Monday

  1. Write the handover as if it were next month. One page: who would run it, and what they would need on day one — the last three years of chapter twenty-two's figures, the price history, chapter twenty-four's four agreements, chapter twenty-three's reserve count, chapter eighteen's documents. Every line you cannot point to is a line the stranger will discover instead.
  2. List every price change of the last five years, with dates. If the list has one entry, or none, you have been absorbing costs the way Black Hops did, and the stranger will find it in the margin before you do. Put the next change on the calendar with a date.
  3. Run the stranger's read. Give the monthly page and the last two years' accounts to your accountant or to a businesslike friend, with one instruction: tell me what this business earns and what it is worth, without asking me anything. Write down every question they had to ask. Each one is a fact that lives only in your head.
  4. Write which one you are building. Practice or asset, one word, dated, on the first page of the handover. If it is the practice, chapter twenty-eight is the plan; if it is the asset, items 1 to 3 are the work, and they are due before anyone asks for them.
  5. If you leave, leave. When the day comes — a manager, a buyer, a successor — take Norris's advice as he took it: set the date, hand over the whole thing, and do not stay in the background. But only after items 1 to 3 exist, because a clean departure hands the stranger the business exactly as it is, and that is the day they find out what it is.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business built to leave exactly as described — figures a stranger could read, prices kept current, agreements written, the founder gone cleanly — that was handed over and failed anyway, because the value had never been in any of those things: it had been in the founder's own relationships, and no document moved it. Black Hops is not that case: what its transfer found was books that had not been read from the outside, which is the advice unfollowed, not followed. Nor is it the opposite, since nothing here shows whether the business the manager took over survived being handed to him; what came after April 2023 is not sourced and the chapter does not guess. The counter-case, if it exists, is the more uncomfortable one for this book's reader, whose business is small enough that the relationships may be all of it — and Monday's third item is where that would show up first, as the question the stranger cannot answer from the page.
