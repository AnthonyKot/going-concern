What Happens After the Sale

Paying, using, finishing and getting the result are four different populations, and most businesses can see only the first — which is why a business can be losing customers for a reason it has never once measured.

The decision What outcome you are claiming the customer will get, how you will know whether they got it, and what you will do about the ones you can no longer reach.

Move 1 The situation

Payment is an event that arrives in your bank and announces itself. The outcome is a state in someone else's life that you have to go and look for, weeks later, when you are busy with the next sale. So businesses instrument the first and infer the second, and the inference is almost always flattering — because the people it went badly for are the least likely to say so. They do not complain. They go quiet, and quiet reads like contentment in every system you are likely to have built.

What follows is one paying population followed through three successive tests. Of the people who paid: how many actually used what they bought; of those, how many finished or stayed the course; of those, how many got the result the sale promised. Each is a smaller population than the one before, and the gaps between them are where a business quietly dies while its revenue looks fine.

Renewal and referral sit inside the second test, and neither settles the third. People refer before they have received anything, renew out of inertia or the cost of switching, and stay with a supplier they like while quietly not getting what they came for. Renewal is evidence worth investigating, not a verdict.

Whether the sale can be repeated at all was the last chapter. Whether the resulting money is enough, soon enough, is the rest of this part. This chapter is only about whether the relationship persists, and why.

Move 2 What actually happened

Turing School of Software & Design is a developer training programme in Denver. It sells a seven-month course — the 2016 report gives the "Published Course Length (in days, including weekends and holidays) 210" — to people changing careers, at a price that for most of them is the largest discretionary purchase of their lives. The promised outcome is unusually explicit: a job, in the field, doing this work.

The school is ordinary. The reporting is not. Turing publishes to the standard set by the Council on Integrity in Results Reporting — a coalition of training programmes that agreed on common definitions, categories and schedules. The categories were therefore written by somebody other than the seller, which is the entire point, and they include several no seller would have volunteered.

The 2016 report starts from the population that paid: "Enrolled Students 86". Then how many finished on time — "67.95%" — and within half as long again, "80.77%". Then employment, at ninety and at a hundred and eighty days: "Employed full-time in paid, in-field positions 71.83% 83.10%". And a line the standard forces into the open — of those, "Hired by school in-field 4.23% 5.63%". The school counts the graduates it employed itself, separately, where you can see them.

Three years later the format is tighter and harder. Back End Engineering, sixty-seven graduates in the report. On time: "60.2%". Within 150% of the published length: "79.5%". At 180 days, "80.6% Employed In-Field", "9.0% Still Seeking a Job In-Field" — and "7.5% Could Not Contact". Median pay "$75,000"; the commonest title, "Software Engineer".

Then the following year, same programme, same standard.

Move 3 The reasoning

Count from the population that paid

Take the 2016 report on its own terms. Eighty-six people paid. About sixty-eight per cent finished on schedule, and about eighty-one per cent finished within half as long again — which means roughly one in five did not finish the programme they had bought inside 315 days, and around a third missed the schedule they were sold.

A business that counted only its graduates would never see those people. They paid; they are not in the numerator or the denominator of anything the seller usually publishes; and each one is someone for whom the published completion path did not work inside the period being measured. Not necessarily someone the purchase failed for — the report cannot say whether they finished later, got the job anyway, or left for reasons of their own. What it can say is that they were there, and cannot be quietly removed from the population that paid. Count from the population that paid, not the population that finished. Everything else is arithmetic performed on survivors.

The category that makes the rest of it honest

Of everything in these reports, the line worth stealing is "Could Not Contact" — 7.5% in 2019, 7.7% in 2020. It is a published count of the people who went silent.

Almost no small business has this number, and almost every small business has these people. The default is to drop them: they are not complaints, not refunds, not cancellations, so they fall out of the denominator and every ratio improves. That improvement is artificial whatever happened to them. And if people with poor outcomes are likelier to go quiet — which is plausible and which these reports cannot establish — then excluding the unreachable makes the published result look better precisely as the underlying result gets worse.

Naming them as their own line is the cheapest integrity move available to a business with twenty customers. It costs nothing but a column, and it is the only one that gets harder to fake as things get worse.

The answer depends on when you ask

The 2016 report gives employment at two dates, and the pair is more instructive than either alone: "71.83%" at ninety days, "83.10%" at a hundred and eighty. Eleven points of the final result had not arrived yet at the three-month mark.

Measure at ninety days and you miss what has not arrived. Measure at a year and you are counting what the world did as much as what you did — and catching the late failures too. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions. Which is why the instruction is to define the outcome with a date attached and then keep the date fixed. No date is intrinsically the honest one. The honesty is in choosing it before you have seen the number, and not moving it afterwards.

Who is allowed to count as a success

One line in the 2016 report exists purely to stop a particular kind of cheating: "C. Hired by school in-field 4.23% 5.63%". Some of the graduates counted as employed were employed by the school that trained them, and the standard requires that to be broken out where a reader can see it.

Left in the aggregate it would be invisible, and hiring your own graduates can mechanically improve the headline rate unless those hires are broken out. The general form of the trap is worth carrying: whenever you count a customer as a success, ask who verified it and whether they had an interest in the answer. Testimonials, case studies and "we're delighted" emails all fail that test. Someone else's payroll is substantially stronger — the verifier is paying a cost and has no reason to improve your statistics — though it is not conclusive either, since a job can be temporary, badly matched or obtained for reasons that had nothing to do with you.

Hold the delivery still and watch the outcome move

Now put 2019 and 2020 side by side, same programme, same standard, same school. Graduation on time: 60.2% then 61.4%. Within 150%: 79.5% then 87.5% — better. Median pay for those who got work: $75,000 both years, unchanged. By every measure the report has of what the school itself did, 2020 was at least as good a year as 2019.

And employment in-field fell from 80.6% to 63.1%, while still-seeking rose from 9.0% to 26.2%. More than a quarter of the people who finished were, six months later, still looking for the thing they had bought the course to get.

A business watching only those measures would have recorded a good year. But what the customer was buying was not the delivery; it was the outcome, and the outcome is produced jointly by your work, the customer, and the world around both. This is the argument for measuring the far end as well as the near one: the near end can look sound while the thing you actually sold is failing.

The report does not say what caused it. It was 2020, and the labour market is the obvious candidate — but the document offers no comparison with other schools, and one programme in one city cannot carry that claim. What the case supports is narrower and enough: delivery and outcome are separate variables. Delivery is substantially under your control; the outcome is jointly produced, and you are only one of its authors.

What this looks like with twenty customers

You cannot publish an audited outcomes report. You can do the three things the standard forces, and all three are free.

Define the outcome before the sale, in a sentence with a date and a test in it — not "improved efficiency" but the thing that will be observably true, and when. If you cannot write it, you do not know what you are selling, and neither does the buyer. Count from everyone who paid. And give the unreachable their own line, rather than letting them evaporate.

Boundaries

The three reports are not a series. The 2016 report counts enrolled students; 2019 and 2020 count graduates included in the report. Different denominators. 2019 and 2020 are Back End Engineering alone, 2016 is everything. The 2019–2020 comparison is like-for-like; the 2016 figures sit beside them, not in line with them.

"Could not contact" is not a neutral success category. Praising its existence is not the same as trusting what is inside it: those outcomes are simply unknown. What can be said without guessing is that excluding them would make the reported rate more favourable regardless of what actually happened to those people, which is reason enough to keep them visible.

A school is an unusually measurable business. The outcome is a job — dated, binary, verifiable. Most businesses sell something whose result is diffuse, delayed or shared with six other causes. The instruction to measure the outcome survives that; the ease of it does not.

One school, self-published, against a standard it opted into. Programmes that join a transparency coalition are unlikely to be the ones with the worst numbers, so nothing here describes the industry, only this school.

Monday

  1. Write the outcome sentence. "By [date], this customer will be able to [observable thing]." One line per product. "They felt it went well" is not enough; the same question asked of everyone, the same way, every time, is.
  2. Add five columns to the record from chapter ten. Did they use it; did they get the outcome; did they renew, reorder or expand; did they refer anyone; and if they left, the reason in their words.
  3. Fill the first column from the population that paid — everyone, including the ones who never started. The gap between paid and used is the number you are least likely to have and most likely to need.
  4. Open a "could not reach" line and count it honestly. Anyone who has not answered two attempts across a fortnight goes in it. Do not distribute them across the other categories.
  5. Ask the five most recent leavers one question. "What were you hoping would be different?" Past tense, no defence, no offer. Their answer, in their words, is the fifth column.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be a business that measured outcomes properly — defined them in advance, counted from everyone who paid, tracked the unreachable — and was made worse off by it: chasing a measurable outcome that turned out not to be the one customers valued, or driving away buyers who resented being followed up. The second is plausible and unpublished. The first is the more interesting failure, because a defined outcome is a target, and a target can be hit at the expense of the thing it was standing in for. No case with numbers on either side has been found; the debt stands. The risk is a limit of defining and managing by a single outcome, not a demonstrated danger of measuring at all.
