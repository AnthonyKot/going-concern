What Happens After the Sale

Lea has delivered three report automations and been paid for all three. A uses theirs every week. B is still doing the report by hand. C has not answered. Those are three different situations, and each needs a different response.

The decision What to do when a customer has paid but the intended result is missing, delayed or unknown — and what that tells you to change before the next sale.

Follow the work into the customer's week

In chapter ten, Lea compared the time and cost of three €900 jobs. She is an employed developer testing a service that automates a weekly spreadsheet report for small consultancies. The example is fictional throughout. Her records showed delivery of eight, eleven and eighteen hours, including corrections and an unpriced addition. She could explain what the work had cost. She had not yet established what it had done for the people buying it.

This chapter continues those same jobs. If you are starting here, take one completed sale and find the promise in its proposal or conversation notes. You need the customer's intended result, what you supplied and what you have heard since. You do not need to reconstruct all the earlier chapters before following up.

Recover the promise before judging the result

Lea promised an automated report, a handover and a round of corrections. The customers wanted less time spent assembling their weekly figures. Those are related, but the proposal did not establish a measured starting point or promise a particular number of minutes saved. She cannot now invent a target and mark the old jobs against it as though everyone had agreed.

She can ask what happened. For the next job she will make the intended result clearer before quoting: the person responsible should be able to produce the agreed report from the agreed input, check it and send it without Lea operating the tool. She will ask how long that takes today and what improvement would be worthwhile. A target should reflect the buyer's work and what she can reasonably supply, rather than a number chosen to make the proposal impressive.

Keep the delivery commitment beside the intended benefit. Lea can test whether her tool processes the agreed file. Whether it saves time also depends on the customer's preparation, checks and working arrangements. Discussing those dependencies helps both people recognise a problem; it does not erase Lea's responsibility for what she agreed to deliver.

Ask about one actual occasion

Lea follows up after each customer has had two weekly reporting opportunities. That interval is an assumption for this example: it gives this particular workflow a chance to be used. A repair needed today, a seasonal purchase and a long implementation need different timing.

Her message is short: “Were you able to use the new process for last week's report? What still needed doing by hand, and about how long did the whole report take, including preparation and checks?” She invites an estimate and records it as one. If the customer would prefer to show her the workflow, they can arrange that. She does not need copies of their underlying business data to record the answer.

Before reading on, choose which customer needs a delivery intervention now. Which job can reasonably be described as helping? Which answer would you be guessing? The record supports helping B with access, recording A's reported improvement and leaving C's result unknown. C's silence does not establish satisfaction or failure, even though C was the most expensive job for Lea to deliver.

Keep the people whose result you do not know

Turing School's historical outcomes reports offer a useful example of making missing information visible. Its H1 2019 Back End Engineering report, using the Council on Integrity in Results Reporting format, lists 67 graduates. At 180 days after completion it reports 80.6% employed in-field, 9.0% still seeking a job in-field and 7.5% under “Could Not Contact”. Those are not the only categories; the employment table also includes people not seeking in-field employment. The percentages should be read with the report's definitions and rounding.

The useful feature is that the unreachable graduates remain visible. A report about only the people who answered would answer a different question. It would not tell the reader what was known about the whole reported group.

The report also separates graduation from employment. Its employment figures concern the graduates included in that report, not everyone who ever enrolled or paid tuition. That boundary matters: you cannot turn a graduate employment rate into a success rate for all customers by changing the label. Nor does an employment result establish how much the school caused it. The report provides categories for observations, not a controlled comparison of causes.

For Lea, the transferable practice is modest. Keep the full list of the jobs being reviewed, state when you checked them, and distinguish what you learned from what remains unknown. The school report does not validate her follow-up schedule or demonstrate that using a spreadsheet improves a small business. Those are proposed practices to try against her own circumstances.

There is also a difference in the service. A one-off automation can succeed and end without another purchase. A subscription may continue while the customer gets little benefit. Lea should record later purchases or introductions separately from the outcome of this job. Asking for a testimonial or another order would not resolve B's access problem.

Turn the answer into the next piece of work

Describe the result at the strength of the evidence

A's estimate suggests a saving of about 60 minutes per report: 90 before, 30 afterwards. Across the two reported uses that is about two hours. It is a customer-reported comparison, based on estimates, over two occasions. Lea has not independently timed it, established whether the reports were equally difficult, or shown that the saving will continue for a year. She can record the useful result without turning it into an annual financial return.

She should also ask whether the output was usable and what still needed checking. A faster report that omits an important figure may not have achieved what the buyer wanted. If the customer values fewer mistakes or being able to hand the task to a colleague more than speed, record that too. A convenient measurement should not displace the reason for buying.

A careful note might read: “A reports using the process for two weekly reports; estimates total preparation and checking at 30 minutes each, against roughly 90 previously. No annual saving verified. Ask whether any required figures still need manual correction.” That note keeps both the benefit and the remaining question available for the next conversation.

Repair the point where the work stopped

B has answered. The outcome is not unknown: the new process has not been used because the intended operator cannot access it. Lea needs to establish whether the issue is an omitted permission, a handover to the wrong person or a requirement outside the agreed setup. Her first step is to arrange a short session with the operator and the person who can authorise access.

Suppose, in this example, Lea discovers that she handed over only to the buyer and missed an access step required by the agreed setup. She spends one hour resolving it. That hour belongs to the job. B's delivery time rises from eleven to twelve hours. Using chapter ten's €50 labour allowance, the amount left after direct costs and sales and delivery time falls from €150 to €100: €900 − €50 − ((3 + 12) × €50).

The next check comes after B has had an opportunity to run a real report. A successful screen-sharing session establishes that access works; it does not yet establish that the weekly task is easier. If B still cannot use it, Lea records the new obstacle and decides what she owes to correct, what needs the customer's action and what would require a new agreement.

This is why outcome follow-up belongs beside the delivery record. It can reveal work that the completion tick missed. At the same time, it should not quietly create unlimited free support: explain the next action and its scope before taking on a new requirement. Lea's future handover will include the intended operator and an access check because this job exposed a specific omission.

Give an unanswered message an honest status

C might be busy, absent, satisfied or disappointed. Lea does not know. She can send one further message offering a simple reply or a short call, provided that fits their agreed contact arrangements. For this exercise she then stops chasing, records the attempts and leaves the outcome unknown. That is a practical limit on her effort, not a rule establishing when every customer becomes unreachable.

Distinguish this from “not yet due”. A customer whose next reporting cycle has not happened has not failed to answer an outcome question that can reasonably be answered. Set the follow-up date for when the benefit could be observed. If circumstances change, record the original date and the reason for a later check. Do not rewrite the old result to make the timing look better.

If someone declines follow-up, record that and respect it. You can keep an outcome unknown without treating the person as a problem to solve. For anonymous counter sales, you may only have voluntary feedback or a sample of identifiable customers. Describe that coverage honestly; do not pretend a list of respondents represents every buyer.

Read the whole group before making a claim

At the first review Lea has three paid, delivered jobs. One customer reports a useful result, one reports a barrier to use, and one has an unknown outcome. Among the two respondents, one reports the intended benefit. Across all three customers, she has that evidence for one. These statements use different denominators; neither justifies predicting a future success rate from three jobs.

She can act without a percentage. Help B use the service already purchased. Keep C's unknown result on the list. Check the operator and access arrangements on the next job. Continue asking A about the usefulness of the output. Each action has an observation behind it, while the larger question of whether this service reliably helps buyers remains open.

Keep these notes in the existing customer record. Add a baseline where available, actual use, outcome status, evidence and date, next action and responsible person. Preserve later entries as dated updates rather than erasing the first check. A separate dated notes sheet is enough if one cell becomes unwieldy. Record subsequent purchases, referrals and reasons for leaving separately when they occur, without making them compulsory signs of success.

Monday

Review a few completed sales you can follow up this week. Keep the full list visible, including people who do not reply. Use your existing record or the shared customer-record CSV; its headings now support both the delivery review and outcome follow-up.

  1. Recover the promised result. Write what the customer wanted, what you agreed to supply and what could reasonably be observed by now. Label any starting estimate reconstructed afterwards. For the next sale, agree the intended result and a suitable check before delivering.
  2. Ask about actual use. Ask what happened on a recent occasion and what remains difficult. Record the answer, its date and whether it is reported or directly observed. Keep “not yet due”, “unknown”, “blocked” and “benefit reported” distinct. Keep the customer's own description beside the status.
  3. Choose a response and carry its cost back. Name the next action, who will take it and when to check again. Add follow-up and correction time to the job record. Then write one change to the next offer or handover, or explain what you still need to learn. An unanswered message is not a reason to invent a diagnosis.

You have completed this review when you can account for each selected customer's status and explain the next action. More replies would improve the evidence, but silence need not prevent you from helping the customer whose obstacle is already clear.

What this review cannot settle

Customers may value something your question misses. Follow-up can take more time than its benefit justifies, and a reported improvement may have other causes. This chapter has no documented counter-case testing this exact review procedure. Use the answers to investigate and improve the work; they do not prove that your service caused every result or that satisfied customers will buy again.

Lea now has a clearer account of both the work and its usefulness, including an extra hour she had not budgeted. She still needs to see what all the jobs, costs and payment timings mean for the business together. Chapter twelve turns from individual customer records to reading the business's finances.
