Part III · Does the money work? · Chapter 11

What Happens After the Sale

Paying, finishing and getting the result are three different populations, and most businesses can only see the first. Renewal and referral are downstream of the third — which is why a business can be losing customers for a reason it has never once measured.

The decision What outcome you are claiming the customer will get, how you will know whether they got it, and what you will do about the ones you can no longer reach.

Move 1 The situation

Part II ended with a sale you could make again. Part III asks whether the money works, and it begins here rather than with the accounts, because every number later in this part is built on customers who stay, come back or send someone. All three are consequences of something earlier and less examined: whether the last customer actually got what they came for.

The asymmetry is structural. Payment is an event that arrives in your bank and announces itself. The outcome is a state in someone else's life that you have to go and look for, weeks later, when you are busy with the next sale. So businesses instrument the first and infer the second, and the inference is almost always flattering — because the people it went badly for are the least likely to say so. They do not complain. They go quiet, and quiet reads like contentment in every system you are likely to have built.

What follows is one number split into three. Of the people who paid: how many actually used what they bought; of those, how many finished or stayed the course; of those, how many got the result the sale promised. Each of those is a smaller population than the one before it, and the gaps between them are where a business quietly dies while its revenue looks fine.

Note the boundary at both ends. Whether the sale can be repeated at all was the last chapter. Whether the resulting money is enough, soon enough, is the rest of this part. This chapter is only about whether the relationship persists, and why.

Move 2 What actually happened

Turing School of Software & Design is a developer training programme in Denver. It sells a seven-month course — the 2016 report gives the "Published Course Length (in days, including weekends and holidays) 210" — to people changing careers, at a price that for most of them is the largest discretionary purchase of their lives. The promised outcome is unusually explicit: a job, in the field, doing this work.

What makes it usable here is not the school but the reporting. Turing publishes to the standard set by the Council on Integrity in Results Reporting — a coalition of training programmes that agreed on common definitions, categories and schedules. The categories were therefore written by somebody other than the seller, which is the entire point, and they include several no seller would have volunteered.

The 2016 report starts from the population that paid: "Enrolled Students 86". Then: "How many students graduate within 100% of published program length (on-time)? 67.95%", and "How many students graduate within 150% of published program length 80.77%". Then employment, at ninety and at a hundred and eighty days: "Employed full-time in paid, in-field positions 71.83% 83.10%". And a line the standard forces into the open — of those, "C. Hired by school in-field 4.23% 5.63%". The school counts the graduates it employed itself, separately, where you can see them.

Three years later the format is tighter and harder. For Back End Engineering, "Graduates Included in Report: 67": "How many students graduated on-time? 60.2%", "How many students graduated within 150% of program length? 79.5%", and at 180 days "80.6% Employed In-Field" against "9.0% Still Seeking a Job In-Field" and "7.5% Could Not Contact". The median was "What median pay rate do graduates earn? $75,000", and the commonest title "73.1% Software Engineer".

Move 3 The reasoning

Three populations, not one

Take the 2016 report on its own terms. Eighty-six people paid. About sixty-eight per cent finished on schedule, and about eighty-one per cent finished within half as long again — which means roughly one in five did not finish the programme they had bought inside 315 days, and around a third missed the schedule they were sold.

A business that counted only its graduates would never see those people. They paid; they are not in the numerator or the denominator of anything the seller usually publishes; and each one is someone for whom the purchase did not work. The whole discipline of this chapter is in that gap. Count from the population that paid, not the population that finished. Everything else is arithmetic performed on survivors.

The category that makes the rest of it honest

Of everything in these reports, the line worth stealing is "Could Not Contact" — 7.5% in 2019, 7.7% in 2020. It is a published count of the people who went silent.

Almost no small business has this number, and almost every small business has these people. The default is to drop them: they are not complaints, not refunds, not cancellations, so they fall out of the denominator and every ratio improves. That improvement is entirely artificial, and it moves in exactly the wrong direction — the worse your outcomes, the more people go quiet, and the better your published numbers get.

Naming them as their own line is the cheapest integrity move available to a business with twenty customers. It costs nothing but a column, and it is the only one that gets harder to fake as things get worse.

The answer depends on when you ask

The 2016 report gives employment at two dates, and the pair is more instructive than either alone: "71.83%" at ninety days, "83.10%" at a hundred and eighty. Eleven points of the final result had not arrived yet at the three-month mark.

So a school that measured at ninety days would have concluded it was materially worse than it was, and one that measured at a year would flatter itself by counting outcomes it had little to do with. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions. This is why the instruction is to define the outcome with a date attached and then keep the date fixed. A moving measurement point is not a measurement, it is a negotiation — and the direction it drifts is always the same, because the honest early number is always the disappointing one.

Who is allowed to count as a success

One line in the 2016 report exists purely to stop a particular kind of cheating: "C. Hired by school in-field 4.23% 5.63%". Some of the graduates counted as employed were employed by the school that trained them, and the standard requires that to be broken out where a reader can see it.

Left in the aggregate it would be invisible, and it is the easiest number in the world to manufacture — hire six graduates and your placement rate improves. The general form of the trap is worth carrying: whenever you count a customer as a success, ask who verified it and whether they had an interest in the answer. Testimonials, case studies and "we're delighted" emails all fail that test. Someone else's payroll passes it.

Hold the delivery still and watch the outcome move

Now put 2019 and 2020 side by side, same programme, same standard, same school. Graduation on time: 60.2% then 61.4%. Within 150%: 79.5% then 87.5% — better. Median pay for those who got work: $75,000 both years, unchanged. By every measure of what the school itself did, 2020 was at least as good a year as 2019.

And employment in-field fell from 80.6% to 63.1%, while still-seeking rose from 9.0% to 26.2%. More than a quarter of the people who finished were, six months later, still looking for the thing they had bought the course to get.

A business measuring only its own delivery would have recorded an excellent year. The delivery was excellent. What the customer was buying was not the delivery — it was the outcome, and the outcome is produced jointly by you and a world you do not control. This is the argument for measuring the far end rather than the near one: the near end can be perfect while the thing you actually sold is failing.

What caused it is not in the report and will not be asserted here. The period is what it is, and a labour market is an obvious candidate, but the document does not say so, no comparison with other schools is offered, and one programme in one city cannot carry that claim. What the case supports is narrower and enough: delivery quality and customer outcome are separate variables, and only one of them is yours.

What this looks like with twenty customers

You cannot publish an audited outcomes report. You can do the three things the standard forces, and all three are free.

Define the outcome before the sale, in a sentence with a date and a test in it — not "improved efficiency" but the thing that will be observably true, and when. If you cannot write it, you do not know what you are selling, and neither does the buyer. Count from everyone who paid. And give the unreachable their own line, rather than letting them evaporate.

Boundaries

The three reports are not a series. The 2016 report counts enrolled students; 2019 and 2020 count graduates included in the report. Different denominators. 2019 and 2020 are Back End Engineering alone, 2016 is everything. The 2019–2020 comparison is like-for-like; the 2016 figures sit beside them, not in line with them.

"Could not contact" is not neutral. Praising the category should not be confused with trusting what is inside it. People who are unreachable are, on average, likely to be doing worse than people who answer, so the published employment rate is probably generous even here.

A school is an unusually measurable business. The outcome is a job — dated, binary, verifiable. Most businesses sell something whose result is diffuse, delayed or shared with six other causes. The instruction to measure the outcome survives that; the ease of it does not.

One school, self-published, against a standard it opted into. Programmes that join a transparency coalition are unlikely to be the ones with the worst numbers, so nothing here describes the industry, only this school.

Monday

  1. Write the outcome sentence. "By [date], this customer will be able to [observable thing]." One line per product. If it does not contain something you could check without asking them how they feel, rewrite it.
  2. Add five columns to the record from chapter ten. Did they use it; did they get the outcome; did they renew, reorder or expand; did they refer anyone; and if they left, the reason in their words.
  3. Fill the last column from the population that paid. Include everyone, including the ones who never started. The gap between paid and used is the number you are least likely to have and most likely to need.
  4. Open a "could not reach" line and count it honestly. Anyone who has not answered two attempts across a fortnight goes in it. Do not distribute them across the other categories.
  5. Ask the five most recent leavers one question. "What were you hoping would be different?" Past tense, no defence, no offer. Their answers belong in chapter eight's reasons column, which is now the same document.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

It would be a business that measured outcomes properly — defined them in advance, counted from everyone who paid, tracked the unreachable — and was made worse off by it: chasing a measurable outcome that turned out not to be the one customers valued, or driving away buyers who resented being followed up. The second is plausible and unpublished. The first is the more interesting failure, because a defined outcome is a target, and a target can be hit at the expense of the thing it was standing in for. No case with numbers on either side has been found; the debt stands.
