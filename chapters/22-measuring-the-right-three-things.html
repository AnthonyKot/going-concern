Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 22

Measuring the Right Three Things

Thirty seconds of numbers, every morning, read aloud by someone who is not you — that was chapter twenty-one's instruction, and it leaves open which numbers. Not the total. The total is the one figure that tells a person who is not you nothing they can act on. What follows is three numbers underneath it, and the reason each one stops telling the truth the day you start managing to it.

The decision Which three figures the business reads every week without you in the room — chosen so that a capable person who did not build the business can tell from them whether it is moving, what it is losing, and how much of it rests on how few customers — and which figures you deliberately leave for a monthly look, so that the weekly three stay readable.

Move 1 The situation

You know the number. Last month's takings, this month's so far, the week's cash figure from chapter twelve — you can say them without looking, and when someone asks how the business is doing, one of them is the answer. The trouble is that they are answers only to you. You hear a month's total and know, from everything else in your head, that it is fine or that it is not; the person you hired hears the same figure and knows only that it is a number.

Now that other people are in the business, the figures have to work without the head that used to interpret them. That is a different requirement from knowing your own numbers. A figure that needs you to explain it is one more thing that only you can do, and chapter seventeen already ranked those. So the question is not which numbers matter — dozens do — but which few can be read, weekly, by someone who is not you, and read correctly. And behind that, quieter: what happens to a number once the people reading it are also the people being judged by it.

Move 2 What actually happened

ConvertKit was in this book at chapter eight, at $1,207 a month and nearly dead. In January 2019, past a million a month in recurring revenue and with its dashboard open to the public, Nathan Barry wrote a walkthrough of what he actually looks at, because the attention that came with the milestone brought "people reading the metrics only on the surface level." The post is a founder's own reading of his own numbers, and this chapter reads it as that. It is also, unusually, a list of the questions a mature owner asks of the figures, in order, with the figures attached.

He starts where everyone does, and immediately moves off it. Monthly recurring revenue is "the most important metric in the business", but "MRR growth is actually the summary of five metrics: new, expansion, reactivation, contraction, and churn" — so the graph he reads is not the total but the breakout, where the monthly change is "all over the map. From as high as $73,000 net new in March to a couple negative months in the summer." A total rising smoothly hid a year that was anything but. On how often to look: "Monthly is as granular as you need to go."

Then loss, which is not one number. "Revenue churn is the percentage of your revenue base you lost in a month, whereas user churn is the percentage of customers you lost in a month." They diverge for a reason he can name: "Because our beginner customers churn more often than our larger customers, user churn at ConvertKit is always higher than revenue churn." Then a third version, net revenue churn, which nets upgrades against the losses, and the figure: "At ConvertKit we are at 2.2% net revenue churn." Then the customer count, read the same way as revenue — not the total but the net new each month, and the admission that goes with it: "It's both interesting and depressing to see so many new accounts come in each month and be canceled out by so many customers leaving."

Then three more. A quick ratio — "Quick Ratio = (New MRR + Expansion MRR) / (Churned MRR + Contraction MRR)" — at "1.47, which is fairly low", though the highest of the three open companies he compares it with, "Buffer at 1.08 and Hubstaff at 1.38". Average revenue per customer: "Buffer is at $21 per customer and ConvertKit is at $57 per customer." And last, concentration: "we have 20,812 generating $1,188,580 in MRR", of whom everyone paying more than the base plan is "6,994 customers (33% of the total) generating $811,117 in MRR (68% of the total). Or put another way, 67% of our customers are generating only 32% of our revenue."

Seven things, then, not three, and Barry nowhere says fewer would do. The compression that follows is this book's, made on the reader's behalf and for a reason the post supplies: a founder auditing his own dashboard can hold seven; a business that has to run without him cannot. What the post does show is an order. Movement first, loss second, and the rest introduced as refinements of those two — which is the order the next section keeps.

And the post shows something else, in passing, that the chapter's title is really about. Twice in it a number moves for reasons that have nothing to do with the business getting better or worse. Each November ConvertKit pushes annual plans, sold at a discount: net revenue that month climbs "as high as $3 million", while MRR drops, because the discount is a contraction — and revenue churn spikes with it, then falls again when "the contraction from annual plans is now outside the rolling 30 day window." Same business, same customers, three figures swinging on a sales calendar. And on comparing churn between companies: "I remember reading an S1 from a company with net negative churn and realizing that they didn't consider any account canceling in the first 90 days as churn. That's convenient, no wonder they had negative net churn!" A number nobody had to falsify. They only had to define it.

Move 3 The reasoning

Movement, loss, concentration

Three figures, read weekly by someone other than you: what came in and what went out underneath the total; what you lost, counted as customers and as money separately; and how much of the money rests on how few of the customers. This is the book's selection, not Barry's, and the ground for it is what each figure lets a person who is not you see.

Movement is the total taken apart. The total says the month was up; the breakout says whether it was up because new customers came or because old ones spent more, and whether the quiet loss underneath was small or merely covered. Barry's summer had negative months inside a year that rose. For a business that does not sell subscriptions the breakout is coarser and still exists: new customers this week against returning ones, from the record chapter eleven asked you to keep. Loss is counted twice because the two counts disagree in an instructive way. Losing five customers is one thing if they were the five smallest and another if one of them was the account that paid for the shop's rent; user churn and revenue churn are the two answers, and which is higher tells you who is leaving. Concentration is the figure most owners can state and least like to: the share of the money that comes from the top handful. Barry's is a third of the customers for two thirds of the revenue, and he reads it as a health check in both directions — he wants the average to rise, but not so far "that we are beholden to a few customers." Chapter fourteen's segments are the same figure from the other side.

What is left out, and why. The quick ratio and average revenue per customer are arithmetic on the first two figures; the cash figure is not left out at all, it is the one you already read weekly from chapter thirteen and it measures survival, not the business. Net revenue against recurring is a distinction a subscription business needs and most of this book's readers do not. Those go in the monthly look — Barry's own "as granular as you need to go" — and they stay out of the thirty seconds because a person reading seven numbers aloud is reading none of them.

The moment a measure becomes a target

The reason to keep the three separate, and to have someone other than you read them, is the quiet thing in the post. Every figure on that dashboard is honest, and two of them moved without the business changing: an annual-plan push made net revenue soar and MRR fall in the same month, and a thirty-day window made churn spike and then recover on a calendar. Barry knows this, which is why he can read his own numbers. A person who does not know it will manage to the figure, and a figure that is managed to stops measuring what it was chosen to measure. The S-1 company did not lie; it drew the definition of churn where it wanted the number to come out, and Barry's own stated goal for the year — push net revenue churn "down to 0%" — is exactly the kind of target that a definition, a discount or a window can hit for you while the customers go on leaving.

The defence is not to stop measuring. It is to fix the definition before anyone is judged by the figure, in writing, and to change it only in the open. A lost customer is lost on the day they do not return, not ninety days later; a discount that lowers the monthly figure is written down as a discount; the concentration figure is read as customers named, not as a percentage. And the person reading the three aloud should be someone whose week does not improve when the number does. That is the limit of the advice: it keeps a figure honest against ordinary pressure, not against a business that wants to deceive itself, and it is worth nothing for the fourth thing you did not measure, which is where the surprise will come from.

Three is also a claim about the reader's size, and it should be held that way. A business of ConvertKit's shape has the staff to read seven and the churn to need them. At two or five people the three are what fits in a morning, and the test of whether they are the right three is not this chapter's argument but Monday's fifth item: whether, after a quarter of reading them, anything happened to the business that they did not show first.

Monday

  1. Write the three definitions on one page before you write any numbers. What counts as a new customer, what counts as a lost one and on which day, and which customers make up "the top". Date the page. The definitions do not change without a note on the page saying when and why.
  2. Fill in last week's three from the records you already keep. New against returning; customers lost and money lost, as two numbers; the share of the week's money from the top ten customers. If any of the three cannot be filled from what you record now, the gap is item one for next week, not a reason to skip it.
  3. Hand the reading to someone else. The thirty seconds in chapter twenty-one's meeting are theirs, not yours, and the person should be someone the numbers do not pay. You listen.
  4. Put the rest on a monthly page. Averages, ratios, the cash figure's trend — reviewed once a month, by you, and not read aloud weekly. If you find yourself adding a fourth number to the morning three, take one away.
  5. After a quarter, write down what surprised you. Every event that mattered — the lost account, the slow month, the supplier — and whether one of the three moved before it happened. If none did, the three are wrong for your business, and this list starts again with different ones.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business that read three well-chosen figures, honestly defined, weekly, by someone who was not the owner — and was blindsided anyway by something the three could not have shown, or, worse, was steered wrong by them: a concentration figure that read as healthy while the top customers were all the same fragile kind, a loss figure that stayed flat while the quality of what came in fell. ConvertKit's post cannot supply that; it is one founder reading seven numbers he already understands, and it reports no decision made on them and no surprise they missed. The chapter's own compression to three is an argument, and the honest statement of its risk is that it is made for a reader the post was not written for, which is why the last Monday item exists: the quarter's surprises are the counter-case, kept by you.
