Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 17

You Are the Bottleneck

Every measurement in Part III was taken with you inside the business, holding the gaps together for free. This part asks what happens when you are not there.

The decision Which recurring work, exceptional decisions, operational knowledge, access and relationships stop moving when you are unavailable — ranked by how long each can safely wait, and which one you take off yourself first.

Move 1 The situation

The usual version of this question is how many hours does the business need from me? It is the wrong question, and answering it well can leave you exactly where you started.

A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you. They are different quantities, and either can be large while the other is small — so hours alone cannot diagnose dependence, and the whole of this part depends on not confusing them.

An owner working five hours a week can be a total bottleneck: if every unusual decision, every refund over a certain size, every password and every awkward customer waits for Sunday evening, then the business does not run on five hours — it runs on your five hours, and it stops for a fortnight when you are in hospital. An owner working sixty hours can fail to be a bottleneck at all, if what fills those hours is ordinary capacity that a trained person could take on Monday. The first business looks efficient and is fragile. The second looks exhausting and is robust. Nothing on a timesheet distinguishes them.

So the diagnostic is not how long does this take? but what waits? Four things can wait for you, and they fail differently:

  • Recurring work — support, invoicing, the weekly deploy. Predictable, and the easiest to hand over, which is why owners start here and often stop here.
  • Exceptional decisions — the refund nobody has a rule for, the customer who wants a change to the contract, the quote outside the usual range. These are rare, which is why they never look urgent enough to delegate, and they queue behind you.
  • Operational knowledge — how the deployment actually works, which supplier will bend, what broke in 2021 and why the workaround is there. Undocumented and usually unnoticed until it is needed under pressure.
  • Access and relationships — the accounts in your name, the registrar login, the customer who buys because it is you. Access fails mechanically: nobody can get into the bank, the registrar, the payment account. Relationships fail socially: the customer, supplier or adviser will deal only with you. Access needs authorised redundancy and a recovery procedure somebody has tested. A relationship needs another person in the room repeatedly, months before you are missing, and it may never become fully transferable.

You can be excellent at the first and hollow in the other three, and from the inside that reads as success. It is the most common shape there is, and it is the one this chapter is about.

Move 2 What actually happened

In March 2010, Patrick McKenzie published an account of running Bingo Card Creator — software that makes bingo cards for teachers — alongside a full-time job in Japan. The constraint was real and he documents it: "I leave the office at 7:30 PM on a very good day, and have an hour and a half of commute both ways". Against that, the business's time cost was "the peak was the week I launched (50 hours in 8 days)", a busy week that "spiked up to as many as 20 hours, and the average over the period is (to my best estimate) about 5 hours". By the time he wrote it, the business had "made it possible to quit my day job at the end of this month".

The method is the interesting part, and it is a method for workload. He divides everything into two ledgers: "build time assets" — things that save time later, whether code, documentation or marketing that keeps working — against time debt, where "time debt is anything that you do which will commit you to doing unavoidable work in the future". Support email is converted from events into processes: a dozen messages are really "six of Email A, 3 of Email B" and three genuinely unusual ones, so A and B stop being email and become a FAQ entry, better copy, or a fix. "Recurring tasks are a great thing to systemize and outsource." And the scope of the whole thing is chosen to fit the constraint, because "90% of the results of your business, and somewhere around 90% of the effort, are caused by non-coding activities" — so the work that gets cut is chosen against the calendar rather than against interest.

It is a good answer to the wrong question. A business was built whose recurring work fits inside five hours a week — the first of the four categories, handled superbly. Whether the other three were handled, the post does not say, and does not claim to. Even his own example leaves three fairly unique issues that no process absorbs, and the post does not show that decisions about price, product and market could have proceeded without him. That is missing evidence rather than demonstrated dependence, and the difference matters: the record here measures volume of work, and volume of work is not dependence.

Chapter sixteen counted Pinboard's revenue for eleven years, and a revenue line says nothing about hours; this chapter needs a different row of the same record. By 2020 "much of the core code on the site dated back to 2009-2010", and things had reached the point where its owner could not "run a bug-for-bug identical copy of the site on a modern laptop". It had never been this week's problem. He spent the pandemic "moving Pinboard onto modern versions of PHP and MySQL" — alone, on a system serving customers throughout. "Doing this on a live system is like performing kidney transplants on a playing mariachi band."

Set the two together. McKenzie's method is to build time assets — code that keeps working after the hours are spent — and his own definition of time debt is anything that commits you to unavoidable work later. Pinboard's core code was written in 2009 and 2010, the years that post was describing. By 2020 the same object was both: an asset that had run a business for a decade, and a piece of unavoidable work that could only be done by someone who knew it, done alone on a system that was serving customers throughout. Nothing in the intervening years' hours would have shown it, because it was never this week's problem. The record names no second operator and does not say whether another engineer could have done it with documentation and time — concentration that was never measured, not irreplaceability.

What neither case establishes. Neither owner ran the experiment this chapter is about: being unavailable. No figure in either record measures what six weeks of illness would have done; both are inferred from the owner's own description of the work. Nor does the comparison show McKenzie's business was more fragile than it looked — his evidence answers a question about hours, and the question about dependence was never asked of it.

Move 3 The reasoning

Ask what waits, and for how long

The test is an absence, and you can run it on paper before running it for real. Take a normal week and ask what would happen if you were unreachable — not on holiday with a laptop, but genuinely unreachable — for one day, for one week, and for one month. Write down what stops in each case and what merely queues, because the difference is the whole diagnosis. Work that queues and then catches up is a workload problem. Work that stops, or that decays while it waits — a customer who goes elsewhere, a certificate that expires, an opportunity with a date on it — is a bottleneck.

Rank what you find by tolerable delay rather than by how much time it takes. A four-hour task that cannot wait a day outranks a two-day task that can safely wait a month. This ordering is unintuitive and it is the point of the exercise: the things that consume the most time are usually the things you have already made routine, and the things that cannot wait are usually rare enough to feel harmless.

The absence table sets the order, not the categories

The ranking rule governs: shortest tolerable delay first, from your own table. The four categories are for making sure you looked everywhere, and they will disagree with it — urgent support is recurring work with a one-hour tolerance, a deployment procedure is operational knowledge that stops the business at once, a safety or regulatory call outranks any password, and some access is recoverable in a week. Where they disagree, the table is right. With a blank table, look in this order:

  • Access first, because losing it usually has the shortest fuse of anything on the list and some of it is fixable the same afternoon: accounts in the business's name, a second authorised person, a recovery procedure written down and tested rather than assumed. Little skill transfer is required, which is what makes it cheap. Relationships share the fuse and not the timescale — introducing a second person to your largest customer is months of ordinary contact, so it starts early and finishes late.
  • Exceptional decisions second, by writing the rule instead of making the call. A refund limit, a discount ceiling, a set of conditions under which the answer is yes without asking. Every decision you convert into a rule is one that stops waiting for you. A rule written calm is more consistent and easier to check than an improvised call under pressure; it is also blunter, which is why it needs its conditions stated and an escalation point for the case it was not written for.
  • Operational knowledge third, and it is the slowest. It is the next chapter's subject, because knowledge that lives only in your head cannot be delegated by deciding to delegate it.
  • Recurring work last — where its tolerable delay allows, which for support often it does not. It is the most visible and the most annoying, and it is where owners start for exactly those reasons; but taking it off yourself while the other three are untouched buys you time in a business that still stops when you do.

Automation can exchange visible labour for concealed dependence

The developer's instinct is to solve all of this with code, and code is a real time asset — the case above is built on that insight. Automation done well reduces both quantities at once: documented, observable, understood by more than one person, it takes the work off you without putting anything in its place. The failure is the undocumented, owner-only kind. There the recurring workload disappears from view while the exceptional diagnostic work becomes more concentrated than it was — the system now runs itself until the day it does not, and the only person who can read it is you. That is the third category arriving by a route that felt like progress. If you automate, the automation is the new thing that needs documenting, and it needs a second reader before it needs a new feature.

Boundaries

Being the bottleneck is a legitimate choice, and this chapter does not overturn it. A practice built to give one skilled person good work and a good income is a real thing to own, and in that shape the owner is the product; removing them removes the business. What is not legitimate is not knowing. Diagnose first, then choose — and if you choose to stay the bottleneck, the credentials and the rules still need doing, because illness does not care which model you picked.

Removing yourself has a cost, and it is not only money. Rules are blunter than judgement, documentation goes stale, a second person makes decisions you would not have made. Systematise the thing customers were paying for and you have removed the product, not the bottleneck. The correct comparison is not against a perfect version of you; it is against the version of the business that exists while you are unavailable.

The evidence here is thinner than the argument. Both cases are one-person software businesses, both are documented by their owners, and neither reports an involuntary absence. The four categories are a framework built from two records and from what they leave out — they are not a finding. Score your own business against them and treat disagreement as information about the framework.

Monday

  1. Write the absence list, then rank it. Three columns — one day, one week, one month unreachable — and for each, what stops rather than queues. Then put every item in order of how long it can safely wait, shortest first. Twenty minutes, and the ranked list is what you work from.
  2. Inventory access this week. Every account, domain, payment processor and bank login the business depends on. Mark which are in your personal name and which have exactly one human who can get in. Most of this list can be fixed cheaply, which is why it is worth doing before anything harder.
  3. Fix the two worst access items now. Move them to the business, add a second authorised person, or write the recovery procedure down and have somebody else test it. For access items this is usually an afternoon; do not assume the same of anything else on the list.
  4. Write one rule for the decision that waits most often. The refund, the discount, the rush job. State the limit and the conditions under which the answer is yes without asking you. One paragraph, and it stops being a decision.
  5. Name the piece of knowledge only you have — the deployment, the migration, the automation you wrote and never documented — and put a date on writing it down. Not this week's work, but dated, because that is the category that punishes you at the worst possible moment.
  6. Pick the one relationship that runs through you and get a second person into the next ordinary conversation. Not an introduction email — a meeting they attend, then the one after that. This is the slowest thing on the list, and the only remedy is starting it early.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

What is owed is an owner who ran this diagnosis properly — found the real dependencies rather than the annoying ones, wrote the rules, moved the credentials, documented the knowledge, handed the recurring work over — and whose business got measurably worse for it: customers who had been buying the owner's judgement and left when they met the rule, or a delegated decision that cost more than every hour it saved. It is hard to find because "worse" has to be measured against an owner who did nothing, and no business runs both arms of that experiment. Until it is found, the asymmetry stands: this chapter recommends work whose benefit shows up only in an emergency that may not arrive, and whose cost is paid immediately and in full.
