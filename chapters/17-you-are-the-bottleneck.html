Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 17

You Are the Bottleneck

Every measurement in Part III was taken with you inside the business, holding the gaps together for free. This part asks what happens when you are not there — and it starts by correcting the question almost everyone asks first, which is about hours, and which is the wrong one.

The decision Which recurring work, which exceptional decisions and which pieces of operational knowledge stop moving when you are unavailable — and which one you take off yourself first.

Move 1 The situation

The usual version of this question is how many hours does the business need from me? It is the wrong question, and answering it well can leave you exactly where you started.

A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you. They are different quantities, they are not correlated, and the whole of this part depends on not confusing them.

An owner working five hours a week can be a total bottleneck: if every unusual decision, every refund over a certain size, every password and every awkward customer waits for Sunday evening, then the business does not run on five hours — it runs on your five hours, and it stops for a fortnight when you are in hospital. An owner working sixty hours can fail to be a bottleneck at all, if what fills those hours is ordinary capacity that a trained person could take on Monday. The first business looks efficient and is fragile. The second looks exhausting and is robust. Nothing on a timesheet distinguishes them.

So the diagnostic is not how long does this take? but what waits? Four things can wait for you, and they fail differently:

  • Recurring work — support, invoicing, the weekly deploy. Predictable, and the easiest to hand over, which is why owners start here and often stop here.
  • Exceptional decisions — the refund nobody has a rule for, the customer who wants a change to the contract, the quote outside the usual range. These are rare, which is why they never look urgent enough to delegate, and they queue behind you.
  • Operational knowledge — how the deployment actually works, which supplier will bend, what broke in 2021 and why the workaround is there. Undocumented and usually unnoticed until it is needed under pressure.
  • Credentials and relationships — the accounts in your name, the registrar login, the customer who buys because it is you. The most concentrated of the four, and the only one that can stop the business dead within a day.

A business can be excellent at the first and hollow underneath in the other three. That combination is the subject of this chapter, because it is both the most common shape and the one that reads, from the inside, as success.

Move 2 What actually happened

In March 2010, Patrick McKenzie published an account of running Bingo Card Creator — software that makes bingo cards for teachers — alongside a full-time job in Japan. The constraint was real and he documents it: "I leave the office at 7:30 PM on a very good day", with an hour and a half of commuting at each end. Against that, the business's time cost was "the peak was the week I launched (50 hours in 8 days)", a busy week that "spiked up to as many as 20 hours, and the average over the period is (to my best estimate) about 5 hours". By the time he wrote it, the business had "made it possible to quit my day job at the end of this month".

The method is the interesting part, and it is a method for workload. He divides everything into two ledgers: "build time assets" — things that save time later, whether code, documentation or marketing that keeps working — against time debt, where "time debt is anything that you do which will commit you to doing unavoidable work in the future". Support email is converted from events into processes: a dozen messages are really "six of Email A, 3 of Email B" and three genuinely unusual ones, so A and B stop being email and become a FAQ entry, better copy, or a fix. "Recurring tasks are a great thing to systemize and outsource." And the scope of the whole thing is chosen to fit the constraint, because "90% of the results of your business, and somewhere around 90% of the effort, are caused by non-coding activities" — so the work that gets cut is chosen against the calendar rather than against interest.

This is a genuinely good answer to the wrong question, and it is worth being precise about what it demonstrates. It shows that a business can be built whose recurring work fits inside five hours a week — the first of the four categories, handled superbly. It does not show that the other three were handled, and the post does not claim they were. Three unusual emails a week still waited for him. So did every decision about price, product and market. The evidence here is about volume of work, and volume of work is not dependence.

For what dependence looks like when the workload is near zero, take a business we have already counted. Chapter sixteen used Pinboard's eleven years of revenue; the same owner, in the same series of posts, describes what maintaining it alone eventually cost. In 2020 he spent the pandemic "moving Pinboard onto modern versions of PHP and MySQL", because "much of the core code on the site dated back to 2009-2010" and things had reached the point where he could not "run a bug-for-bug identical copy of the site on a modern laptop". He describes the operation itself in one line: "Doing this on a live system is like performing kidney transplants on a playing mariachi band."

Set the two together and the distinction is visible. Here is a business that had asked almost nothing of its owner week to week for a decade — the flat line of the previous chapter — and which nevertheless contained a piece of work that only one person on earth could do, that could not be scheduled, that had been deferred for years precisely because it was never this week's problem, and that would have been a catastrophe in the hands of anyone who did not remember why the 2009 code was written that way. Low routine labour did not mean low dependence. It concealed it, and it concealed it for about a decade.

What neither case establishes. Neither owner ran the experiment this chapter is really about: being unavailable. Nothing here shows what would have happened to either business during six weeks of illness, and no figure in either record measures dependence directly — both are inferred from the owner's own description of the work. Nor does the comparison show that McKenzie's business was more fragile than it looked; it shows that his published evidence answers a question about hours, and the question about dependence was not asked of it.

Move 3 The reasoning

Ask what waits, and for how long

The test is an absence, and you can run it on paper before running it for real. Take a normal week and ask what would happen if you were unreachable — not on holiday with a laptop, but genuinely unreachable — for one day, for one week, and for one month. Write down what stops in each case and what merely queues, because the difference is the whole diagnosis. Work that queues and then catches up is a workload problem. Work that stops, or that decays while it waits — a customer who goes elsewhere, a certificate that expires, an opportunity with a date on it — is a bottleneck.

Rank what you find by tolerable delay rather than by how much time it takes. A four-hour task that cannot wait a day outranks a two-day task that can safely wait a month. This ordering is unintuitive and it is the point of the exercise: the things that consume the most time are usually the things you have already made routine, and the things that cannot wait are usually rare enough to feel harmless.

Remove the categories in the order they fail

Four categories, and the sequence to work in is not the order of comfort:

  • Credentials and relationships first, because this is the only category that can stop the business within a day and the only one you can fix in an afternoon. Accounts in the business's name, a second person with access, the customer who has met somebody else. There is no skill transfer required, only intent.
  • Exceptional decisions second, by writing the rule instead of making the call. A refund limit, a discount ceiling, a set of conditions under which the answer is yes without asking. Every decision you convert into a rule is one that stops waiting for you, and the rule is usually better than your judgement because you wrote it calm.
  • Operational knowledge third, and it is the slowest. It is the next chapter's subject, because knowledge that lives only in your head cannot be delegated by deciding to delegate it.
  • Recurring work last — the opposite of what most owners do. It is the most visible, the most annoying and the least dangerous, and hiring someone to take it while the other three are untouched buys you time in a business that still stops when you do.

Automating creates a smaller, harder dependence

The developer's instinct is to solve all of this with code, and code is a real time asset — the case above is built on that insight. But automation converts a visible dependence into a concealed one. The task no longer takes your time, so it disappears from view; what remains is a system that only you understand, failing in ways only you can diagnose, which is precisely the third category. Pinboard's migration is the pure form of this: eleven years of a business running itself, and one unschedulable job underneath it that had no second candidate. If you automate, the automation is now the thing that needs documenting.

Boundaries

Being the bottleneck is a legitimate choice, and this chapter does not overturn it. A practice built to give one skilled person good work and a good income is a real thing to own, and in that shape the owner is the product; removing them removes the business. What is not legitimate is not knowing. Diagnose first, then choose — and if you choose to stay the bottleneck, the credentials and the rules still need doing, because illness does not care which model you picked.

Removing yourself has a cost, and it is not only money. Rules are blunter than judgement, documentation goes stale, a second person makes decisions you would not have made. Some businesses have been made worse by an owner who systematised the very thing customers were paying for. The correct comparison is not against a perfect version of you; it is against the version of the business that exists while you are unavailable.

The evidence here is thinner than the argument. Both cases are one-person software businesses, both are documented by their owners, and neither reports an involuntary absence. The four categories are a framework built from two records and from what they leave out — they are not a finding. Score your own business against them and treat disagreement as information about the framework.

Monday

  1. Write the absence list. Three columns — one day, one week, one month unreachable — and for each, what stops rather than queues. Twenty minutes, and it is the only diagnostic in this chapter that matters.
  2. Inventory the credentials this week. Every account, domain, payment processor and bank login the business depends on. Mark which are in your personal name and which have exactly one human who can access them. That list is your shortest-fuse dependence.
  3. Fix the two worst credential items now. Move them to the business, add a second holder, or write the recovery procedure down and tell someone where it is. This is an afternoon, not a project.
  4. Write one rule for the decision that waits most often. The refund, the discount, the rush job. State the limit and the conditions under which the answer is yes without asking you. One paragraph, and it stops being a decision.
  5. Name the piece of knowledge only you have — the deployment, the migration, the supplier who bends — and put a date on writing it down. Not this week's work, but dated, because that is the category that punishes you at the worst possible moment.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

What is owed is an owner who ran this diagnosis properly — found the real dependencies rather than the annoying ones, wrote the rules, moved the credentials, documented the knowledge, handed the recurring work over — and whose business got measurably worse for it: customers who had been buying the owner's judgement and left when they met the rule, or a delegated decision that cost more than every hour it saved. The shape is findable in principle, because the businesses that survive it publish and the ones that regret it sometimes do too. What makes it hard is that "worse" here has to be measured against a counterfactual owner who did nothing, and no business runs both arms of that experiment. Until it is found, note the asymmetry honestly: this chapter recommends work whose benefit shows up only in an emergency that may not arrive, and whose cost is paid immediately and in full.
