Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 17

Taking Work Off Yourself

By 2020 Maciej Cegłowski could no longer run an identical copy of Pinboard on a modern laptop. Much of the code had been written by "Past Me, a vindictive, inscrutable nemesis who devoted his life to sabotaging Present Me." Rebuilding it while the service ran was, he wrote, "like performing kidney transplants on a playing mariachi band." Everything depended on the one person holding the scalpel. How do you get some of that out of your head before you need to?

The decision Which dependency to reduce first, who could take the work, and what a bounded test must show before you can rely on the transfer.

The band keeps playing

Pinboard was the case in chapter sixteen: a business that paid its owner and still would not stop needing him. His 2020 anniversary post is about maintenance: "moving Pinboard onto modern versions of PHP and MySQL", on code that "dated back to 2009-2010", while customers kept saving links. The best case, he wrote, was that nobody noticed. The worst was that the music stopped and there was no way back. It did not stop. It was also one person's nerve and memory that kept it going.

You may not have eleven years of code. You probably have something that works the same way: the quote only you price, the account only you can log into, the customer who only talks to you. Hours do not find it. A task can take ten minutes a week and still stop a delivery when nobody else can authorise it. So imagine being unreachable for a day, a week, a month, and list what waits, what waiting costs, and how long the delay is tolerable. Recurring work, unusual decisions, access and relationships are headings to jog your memory, not a ranking. A customer deadline can matter more than a daily annoyance.

Noor is invented. She runs a small consultancy maintaining report tools; Ivo already helps on some deliveries. She wants Tuesday mornings free of routine support. Customers have an agreed noon response for reports that fail in the morning — a response, not a promise that every fault is fixed by noon. Her list:

Illustrative dependencies before choosing a transfer

She picks the morning response, because missing noon breaks a promise already made. The account renewal gets a date before it expires. The quote can wait until Thursday, because the prospect agreed to. A different deadline would change the order.

Knowledge moves. Intuition has to be grown.

At Gymdesk, Eran Galperin wrote, "for the first 5 years of the company’s existence, I was doing everything." When it grew, he hired people to take the small jobs, and found that he had misjudged them: "Tasks that would take 15-20 minutes of my day as a side activity, required hours for a dedicated person to complete." His example is a customer asking for a feature the product seems not to have. He would work out what the customer was really trying to do, whether it already existed, whether there was another way, and whether it was planned. That "would take me a couple of minutes". For a representative without his context, it "can take close to an hour, and require my participation at each one of those decision points."

His diagnosis is the sentence to keep: "I can transfer knowledge, but not the intuition and cross functional experience with all aspects of the business that came with years of performing all roles in the company." His response was broader than writing things down. He built a customer-service knowledge base with repeatable checks, and he began "hiring people to be responsible for entire aspects of the company" rather than single tasks. He is honest that it was unfinished: "still a work in progress, and I still feel too embedded in many of the processes". And he names the dilemma every handover hits: "Do I step in and make it better, or let the employee make a mistake and hopefully learn from it?"

The practical reading: a checklist carries steps, but the time goes at the decision points. Write those down with real examples and the reason for each choice, give the person the authority the decision needs, and expect judgment to grow through practice rather than arrive on a page. It does not have to stay yours forever. It will not leave you in one document either.

Find the person and the authority, not just the task

“Help with support” is too vague to hand over. Noor and Ivo agree that on the covered morning he checks incoming report failures, identifies the customer's situation, uses the approved recovery steps and sends an accurate response before noon, saying whether the fault is fixed, worked around or still under investigation. They reserve ninety minutes of his Tuesday for it, with no delivery booked against it. If a case threatens to overrun, or too many arrive, he contacts Noor while there is still time to keep the promise. She stays reachable for this first trial. She has not yet earned an uninterrupted morning.

He gets his own authorised access to the support inbox, the job history and the test environment — not her password. Noor writes down which recovery actions he may take, which changes need her approval and who takes an escalation if she is unavailable. Ivo agrees and says what he has never done before. Willingness, skill and a free calendar each need checking: a capable person with a full week is not cover.

Nobody suitable? Stop at a clear requirement: the work, the response time, the access, the authority, the skill and the hours needed. That is what chapter nineteen starts from. Changing the promise or arranging limited cover may suit you better than a new role.

Write enough to try it, then try it

Noor and Ivo work through an old, cleaned-up request together. She explains what she noticed, and which observation would have changed her answer; he writes the steps where he will look for them next time. Their version: confirm the report and the period; find the input and the error; compare with the known examples; try an approved recovery in the test environment; check the output; tell the customer the actual status. A new data source, totals that change without explanation, or anything outside the approved steps goes to Noor before any live change. The page names its owner and a review date, because the next software update will make part of it wrong.

Then they test it at the level the consequences allow. First a known case in the test environment, with Ivo explaining his reasoning aloud. Then a live morning within the limits. This is Galperin's dilemma, and they settle it in advance: Noor does not give hints at every hesitation, but they agree when Ivo must ask and when she must step in. Every intervention is written down, rather than quietly finishing the task and calling it delegated. The standard is the promised result in the available time, not the founder's speed.

The first live request has an unfamiliar column in its input. Ivo follows the stop rule instead of guessing. The explanation is in an old customer agreement Noor never mentioned. She supplies it; he answers before noon. These are the invented results:

Two bounded attempts, with remaining dependence visible
AttemptTime usedResult and dependence
First live requestIvo: 35 minutes. Noor: 10 minutes supplying context.Accurate response before noon; still depended on Noor's account history.
Later comparable requestIvo: 25 minutes. Noor: no intervention during the task.Accurate response before noon using the corrected record; result reviewed afterwards.

Ten minutes of Noor on the first request, none on the second: the gap was a missing document, not a missing skill, and linking the agreement into the instructions closed it for this kind of request. That is the result, and it is also its limit. Two requests do not show that Ivo can handle every exception, three failures at once, or a week without her. Preparation and review cost hours too, and those go into the period review. So Noor's next step is one full covered morning, with a named person to escalate to if she is unreachable. “Ask Noor” is a good training arrangement. It is not independence.

Two things need their own test. Access: a recovery route for the software account, tried by the person who would use it. Relationships: a customer who only talks to Noor needs to meet Ivo in ordinary work and learn what he can decide; a copied contact list transfers neither trust nor permission. Some of it may stay personal, and it is better written down as a limit than promised away. If you keep specialised work deliberately, because doing it is the point of the practice, say what can wait, who communicates during an absence, and what cover is feasible.

Noor now has one task Ivo can do on a Tuesday without her, a full covered morning still to try, and a precise list of what still comes back to her. Ivo has no more time to give. Whether to find more help, and what it would cost, is chapter nineteen.

Monday · one task, one person, one page

Do this one thing. Pick the task that most often waits for you. Write its three decision points — the moments you choose, not the clicks — with one real past example and the reason for your choice at each. You now hold one page someone else could try the task from.

Nobody to give it to? Write the requirement instead: the work, when it must be done, the access and authority it needs, the skill, and the hours. That page is Monday done, and it is the input to chapter nineteen.

If you have another hour:

  1. Give the page and one old case to the person who could take the work and ask them to talk you through what they would do. Note the exact point where the page failed.
  2. Run one supervised live attempt within agreed limits. Record the time, every intervention and its cause.
  3. Test the access. Give the person their own authorised access and have them try the recovery route before they need it.
What this chapter rests on, and what it cannot show

Pinboard's and Gymdesk's accounts are their founders' own. They describe concentrated knowledge, maintenance on a live service and an unfinished handover; neither reports an absence test, and Galperin's describes no measured before-and-after. Patrick McKenzie's 2010 account of running a software business on about five hours a week describes systemising recurring tasks, not what would stop during an absence. Noor, Ivo and their results are invented to show the reasoning.

Still owed: an owner who reduced a dependency through a tested transfer and whose business became worse because the transferred work lost something customers valued.
