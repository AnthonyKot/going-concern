Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 21

Meetings That Aren't Theater

With three people in the business you do not have meetings; you have interruptions, and you are the one being interrupted, because every question still comes to you. The alternative you are avoiding — a fixed meeting, at a fixed time, whether or not there is anything to say — sounds like theatre — the fixed hour that exists because it is in the calendar. It is the opposite of it, if it is built right.

The decision Whether the business gets one owned, timed, repeating meeting — the same shape every time, held whether or not anyone feels like it — or keeps running on got a minute?, which is also a meeting rhythm — the one nobody has costed, and the one in which you are the agenda.

Move 1 The situation

You did not start a business to sit in meetings, and with two or four people you have managed not to. What you have instead is the tap on the shoulder. Got a minute? Which supplier. Whether to take the odd job. What the customer on the phone was promised last week. Each one is short, each one is reasonable, and each one lands on you in the middle of something else, because you are the place where the answers are kept — chapter seventeen's bottleneck, seen from the inside. Nobody calls this a meeting. It is one: a meeting with no start time, no end, no agenda, and always the same attendee.

The thing you are avoiding is the fixed one — every morning at nine, or every Monday, the same people, the same questions, whether or not there is news. You know the kind, or have heard about it: an hour long, for the benefit of whoever ran it, producing a feeling of management and nothing else. That is theatre, and you are right to refuse it.

But the refusal has a cost you are not counting, because it arrives in pieces: the ten interruptions a day, the decision made twice because two people asked separately, the thing nobody knew was blocked until Thursday. The question is not whether to have meetings. It is whether the meeting the business already has — the unstructured one, with you as the room — is cheaper than a structured one with a clock on it. The case below is a company that tried it every way, and found that the theatre was not where it expected.

Move 2 What actually happened

Chapter twenty's Turnbull built a calendar so that a firing would not depend on his nerve. A year after that post, in February 2016, he wrote up the same kind of repair for the other thing that had been running on mood — what Groove's remote team had tried in the way of meeting rhythms, with what he liked and did not like about each. A few paragraphs in he gives a disclaimer this chapter takes at its word: "Our team is different from your team, and what works for us might be different from what works for you." He had tried four by then.

First, none: "we scheduled no meetings, and opted only to meet on demand when things arose. To put it bluntly, it simply didn't work." The reason is the tap on the shoulder, in a remote team's clothes. On-demand meetings "often started in the form of an instant message saying something like ‘hey can we chat about this?’, they were the remote equivalent of the tap-on-the-shoulder interruption that destroys so much focus and productivity in office environments." Second, a Monday call — weekends, last week, this week — which they kept, with the cost stated: "An hour-long meeting can certainly be disruptive." Third, a Friday call for culture only, a poker tournament over a voice call. It lasted a few months: "perhaps the biggest mistake we made was trying to schedule non-work activities during the work day", so that "We felt guilty doing these calls because we had work we needed to do." A meeting with no work in it, in other words, is the first one skipped when there is work.

Fourth, the one he did not want. It came out of a coaching retreat. Daily standups, "which we had previously only done via Slack posts", would now be live, at ten in the morning, Monday to Friday — a change that, in his words, "scared me a bit", because a meeting every day looked like the worst interruption of all. "But Kris, our business coach, pushed us to try it." What made him willing was something the coach showed him at the same retreat: a video of another company's daily standup, in which, as Turnbull describes it, "nearly 20 people participated and shared updates, and the meeting still took less than 7 minutes. It made our 9-person 45-minute meetings look amateur in comparison." Nearly twenty people in under seven minutes, against nine in forty-five. The difference was not fewer people or fewer meetings. "That missing piece is structure."

Structure, in the post, is concrete. An owner: "Lesley owns meetings for our team, and handles scheduling and moderating." A clock on every part: "Monday Recap Meeting — 30 Minutes", of which twenty are for weekends and personal things, then good news, then standups at "No more than 60 seconds per person, often less", then thirty seconds of numbers, thirty seconds to close. And the same skeleton every day: "Tuesday–Thursday Daily Standup — 10 Minutes" with the identical segments minus the twenty of talk; "Friday Update — 20 Minutes", the same again plus ten minutes of the week's customer feedback. The culture time did not disappear. It was given a fixed twenty minutes inside a work meeting. That is the chapter's reading of why it survived: put there, it could not be skipped for work.

The result he reports is a feeling, not a figure: "after a couple of months of this", "our team has never been operating more efficiently." No count of interruptions before and after, no hours recovered. What the post establishes is narrower and still the point: meetings that had run forty-five minutes with nine people were replaced by one with ten minutes on the clock, and the thing that changed was the structure, not the number of meetings.

Move 3 The reasoning

The theatre is the unstructured meeting

A meeting is theatre when it has no owner, no clock and no fixed shape — and by that test the meeting most small businesses already run, got a minute?, is the most theatrical of all: it performs urgency, it is attended by whoever wandered in, and it ends when the owner gives up. Groove's first rhythm was no rhythm, and it produced exactly the silo-and-interruption pattern that a shop with three people produces without noticing. The scheduled meeting you are avoiding is not the alternative to that. A scheduled meeting with no structure is the same theatre with a start time — the forty-five-minute version — and the fixed one that works is a third thing.

Each part answers a way the meeting goes wrong. An owner who is not you, if there is anyone else it can be: someone whose job is the clock and the agenda, so that the meeting is not another place where you are the room. A clock on every segment, not on the meeting — sixty seconds a person for done, next, blocked; thirty seconds for the numbers — because a meeting with a total length and no internal one drifts until the total is reached. The same shape every time, so that nobody prepares, nobody decides whether today's is worth attending, and the thing that was blocked on Tuesday is said on Tuesday.

Groove's minute counts are Groove's, and the post says so. What transfers is the ratio, and the direction it runs: a structured meeting with more people cost less than an unstructured one with fewer. For two people at a counter, this book's translation — and it is a translation, not something the post shows — is three minutes at opening, standing up: a minute each, and the numbers segment is chapter twelve's weekly cash figure said out loud, which is where chapter twenty-two starts. Monday's fifth item is what tests it.

Held whether or not anyone feels like it

The Friday call is the instructive failure, because it was the meeting everyone liked. It died because it was optional in practice — a meeting with no work in it cannot compete with work, and the guilt Turnbull describes is what an optional meeting produces in people who have things to do. The daily standup survived for the opposite reason: it was the same whether there was news or not, and it was short enough that nothing to report cost sixty seconds. A meeting that is held when there is something to discuss is an interruption with a calendar invitation. A meeting held at the same time regardless is where things get said before they become urgent.

The boundary: the rhythm is for coordination, not for decisions. Ten minutes a day is where the blocked thing gets named and the number gets heard; it is not where the supplier gets changed or the hire gets decided, and a standing meeting that starts making decisions will grow back toward the length it had before. The decisions have their own dates — chapter twenty put one in the calendar — and the daily meeting's job is to notice that a decision is needed and send it there.

What this case cannot show is that the rhythm works for a business whose people are not at screens. Groove's team was remote, and the on-demand interruption arrived as a message; a workshop's arrives as a person. The mechanism is the same — the answer is still in your head and everyone still has to come to you for it — but the post is silent on a team in one room, and so is this chapter beyond a proposal: that a fixed three minutes at opening costs less than a day of being asked, which the count in Monday's fifth item will confirm or not.

Monday

  1. For the next five working days, keep a tally of the meetings nobody calls meetings. Every got a minute?, every question at your desk: minutes, and how many people were in it. At the end of the week multiply minutes by people and add it up. That is the cost of the rhythm you have now, and it is the number item 5 is measured against.
  2. Fix one slot. Same time every working day, at the start of it, ten minutes or fewer for five people or fewer, standing if you are in the same room. Put it in the calendar for four weeks before anyone can object to the first one.
  3. Write the skeleton on one line and do not vary it. Per person, a minute — Groove's figure, and as good a starting number as any: what got done, what is next, what is blocked. Then half a minute: this week's cash figure from chapter twelve, read aloud by you. Then stop, even if there is time left — especially if there is time left.
  4. Give the clock to someone else. Whoever is not you and is willing owns the start, the order and the stop. If it is a two-person business and the other person is your partner, they own it; the point is that you attend rather than run.
  5. After four weeks of the slot, tally again for five days. Same count as item 1. If the interruptions did not fall, the meeting is theatre and this chapter is wrong for your business; drop it and say so. If they did, keep it and do not let it grow — the day it passes ten minutes, cut something, and the thing to cut is whatever started being decided instead of reported.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a small business that built exactly this — an owner, a clock, the same shape daily, held regardless — and had it become theatre anyway: ten minutes of people reporting to the ritual rather than to each other, the blocked thing still unsaid because sixty seconds is not where anyone admits it, the interruptions unchanged because the answers still lived in the owner's head and the meeting only added a place to ask. That is a real way this advice fails and the Groove post cannot rule it out; it reports two months and a feeling. The nearer limit is also unmeasured: for a business of two, the fixed rhythm may cost more than the interruptions it replaces, and nothing here shows where the line sits. Monday's fifth item is the test this chapter offers in place of the case it does not have — count before, count after, and believe the count.
