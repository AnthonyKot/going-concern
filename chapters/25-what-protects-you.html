Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 25

What Protects You

Something is working, and now you are afraid of losing it. The fear reaches for instruments — a patent, a trademark, a non-disclosure agreement, an exclusivity clause, secrecy — and each one feels like protection in the moment you buy it. Whether it protects anything is decided later, on the day the thing you were afraid of actually happens, and that is the only day worth judging it by.

The decision For each thing you are tempted to buy because it feels protective, what it actually does on the day of the event it is meant for, what using it would cost on that day, and whether the money is better put into the one kind of protection at this scale that has to be earned rather than bought — being the one the customers choose, and come back to.

Move 1 The situation

The product sells, or the method works, or the customers keep coming back, and for the first time you have something that someone else might want to take. The feeling is specific: a competitor down the road could do what you do; a supplier could sell your design to someone bigger; the person you trained could leave with the list. Every one of those is possible, and the market for things that make the feeling go away is large — registrations, agreements, clauses, a lawyer's letter kept in a drawer. Chapter twenty-four ended on the agreements you clicked: a reason to read them, not a reason to feel protected by them. This chapter asks the same of the ones you would buy on purpose.

The question to ask of each one, and the one the sellers are not paid to raise: on the day the thing you fear actually happens, what does this instrument do? Not what it represents, not what it signals to an investor you do not have, but what changes in the outcome because you hold it. Some instruments change the outcome. Some give you the right to start an expensive process you have not budgeted for. And some do nothing at all except make the months before the event more comfortable — which is a real thing to buy, as long as you know that is what you bought.

Move 2 What actually happened

Studio Neat has been in this book twice — chapter three for selling the Glif before it existed, chapter nine for what its second campaign showed about price. Two people and a small moulded clamp that reached its funding goal eighty-five minutes after one link in October 2010 and shipped in December. The same long post those chapters drew on has a section neither of them opened, headed A Word on Patents, and it starts with the fear from Move 1 in the founders' own words: "I remember when our first project, the Glif, started to blow up, and I was freaked out we didn't have a patent on the design."

What follows is their own understanding, and they say what it is worth before they give it: "make sure you contact an expert rather than relying solely on the advice from two dudes with no legal expertise." With that said, the understanding they arrived at is that the path to a patent is "a black hole that sucks up money and spits out sadness", and that the thing most people believe about a patent is the thing it does not do: "Many people seem to have the misconception that patents are magical things that act like a force field around your product, preventing anyone from ever ripping it off." Then the correction: "Patents do nothing to prevent people from stealing your idea. All they do is give you the legal authority to take action if you believe someone is infringing. You would still need to hire a lawyer to take the perp to court." The rest of what they learned is the American system as they understood it — that the cheaper kind, a design patent, "only protects the visual ornamentation of an object, not its function"; that "Most patents only protect infringement within the United States" — and they stay there, as two non-lawyers' reading of one jurisdiction.

The decision: "we have realized that patents are basically designed to protect large corporations, and the idea of a small company of two people obtaining a patent is fairly ridiculous. We decided to pass."

And the thing they had been afraid of is on the same page, not as a fear but as a fact: "To put things in perspective, since launching the Glif, there have been a few direct imitations that have cropped up, out of Germany and Taiwan." They link to both — Amazon listings. The post does not say whether the copies appeared before or after they stopped wanting a patent, and it does not need to, because what they say about them is the reading they had just given: "There is not much we can do, other than shrug our shoulders and carry on." They then run the counterfactual themselves, with the copies in view: "Let's imagine for a moment we did have a heavy-duty international utility patent. How would that change things? Would we really be willing to sink the time and money into going after them? Our guess is no. We'd rather just spend that time designing more great products." And they name what they were counting on instead: "As long as our brand remains strong, we think enough customers will choose the real deal versus the knockoff."

That is a belief, stated at the time, and the post does not measure it. Nothing in it reports what the imitations did to sales, whether the brand held, or what a customer who bought the copy would have paid for the original. What the case establishes is narrower and is enough: a business at the reader's scale met the exact event a legal protection exists to prevent, had read — before or while it happened — what the protection would do on that day, declined it, and put its money into something else.

Move 3 The reasoning

Protection is what changes the outcome on the day

An instrument protects you only if it changes what happens on the day of the event it was bought for. If what it gives you is the right to begin a process — a claim, a suit, a demand — then what you bought is standing, and the protection is the process, priced separately and paid later. Studio Neat's sentence about patents is the general case stated for one instrument: the filing does not stop the copy; it lets you hire a lawyer to argue about the copy. The same question goes to the others the fear reaches for, and the post does not answer it for them; you do, instrument by instrument. On the morning the person you trained opens the list at a competitor's desk, what does the non-disclosure agreement do — stop the opening, or give you a claim about it? On the day the supplier ships your design to someone bigger, what does the exclusivity clause do? Where the honest answer is a claim, what you bought is standing, and the process behind it has a price the instrument's price does not include. Which answer is honest is a question for someone who practises where you do, and it is the question Monday's last item puts to them.

This is not an argument against buying them. It is an argument for buying them with the second price written next to the first. Gerhardt and Provost ran that sum in the open — would they sink the time and money into going after the imitators, and their guess was no — and once the answer is no, what the instrument would have bought is the comfort of the months before the event, at a patent's price. The sum comes out differently for a different business. Chapter four's Cydoc held two United States patents, in healthcare software sold to regulated buyers behind a four-thousand-dollar integration, and chapter four's point about them was that a patent is evidence of novelty and not of demand — a different question, asked of a different business, and neither chapter says whether the other's decision was wrong. Two decisions at two sums are not in tension; the difference is the sum, not the principle.

What protects a business of this size

What they leaned on instead they call brand, and they say what they mean by it: enough customers choosing the real deal over the copy. At this size that choice leaves a record, and the book has been keeping it since chapter eleven — the customers who came back, in the renew-and-reorder column, against the ones who paid once. A copy takes the customers who were never choosing you — who wanted the object at the lowest price and would have bought the copy had it come first. It takes the customers who chose you only if the thing they chose you for is in the object alone, and at this scale it rarely is: it is in the record of what you did last time, in the answer to the email, in the next product arriving before the copy of the last one is finished. They shrug in one sentence and in the next say where the time goes instead: "We'd rather just spend that time designing more great products." Read together, that is a decision about where the protection actually lives.

Being chosen protects the business only where the customers who chose it are the ones paying for it. Chapter twenty-two's first figure — returning against new — says how many are; its concentration figure says who the largest are by name and how much of the money they hold, and whether those names are in the renew-and-reorder column is the question. Where the money comes from people who never chose you, a copy takes them, and no brand holds what was never held. And the paper from chapter twenty-four is not made redundant by any of this. The sentence that makes the work yours is protection of the first kind: it changes what is true on the day. Your terms are the second kind, and chapter twenty-four's order — written, delivered, read once as the customer would — is what decides whether the claim they give you is worth anything. A reader in the Netherlands or anywhere in Europe holds different instruments from the two the post names — registered designs, trade marks, the routes and costs of each — and which of them changes the outcome on the day is Monday's last item, not this chapter's to say.

Monday

  1. Write the event, not the fear. One sentence: the thing I am afraid of is [who] doing [what] with [which part of the business]. If the sentence will not fill in, there is no event yet, and nothing below applies until there is.
  2. For every protective thing you hold or are about to buy, write what it does on that day, in one line. If the line contains sue, claim or enforce, write what you think that process would cost next to the instrument's price — or the word unknown — and add them. That total is what the protection costs; the first figure alone is what the comfort costs. Item 5 replaces your figure with a real one.
  3. Price the event from the record. From chapter eleven's renew-and-reorder column and chapter twenty-two's returning-against-new figure, with the concentration figure's names beside them: which customers would a copy actually take — the ones who came back, or the ones who never did — and what their money was last year. Set that against item 2's total.
  4. Put the next money into the thing that cannot be copied, with a date. The next product, the next version, the record, the reply time — whichever is the reason your customers chose you rather than the object. Date it, because designing more great products is a plan only when the next one has a ship date.
  5. Ask an expert once, with items 1 to 3 on paper — in the hour chapter twenty-four told you to buy, if you have not bought it yet. Studio Neat's own instruction. Take the event, the instruments and the two prices to someone who practises in your jurisdiction, and ask which instrument would change the outcome on the day — including the ones you have not heard of. Then decide, and write down which price you paid.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business that ran this reasoning correctly — priced the instrument with its process, found the sum said no, and put the money into being chosen again — and lost the customers who had chosen it to the copy anyway, because the thing they had chosen it for turned out to be in the object after all, and the brand did not hold. Studio Neat's post is not that case, and it is not the opposite either: it is the bet placed, with no report of how it paid. The nearer thing the chapter lacks is any case for the other instruments the fear reaches for — insurance above all, which is protection of the first kind when it pays and of no kind when it does not, and for which no small business's account with a policy and a claim was found. Until either arrives, the test in Monday's second item is what stands in: what does it do on the day, and what does that cost.
