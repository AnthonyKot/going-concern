Part V · Will it last? · Chapter 25

What Protects You

Something is working, and now you are afraid of losing it. The fear reaches for instruments — a patent, a trademark, a non-disclosure agreement, an exclusivity clause, secrecy — and each one feels like protection in the moment you buy it. Whether it protects anything is decided later, on the day the thing you were afraid of actually happens, and that is the only day worth judging it by.

The decision For each thing you are tempted to buy because it feels protective, what it actually does on the day of the event it is meant for, what using it would cost on that day, and whether the money is better put into the one kind of protection at this scale that cannot be copied — being the one the customers choose again.

Move 1 The situation

The product sells, or the method works, or the customers keep coming back, and for the first time you have something that someone else might want to take. The feeling is specific: a competitor down the road could do what you do; a supplier could sell your design to someone bigger; the person you trained could leave with the list. Every one of those is possible, and the market for things that make the feeling go away is large — registrations, agreements, clauses, a lawyer's letter kept in a drawer.

What none of the sellers of those things will tell you is the question this chapter asks of each one: on the day the thing you fear actually happens, what does this instrument do? Not what it represents, not what it signals to an investor you do not have, but what changes in the outcome because you hold it. Some instruments change the outcome. Some give you the right to start an expensive process you have not budgeted for. And some do nothing at all except make the months before the event more comfortable — which is a real thing to buy, as long as you know that is what you bought.

Move 2 What actually happened

Studio Neat has been in this book twice — chapter three for selling the Glif before it existed, chapter nine for what its second campaign showed about price. Two people, no outside money, a small physical product that took off in January 2011. The same long post those chapters drew on has a section neither of them opened, headed A Word on Patents, and it starts with the fear from Move 1 in the founders' own words: "I remember when our first project, the Glif, started to blow up, and I was freaked out we didn't have a patent on the design."

What follows is their own understanding, and they say what it is worth before they give it: "make sure you contact an expert rather than relying solely on the advice from two dudes with no legal expertise." With that said, the understanding they arrived at is that the path to a patent is "a black hole that sucks up money and spits out sadness", and that the thing most people believe about a patent is the thing it does not do: "Many people seem to have the misconception that patents are magical things that act like a force field around your product, preventing anyone from ever ripping it off." The sentence this chapter is built on is the correction: "Patents do nothing to prevent people from stealing your idea. All they do is give you the legal authority to take action if you believe someone is infringing. You would still need to hire a lawyer to take the perp to court." Their other points are about the American system as they understood it — that the cheaper kind, a design patent, "only protects the visual ornamentation of an object, not its function"; that "Most patents only protect infringement within the United States" — and they stay there, as two non-lawyers' reading of one jurisdiction.

The decision: "we have realized that patents are basically designed to protect large corporations, and the idea of a small company of two people obtaining a patent is fairly ridiculous. We decided to pass."

Then the thing they had been afraid of happened. "To put things in perspective, since launching the Glif, there have been a few direct imitations that have cropped up, out of Germany and Taiwan." They link to them. And their response is the response of people who had already decided what a patent would and would not have done: "There is not much we can do, other than shrug our shoulders and carry on." They then run the counterfactual themselves: "Let's imagine for a moment we did have a heavy-duty international utility patent. How would that change things? Would we really be willing to sink the time and money into going after them? Our guess is no. We'd rather just spend that time designing more great products." And they name what they were counting on instead: "As long as our brand remains strong, we think enough customers will choose the real deal versus the knockoff."

That is a belief, stated at the time, and the post does not measure it. Nothing in it reports what the imitations did to sales, whether the brand held, or what a customer who bought the copy would have paid for the original. What the case establishes is narrower and is enough: a business at the reader's scale faced the exact event a legal protection exists to prevent, had declined the protection on a stated reading of what it would do on that day, and put its money into something else.

Move 3 The reasoning

Protection is what changes the outcome on the day

An instrument protects you only if it changes what happens on the day of the event it was bought for. If what it gives you is the right to begin a process — a claim, a suit, a demand — then what you bought is standing, and the protection is the process, priced separately and paid later. Studio Neat's sentence about patents is the general case stated for one instrument: the filing does not stop the copy; it lets you hire a lawyer to argue about the copy. The same test applies to the others the fear reaches for. A non-disclosure agreement does nothing on the day the disclosure happens; it gives you a claim afterwards. An exclusivity clause does not stop the supplier selling elsewhere; it gives you grounds. Each is worth exactly the process behind it, and the process has a price the instrument's price does not include.

This is not an argument against buying them. It is an argument for buying them with the second price written next to the first. Gerhardt and Provost ran that sum in the open — would they sink the time and money into going after the imitators, and the answer was no — and once the answer is no, the instrument's value is the comfort it gave before the event, which is what they were paying to avoid. The sum comes out differently for a different business. Chapter four's Cydoc held patents, in healthcare software, where the stakes, the buyers and the money were all of another order; two decisions at two scales are not in tension, and the difference is the sum, not the principle.

What protects a business of this size

The thing they leaned on instead is the thing this book has been building since chapter eleven: being the one the customer chooses again. A copy takes the customers who were never choosing you — who wanted the object at the lowest price and would have bought the copy had it come first. It takes the customers who chose you only if the thing they chose you for is in the object alone, and at this scale it rarely is: it is in the record of what you did last time, in the answer to the email, in the next product arriving before the copy of the last one is finished. "We'd rather just spend that time designing more great products" is not a shrug. It is a decision about where the protection actually lives.

Which sets the boundary. Being chosen again protects the business only where the customers who chose it are the ones paying for it — the concentration figure from chapter twenty-two says whether they are. Where the money comes from people who never chose you, a copy takes them, and no brand holds what was never held. And the paper from chapter twenty-four is not made redundant by any of this: the agreements that are yours to write — who owns the work, what your terms say — are protection of the first kind, because they change what is true on the day rather than what you may argue about afterwards.

Two limits on the case, stated as the case states them. The reading of patents is two founders' understanding of American law, offered with their own instruction to ask an expert, and a reader in the Netherlands or anywhere in Europe faces a different regime — registered designs, trade marks, the routes and costs of each — that this chapter does not describe and the source does not cover. And the bet they made is reported as a belief, not a result: the post says the brand would hold, and does not come back to say whether it did. The chapter uses the decision and the reasoning. It cannot borrow an outcome the source does not report.

Monday

  1. Write the event, not the fear. One sentence: the thing I am afraid of is [who] doing [what] with [which part of the business]. If the sentence will not fill in, there is no event yet, and nothing below applies until there is.
  2. For every protective thing you hold or are about to buy, write what it does on that day, in one line. If the line contains sue, claim or enforce, write the lawyer's estimate for that process next to the instrument's price, and add them. That total is what the protection costs; the first figure alone is what the comfort costs.
  3. Price the event from the record. From chapter eleven's columns and chapter twenty-two's concentration figure: which customers would a copy actually take — the ones who chose you again, or the ones who never did — and what their money was last year. Set that against item 2's total.
  4. Put the next money into the thing that cannot be copied, with a date. The next product, the next version, the record, the reply time — whichever is the reason your customers chose you rather than the object. Date it, because designing more great products is a plan only when the next one has a ship date.
  5. Ask an expert once, with items 1 to 3 on paper. Studio Neat's own instruction. Take the event, the instruments and the two prices to someone who practises in your jurisdiction, and ask which instrument would change the outcome on the day — including the ones you have not heard of. Then decide, and write down which price you paid.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is a business that ran this reasoning correctly — priced the instrument with its process, found the sum said no, and put the money into being chosen again — and lost the customers who had chosen it to the copy anyway, because the thing they had chosen it for turned out to be in the object after all, and the brand did not hold. Studio Neat's post is not that case, and it is not the opposite either: it is the bet placed, with no report of how it paid. The nearer thing the chapter lacks is any case for the other instruments the fear reaches for — insurance above all, which is protection of the first kind when it pays and of no kind when it does not, and for which no small business's account with a policy and a claim was found. Until either arrives, the test in Monday's second item is what stands in: what does it do on the day, and what does that cost.
