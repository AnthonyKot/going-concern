The Business That Does Not Invoice

Most of the businesses this book is written for take money across a counter or in a chat, on the day, in cash or a transfer, with no contract, no invoice and no month-end. None of the businesses in this book is one of them — because that trade does not publish, and this book only uses what can be checked.

The decision Which of the instruments in the last thirteen chapters survive contact with a trade that has no signature, no invoice and no month — and what stands in for the ones that do not.

Move 1 The situation

You sell to whoever walks in, or whoever messages. They pay when they take the thing, or they do not take it. There is no contract to sign, no invoice to send, and no month in which revenue is recognised; there is Saturday, and there is the week after. Your supplier is paid before your customer is, your prices move with the supplier's, and if you keep a record it is the bank app, a notes file, and what you remember. You may also have no accountant, no bookkeeper, and no salary you could go back to.

Thirteen chapters have handed you instruments, and every one was built on a business that had a dashboard: a filing, a founder's monthly post, an outcomes report to a standard. That is not a bias the book chose. It is a bias in what exists to be checked, and it has a shape you should know about before you go on: the businesses that publish are the ones with the instruments already, so the evidence in this book comes disproportionately from the kind of business that could measure itself. Yours is the kind that could not, or did not, and it is the more common kind by a wide margin.

So you have been translating. The lease became a stall, the email became a message, the Trello board became a notebook, the twelve-month stopping rule became a number of units. Most of those translations were made in passing, in the chapter where they arose. This chapter makes them once, in one place, and says plainly which instruments do not translate at all.

Move 2 What actually happened

Nothing that can be shown here. This is the one chapter in the book whose case is an absence, and the absence is the finding.

Consider where the book's cases came from. Webvan and Instacart from filings a public company is obliged to make. Everpix from a dataset a dead company chose to publish. Bingo Card Creator, Plausible, ConvertKit and WP Curve from founders who wrote monthly posts with the numbers in them, for an audience of other founders. Cydoc from a post-mortem. Turing from an outcomes standard a school opted into. Every one of them was a business with a reason to write its numbers down and somewhere to put them — a regulator, a community, a coalition. Each was also, not by coincidence, a business that already had the numbers to write.

A café on a high street, a dressmaker selling through a phone, a mobile trade paid in cash at the door: none of these has a filing duty worth reading, an audience of peers waiting for the monthly report, or a standard to opt into. They leave a trace in aggregate statistics and in the memory of the person who ran them, and the second of those is the source this book has refused from the start, for the reason given in chapter ten: the seller describing his own month is the weakest evidence there is, and describing it years later is weaker still.

Which leaves a gap that has two edges. The first is that no case follows, and the reasoning below is therefore reasoning alone, held to the standard chapter twelve set for its three weekly numbers: a prescription without a case, declared as such. The second edge cuts the other way and is the reason this chapter exists at all. If the businesses that publish are the ones that could measure, then the instruments in this book have been tested only on businesses that found them easy. A trade that cannot sign, invoice or close a month is the harder test, and the honest position is that the book has not run it. What follows is the best translation available, and the debt at the end is the largest in the book.

Move 3 The reasoning

Take the instruments in the order the book gave them. For each, what it was built on; what it becomes in a trade with no signature and no month; and where it stops.

The number hidden in the fixed cost

Chapter one's principle does not scale; its number does. A twelve-month lease on a room, a generator, a phone contract and a supplier who wants paying up front have a breakeven exactly as a warehouse does — but here the floor does not sit still. Where prices move faster than the calendar, a breakeven in money is stale before it is honoured. Compute it in units instead: how many of the thing you sell, at this week's cost of making it, cover this week's rent, this week's supplier and the minimum you take home. That number is rebuilt every time the supplier's price moves, and a number that is rebuilt weekly is still a number, which is more than most owners of this kind of business can state.

Asking, and the ledger it fills

Chapters two and eight survive the change of channel with the noun swapped. Across the counter: where do you get this now, and what annoys you about it. In a message to someone who asked the price last month and went quiet: what stopped you. The ledger those answers fill is a notes file, one line per person, dates and amounts and things they said — and never a compliment. The one rule that tightens rather than loosens in this trade is who counts as paid. A screenshot of a transfer is not money until it clears; "I'll take it" in a chat is chapter two's enthusiasm, worth zero. Count only paid and collected.

The rung, when nobody signs

Chapter three's ladder assumed a buyer who could commit in writing. Here the fourth rung is money sent before the thing is made or bought in: the deposit by transfer, the half up front before the tailor is paid, the standing order for Friday's lunch. Where nobody preorders at all, the pipeline is the smallest version of the trade that can run on the days you are not employed — a stall, a weekend, a pop-up — and its takings over three months are the evidence, read alongside how many people walked past. The second axis is unchanged and sharper: could you make forty of these at this week's fabric price if forty people paid, and if you could not, what happens to the forty deposits.

The stopping rule, in units

Chapter four's rule needs a threshold, a date and a consequence, and only the first changes shape. A money threshold six months out is a different number by the time it arrives; write it in units sold to people who could have said no, or in weeks of floor covered, and let the money convert on the day. The consequence is wider than the book's cases made it look, because there may be no salary to return to: the honest menu is stop, cut the line that is not moving, or take paid work of any kind and keep the stall on Saturdays — the last being the version of Galperin's retreat available to someone who never had a desk.

Who it is for, when you do not choose who walks in

Chapter six's refusal survives, but the instrument is different. A business that cannot pick its customers still picks: by what is on the menu, what it costs, and when the door is open. The three you turn away are written as those decisions — the seven o'clock full English you do not serve, the size you do not stock, the hour you do not answer — and each refusal should buy something you can name. If it buys nothing, it is not a position; it is a gap in the menu.

Where they already are

Chapter seven's four questions were never about the internet. The school gate at half past eight clears all four. So does the church group, the estate's chat, the market on a Tuesday. What accumulates in this trade is the list of people whose number you have and who have bought once; what is contemporaneous is the boosted post, which stops the day the money does. The two-cent arithmetic is airtime: what it costs to reach a stranger against what it costs to message someone who has already paid, and if you do not know the second number it is because you are not doing it.

The record, and the people who vanish

Chapters ten and eleven are the ones that ask most of a business with no month-end, and they are the ones that matter most, because nothing else will tell you whether the trade repeats. One line per sale in whatever you keep: date, what, price, paid how, known face or new one, and — the column that costs nothing and gets harder to fake — whether they were seen again. The could-not-reach line is the people who paid once and were not seen again, and in a walk-in trade that line is most of the ledger, which is exactly why it has to be counted rather than assumed. The outcome sentence is small and repeated: half of this week's new faces back by the end of the month; the dress worn out of the house and a second ordered.

Three numbers on Sunday night

Chapter twelve's weekly set survives whole, with one substitution and one addition. Cash, after what is owed to the supplier and before what will not be paid — and, where prices move, what that cash will still buy at next week's restock, because cash that sits is not standing still. Committed demand has no signed line here and should not be given a false one; what stands in for it is the count of faces seen before, read against the same week last year. The operating quantity is things sold, never things made or bought in, because stock bought on a quiet page is the way this trade dies while its shelves look full.

What does not translate

Three things, and they should be said in the same breath as the rest. The first is the book's own apparatus: the counter-case, the register of claims, the check against a source. A trade that publishes nothing cannot be checked from outside, which means the only record of your business that will ever exist is the one you keep, and the discipline this book borrows from filings and standards has to come from you, unwatched. The second is time. Every instrument here was built on a business that could hold a price for ten asks, wait six months for a threshold, or watch six comparable weeks; where the currency moves faster than that, the honest answer is that the intervals shrink and the book does not know by how much. The third is the salary. A surprising amount of Part I assumed there was one to keep, one to leave, or one to return to, and the reader with none is running every experiment in this book at a higher stake than any of its cases did. That is not a reason to skip the experiments. It is a reason to run them smaller and write the stopping rule first.

Monday

  1. Start the record, in whatever you will still be keeping in March. One line per sale for a fortnight: date, what, price, paid how, known face or new, seen again. A notes file is enough. A blank cell is a thing you did not write down, not a zero.
  2. Write the floor in units. How many of the thing you sell, at this week's cost, cover this week's rent, the supplier and the minimum you take home. Rebuild it the day the supplier's price moves, and keep the old number beside the new one.
  3. Write the three you turn away as menu, price and hours. Not customers you cannot serve — ones you have decided against. Next to each, what the refusal is supposed to buy. If nothing, it is a gap, not a position.
  4. Message the last five who paid once and were not seen again. One question, past tense, no offer: what stopped you. Their answers, in their words, go in the record. Two attempts across a fortnight and no reply goes in the could-not-reach line, and stays there.
  5. Three numbers, same evening each week. Cash after what you owe and what it will still buy at restock; faces seen before, against the same week last year; things sold, not things made. Six weeks before you read a direction. One week if the cash for the supplier is not there.

The case this chapter does not have

Not a counter-case — a case. It would be a walk-in or phone-and-cash business whose owner kept the record above for a year and published it: one line per sale, the floor in units and how often it moved, the could-not-reach count, the three weekly numbers, and what the owner did when they moved. Nothing in it would need to be large. It would need to be kept while the outcome was unknown and released whether or not the business survived, which is the standard every other case in this book was held to and the reason there is none here. A café ledger for twelve months, honestly kept, would be the most useful document in Part III. If one exists, it was not found. If you keep one, it is the case, and the method page says where to send it.

Reading

  • Note · This is the second chapter anchored on something other than a named company, and the only one anchored on nothing checkable at all. The departure is deliberate: the trade it is about is defined by not publishing, and inventing a case for it would break the standard the rest of the book is held to. No figure in this chapter is a claim about the world, and none is registered.
