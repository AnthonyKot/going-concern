Part III · Optional worked example · Stock and cash

The Business That Does Not Invoice

This example is for businesses that buy stock before collecting payment. It is optional; the main financial path continues from the cash forecast to customer value.

Saturday's takings are in the till. Some belong to the stock you have already bought, some must fund the next order, and some may be available to take home. Before you buy again, separate those amounts. You can do it with a notebook.

The decision How much stock can you buy before the next selling day, while meeting the payments that fall due first — and what does the last selling day actually tell you to buy?

Move 1 Start with what changed hands

You sell across a counter, at a market, or through messages. Customers usually pay when they collect. There may be no invoice waiting thirty days for payment, but there is still a gap: the supplier wants money before you know how much will sell. Chapter thirteen's timing question survives. The money gets stuck in goods rather than in an unpaid customer bill.

The title describes that selling pattern, not an exemption from keeping records or issuing documents where required. Nor does a sale need a signed document to create obligations. This interlude is about managing the trade; it does not decide which records your jurisdiction requires. A notebook can help you make a buying decision without being your complete accounts.

The previous chapters followed Lea's consultancy. Here the useful unit is a selling session and the stock left after it. You need the same distinctions: what customers bought, what it cost to supply, what money actually arrived, what remains to be done, and what falls due next. You do not need to reproduce a consultant's customer sheet for every anonymous walk-in sale.

Start with one product line if reconstructing the whole shop would prevent you starting. Count what you had before the session, what arrived, what sold, and what remains. Record damaged goods, gifts and items taken for your own use separately. A missing item is a discrepancy to investigate, not automatically another sale. Keep the receipts or other evidence beside the totals so that you can explain a difference later.

Then reconcile the money. Cash in the till and payments confirmed in your own account are different from transfers somebody says they sent. Card takings awaiting settlement need their own expected receipt date. Record payment charges rather than silently treating the net bank deposit as the full sale price. Where money and goods move on different days, record both. A deposit received is also a promise you still have to fulfil.

This record has a purpose beyond tidiness. When the supplier asks for the next order, you want to distinguish three answers: the goods earned a useful margin; the money is available in time; and there is enough evidence to risk buying this quantity. A good answer to one does not supply the other two.

Move 2 One Saturday, three different totals

This is an invented example, not a reported business case. Mira sells one kind of bag at a weekly stall. Every figure below is hypothetical. To keep the arithmetic visible, the model excludes VAT and other taxes throughout, including its cash figures. It has no loans, unpaid bills, customer deposits or payment charges. Real records must include those items where they apply. This is a management illustration, not a tax-account template.

Before buying this week's stock, Mira has €500 business cash and no bags. She buys 20 bags at €12 each, paying €240. The stall and transport cost another €60, paid before opening. Each bag sells for €25. On Saturday she sells 14, hands them over and receives all €350. The six remaining bags are undamaged and available for another session. She then takes €100 out of the business for household spending.

Before reading the answers, write down three numbers: Saturday's profit before owner labour and tax, closing cash, and the cost of the stock still held. They should not all be the same. If you subtract the entire purchase from sales when finding profit, ask where the six remaining bags have gone.

The business made €122 in this simplified profit view, yet cash fell by €50. The bridge is €122 profit, less €72 now held in stock, less €100 drawings: a €50 cash decrease. The €100 is money taken out by the owner, not another cost of bags sold. The €72 is stock at purchase cost, not a promise of €150 in future receipts. Those bags might sell, need a discount, be damaged, or remain on the shelf.

Mira spent eight hours buying, travelling, selling and keeping the record. At an invented allowance of €15 an hour, that is €120 of owner time. Subtract it from the €122 once to get a €2 economic remainder. This allowance is not a wage payment in the model and does not leave the till. It asks a separate question: whether the trade rewards the work at the rate Mira chose. Neither €122 profit nor €100 drawings answers that by itself.

The next order changes the answer

The supplier now quotes €15 per bag. Mira wants another 20 and must pay before next Saturday. Another €60 stall-and-transport payment falls due before opening. She chooses a €120 minimum business cash balance for this exercise. That is a planning choice, not a universal reserve. There are no other cash movements before the next session in this model.

Twenty bags would cost €300. Starting with €450, paying €300 and then €60 leaves €90 before the first customer arrives: €30 below the chosen minimum. Expected Saturday sales cannot fix a shortage that occurs before opening. An order of 18 costs €270 and leaves exactly €120; an order of 14 costs €210 and leaves €180. The first calculation sets a maximum under these assumptions. It does not tell Mira that buying 18 is wise.

She already has six bags. Buying 14 brings available stock to 20; buying 18 brings it to 24. Last Saturday's 14 sales do not establish demand for either quantity next week. Suppose Mira chooses to buy 14 and keep the extra €60 above her minimum, reviewing after the next session. That is a proposed decision in an illustration. Its cost is the possibility of running out and losing sales; the larger order's cost is more money exposed to unsold stock. Neither option removes uncertainty.

At the unchanged €25 selling price, a newly bought bag now leaves €10 before session costs and owner time, rather than €13. Covering €60 session costs and the same €120 time allowance would require 18 sales at that new margin. This is a replacement-cost planning calculation, assuming each sale uses a €15 bag. It is not next week's accounting profit: six bags were bought at the old cost. Keep the old purchase record and the new quote.

That comparison exposes a difficult joint. Selling 14 new-cost bags would leave €140 before session costs, or €80 after them, below the €120 time allowance. Buying enough to pursue 18 sales does not make those sales happen. Mira needs to test a different price, reduce a cost or time commitment, accept a lower return deliberately, or change the offer. More stock alone does not repair the margin.

Move 3 Buy from the record, then test the assumptions

The example has one product so that the decision can be followed. In your record, separate lines whose costs, prices or selling patterns differ enough to change the order. Twenty items sold tells you little about what to replenish if the popular sizes sold out and the rest never moved. Write down requests you could not fill as well as completed sales. A stockout hides demand; a quiet afternoon does not explain its own cause.

Compare sessions with their conditions attached: opening hours, location, availability, prices and any unusual event. Ask customers what they wanted when the answer would change a buying decision. Their answers suggest a test; they are not paid orders. A message asking for a price, a reservation without payment, a deposit and a collected purchase belong in different categories. Do not turn the entire chat history into committed demand.

For a made-to-order trade, replace bags on hand with materials and unfinished orders. Match each deposit to the work, balance due and delivery promise it supports. Check whether the materials and hours can be supplied before accepting more. Money received early may improve the timing while creating an obligation that consumes it. The notebook should show what is owed to customers as clearly as what they owe you.

Keep benefit separate from a return visit

The customer review’s follow-up still asks whether the customer got what was promised. A repaired zip that works is a successful repair even if the customer never needs another. A second purchase is evidence of another purchase; it does not by itself tell you whether the first item fitted, lasted or arrived on time. Record a complaint or reported result against the relevant job where you can, and keep unknown outcomes unknown.

In an anonymous walk-in trade, you may be unable to link purchases or contact the buyer. Do not label every unfamiliar face a new customer or every absent face a failed outcome. Use the evidence you have: returns and their reasons, corrections, voluntary feedback, and repeat purchases you can actually identify. Explain the coverage. You can learn from five conversations without pretending they describe everyone who bought.

Move the review to the next commitment

Review before the next stock order or other payment you cannot easily undo. A weekly rhythm fits Mira's invented stall; a daily restock or a long supplier lead time needs a different schedule. There is no six-week waiting requirement. When a quote changes, update the affected margin and cash forecast before using them again. Keep the previous version so that the reason for changing the order remains visible.

For the cash forecast, start with money actually available and list payments in date order through the next receipt and beyond it to the obligations that receipt must cover. Keep the bank balance and cash in hand visible before subtracting future bills. Include household withdrawals you intend to make, and applicable tax payments, refunds and debts. If you earmark cash for an obligation, do not count both that reservation and its eventual payment as two expenses. Chapter thirteen shows the running-balance method.

Then change the assumption most capable of upsetting the order. If the next selling session is cancelled, Mira's smaller order still leaves €180 after the modelled payments, but brings no receipts. Another session would require another forecast, including whether another fee is due. If some stock spoils, reduce the saleable count and record the loss; do not carry the old value forward because the supplier was already paid.

The record also gives stopping a concrete meaning. Set the next review date and name the result that would make you reduce the order, change the line or stop that session. Units alone are insufficient when the margin changes: 18 sales at one price and cost are a different result from 18 at another. State the cash and return you need alongside the quantity. Choose a smaller test when the loss would threaten money needed elsewhere.

Waiting has a cost too. Ordering nothing protects cash but can leave regular customers unserved and stock unavailable when the next opportunity arrives. Put that consequence beside the buying risk. Your task is to choose an exposure you can explain from the current record, not to collect enough records to make uncertainty disappear.

Monday

  1. Reconstruct one completed session. Use the blank session review or a notebook. Count stock, reconcile receipts and payments, and separate drawings from operating costs. Mark missing evidence. Trace any unexplained difference before relying on the total.
  2. Price the next order. Write the current supplier quote, stock already available and payments due before the next receipts. Calculate the lowest cash balance for your proposed quantity and for a smaller order. Show what each leaves available to sell.
  3. Choose and date the test. Record the order, why its risk is acceptable, and the assumption that would change it. Test a missed selling session or another relevant setback. Review before committing again; bring the result into chapter fourteen's customer-value decision.

You have finished when you can explain the next order from stock, margin and dated cash, including what remains unknown. A filled sheet with an unexplained balance is unfinished.

The case still owed

Mira is arithmetic, not evidence that this practice improves a business. This chapter still owes a documented walk-in or message-based trade showing its records, buying decisions and later outcomes, including decisions that went badly. The absence is a limitation of this book's research, not proof that such businesses publish nothing. The worksheet and review rhythm are proposals to test, not a method validated by a successful invented seller.
