What to Charge

In the spring of 2011 Studio Neat sold every one of the Cosmonaut's first three thousand slots in two days, and was still about $5,000 short of the money needed to make it. Demand had answered. Price had not. What will you charge the next buyer, and how will you know whether it was right?

The decision What you will charge the next buyer, what that price includes, and what you need to learn from quoting it.

The Cosmonaut: sold out, and short

Studio Neat — the Cosmonaut, 2011

In March 2011, six months after the Glif campaign in chapter two, Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost returned to Kickstarter with the Cosmonaut, a thick rubber stylus shaped more like a dry-erase marker than a pen. They set a $50,000 goal to manufacture it.

Instead of setting ordinary reward prices, they offered the first 3,000 on a pay-what-you-want basis. The campaign was all-or-nothing: below $50,000, nobody would be charged and nobody would get a stylus. Fifty thousand dollars over three thousand units is $16.67 each, this book's division, not theirs. They let the buyers choose the number.

All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours, and the campaign was still about $5,000 short. Every available order had been taken, and the total could not fund the work. They changed the offer and opened new, unlimited reward tiers. By 20 April, 6,192 backers had pledged $134,236. The Cosmonaut shipped in December, after an original June estimate and several manufacturing delays.

And $134,236 was not profit. In their later account, Kickstarter took 5% and payment processing about 3%, and they allowed for about 5% of backers whose payments fail. Their fulfilment provider cost around $2 an item before packing, postage and storage. Manufacturing still had to be paid for, and six thousand people were waiting through the delays.

A price has to pass two tests that do not calculate each other: the sale must be worth it to the buyer, and delivering it must be worth it to you. The Cosmonaut passed the first three thousand times and failed the second. You need a first quote before you know enough to be comfortable, because the information that would make you comfortable is produced by quoting. So build it from three things, in order.

A floor, an alternative, and one proposal between them

The floor. Start with the unit you actually sell: one pen delivered, one boiler serviced, one month of support, one defined piece of consultancy. Add every cost one more sale causes: materials, payment fees, delivery, returns, subcontractors, support, and the labour to fulfil the promise, including yours, at what it would cost to replace it competently. Mark the estimates you have not tested. This does not cover the whole business; selling time and administration come later, in Part III. It tells you which accepted prices are sales you cannot afford to make. Cost tells you when to refuse a sale, not what creates one.

The buyer's alternative. Write down what this buyer does without you: loses a Saturday doing it badly, hires a larger firm, keeps an employee on it, lives with the risk. That places your price inside their real decision. It is evidence for a proposal, not a claim on all the value you can imagine: saving a firm €100,000 does not entitle you to €10,000 because ten per cent fits on a slide. If you are arriving from a salary, note that your salary tells you what your time cost an employer, and almost nothing about what a small client will pay for a report, an installation or a decision.

One proposal between them. A price for a defined piece of work, stating what is included, what ends the work, the payment terms and what would need a new quote. If the scope is genuinely uncertain, quote a paid diagnostic or a first phase, or give a range and say what decides the final charge. Where the customer is deliberately buying uncertain effort, as in investigation or emergency repair, hours are the honest unit: give a rate, an estimate, a spending limit and the point at which you will ask permission to continue.

Lea, the invented developer from chapter one, does this for her report job. Her floor: €50 of tools and eight hours of delivery at the €50 an hour she would pay someone competent, so €450. That is her estimated delivery cost, on the assumption that the job takes eight hours. It is not a sustainable price: it leaves nothing for the hours spent winning the job, for software and bookkeeping she pays whatever she sells, or for the jobs that overrun. The alternative, for one prospect: an analyst who spends about two hours every Monday rebuilding the same client report. Her proposal: €900 to automate one weekly report: one input file, one output, a handover and one round of corrections; changes to your source systems and ongoing maintenance are not included. €900 sits well above her floor and well below a year of that analyst's Mondays. Whether it is enough depends on whether the eight hours hold and what winning each job costs, and only the jobs themselves will say. Chapter ten picks her up after three people have paid it.

Learn from the replies without chasing each one

For a repeatable offer, quote the same unit at the same price to about ten comparable buyers before changing it. Ten is a manageable first batch, not a reliable sample. Record the price, the response, the stated reason and the work you actually had to do; chapter six's sales record will do. Set a date to read it even if you have not reached ten.

When someone says expensive, ask what they mean: a cheaper offer, no budget, someone else's approval, doubt that it will help. If they will not say, record the reason as unknown. Before discounting, try a smaller scope, a later date or payment in advance, and write the change beside the quote, so a sale of less work does not look like proof of the original price. A quick yes deserves the same caution: you may have undercharged, or the buyer may have a costly problem and trust you to solve it. Where input costs move quickly, recalculate the floor before quoting again; a tidy experiment is no reason to keep selling at a loss.

On Dutch ground, a price advertised to consumers must include VAT, so the floor is compared with the price net of it; the section at the end of this chapter gives the rule and the arithmetic.

Monday · one written quote

Do this one thing. Write the quote for your next buyer, for one defined unit: what is included, what ends the work, the price and the payment terms. Under it, write the floor in one line: every cost this one sale causes, your own hours at replacement cost included, with VAT removed if the price is for consumers. You now hold a quote you could send and the number below which you should not.

Missing a cost? Put in your best estimate and mark it untested. If being wrong about it would change the price or the scope, do not send the quote yet: asking the supplier, the accountant or whoever knows for the real figure, with a date for the answer, is Monday's action and counts as finishing. Only a cost too small to move the price can go out as an estimate.

If you have another hour:

  1. Find the buyer's alternative. Ask one buyer what they do today without you and what it costs them. Keep what they said apart from what you assumed.
  2. Start the record. A line per quote: price, response, reason (or unknown), the work it took. Set the date you will read it, even if ten have not come in.
  3. Check the unit. If the work is genuinely uncertain, rewrite the quote as a rate, an estimate, a spending limit and the point at which you will ask to continue.
What this chapter rests on, and what would prove it wrong

The Cosmonaut figures come from Studio Neat's own long retrospective, checked against a 2015 archive copy. The final total cannot tell anyone which price worked: new tiers, more backers, press, time and the deadline all arrived together. The first pledges were not ordinary purchases either; backers could see the shared goal and risked getting nothing, so their average is not a shop price. The campaign establishes only that taking every available order can still leave a project unable to fund the work. The quoting routine is this book's proposal; the campaign does not test it. Lea's floor, alternative and quote are invented.

Still owed: a documented small business that held a first price steady through a batch like this and was wrong to, because the first buyers were unusually price-sensitive or the offer lost credibility by being too cheap. None has been found. In your own record, find out why a batch failed before treating it as a verdict on the market; ten buyers help you organise evidence, they are not a representative sample. The debt stands.

On Dutch ground · VAT is inside the consumer price

If you sell to consumers in the Netherlands, the price you advertise is not your pre-tax target with VAT added later. ACM says a consumer offer must state the price in euros including VAT and include unavoidable additional costs. Where a cost depends on the order, such as postage, the offer must explain or exemplify how it is calculated.

The arithmetic changes the floor. At the standard 21% rate, a consumer price of €121 contains €21 of VAT and leaves €100 before your costs. A price of €99.95 contains €17.35 of VAT; the Belastingdienst calculates the included amount as €99.95 × 21/121. VAT collected is not sales income available to pay the maker or absorb a discount.

Do not infer from this section which rate, exemption or cross-border rule applies to your offer. Business-to-business presentation and invoicing also have their own requirements. Ask a Dutch accountant which regime applies, then keep two explicit numbers in the price record: what the customer sees and what remains excluding VAT.
