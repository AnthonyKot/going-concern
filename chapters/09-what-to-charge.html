Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 9

What to Charge

Studio Neat sold every one of the Cosmonaut's first three thousand slots and was still short of the number required to make it. Demand had answered. Price had not.

The decision What number you will put in front of the next buyer — one high enough to carry the promise, specific enough to test, and provisional enough to change when the evidence does.

Move 1 The situation

The last chapter ended with a person asking you the question you had been working to earn: how much? You now have to answer before you possess the information that would make the answer comfortable. There are too few customers to know demand at several price points, no mature cost history, and no trustworthy lifetime value. But refusing to name a price until those exist is circular. They are produced by selling, which requires naming a price.

A maker's first refuge is effort. You estimate twenty hours, multiply by an hourly rate, add the software licence and call the result a price. It feels defensible because every input belongs to you and can be put in a spreadsheet. A physical-product founder performs the same manoeuvre with material and freight. Add a percentage to cost, compare with two competitors, round to a pleasing nine.

Those calculations are necessary. None answers the buyer's question. The buyer is comparing your offer with doing nothing, doing it themselves, hiring somebody else, accepting the risk, or spending the money on an entirely different problem. Your hours do not appear in that comparison unless the buyer has specifically chosen to buy hours.

This is where pricing advice usually becomes theatrical. You are told to charge what the work is worth, as if value were a number waiting behind the sofa, or to raise prices until people complain, as if complaints paid invoices. Both slogans conceal the decision. A price has to pass two independent tests: the sale must be worthwhile for the buyer, and fulfilling it must be worthwhile for you. Neither test calculates the other.

So this chapter will not find the correct price. There is no such number available before the asks. It will build a price you can defend for the next ten of them, and a record that tells you what to change afterwards.

Move 2 What actually happened

In March 2011, six months after the Glif campaign that appeared in chapter three, designers Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost returned to Kickstarter with the Cosmonaut: a thick rubber stylus shaped more like a dry-erase marker than a pen. They set a $50,000 goal to manufacture it.

Instead of setting ordinary reward prices, they offered the first 3,000 Cosmonauts on a pay-what-you-want basis. The campaign was all-or-nothing. If the pledges did not reach $50,000, nobody would be charged and nobody would receive the product. That put the backers into a small collective-action problem: a cheap reservation helped use up the scarce supply while leaving somebody else to close the funding gap.

The arithmetic existed before the campaign opened. Fifty thousand dollars divided by three thousand units is $16.67 per unit. That division is this book's, not a number Studio Neat published. They let the buyers fill in the number that would determine whether the aggregate reached it.

All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours. The campaign was still about $5,000 short. If the roughly $45,000 pledged at that point all belonged to those slots, dividing it by 3,000 gives an average near $15. That is also this book's arithmetic, and the source does not let us verify the assumption behind it. The argument does not need the average: the supply was exhausted while the aggregate remained below the project's stated floor. Studio Neat had found demand and a pricing failure in the same result.

They did not preserve the experiment at the expense of the product. They opened additional, unlimited reward tiers. By 20 April, 6,192 backers had pledged $134,236. The Cosmonaut eventually shipped in December, after an original June estimate and several manufacturing delays.

The final total is the glamorous number and the least useful one for pricing. New tiers, additional backers, press, time and the Kickstarter deadline all arrived together. It would be fiction to assign the result to one price change. Nor does the record say that Studio Neat calculated a customer's economic value and charged a share of it. This is not a value-pricing case. It is a case in which the amount people freely chose failed the project's own requirement.

And $134,236 was not profit. In Studio Neat's later account of its five campaigns, Kickstarter took 5% and payment processing about 3%; they advised allowing for about 5% of backers whose payments fail. International shipping had been the large surprise on their first project. Their fulfilment provider cost around $2 an item before packing materials, postage and monthly storage. Manufacturing still had to be paid for, and the Cosmonaut's delays had to be managed for more than six thousand people.

This is cost of goods in its proper role. It does not tell you what a buyer will accept. It tells you which accepted prices are not sales you can afford to make.

Move 3 The reasoning

Build the floor before you negotiate above it

Start with the unit you actually sell. One pen delivered. One boiler serviced. One monthly account supported. One workshop for twelve people. One defined consultancy outcome. Then attach the costs caused by one more sale: materials, payment fees, delivery, returns, subcontractors, support and the labour required to fulfil the promise.

Labour belongs here even when it is yours. Not because the customer owes you for every hour, but because an hour spent on this customer cannot fulfil another order, find the next customer or be spent at home. Use the cost of replacing the work competently, not the comforting fiction that your own evenings are free. Add a modest allowance for the failures that arrive with volume. The result is a floor: below it, each sale consumes the business it appears to grow.

The floor is not the price. Studio Neat's $50,000 goal was an aggregate version of one. It could expose the pay-what-you-want result as insufficient; it could not make buyers pledge more. Cost tells you when to refuse a sale, not what creates the sale.

Name the alternative, not an abstract value

Next write down what this buyer does without you. For a local service, the alternative may be losing Saturday to doing it badly. For a professional practice, it may be a larger firm, an employee, a fine, a delay or living with uncertainty. For a product, it may be a cheaper object that works differently. These alternatives locate the price inside the buyer's real decision.

Do not turn that into a claim on all the value you can imagine. Saving a company €100,000 does not entitle you to €10,000 merely because ten per cent fits in a slide. You may not control the saving; the buyer may not believe it; procurement may compare day rates; the result may depend on their team. The alternative is evidence for a proposal, not a moral right to the buyer's upside.

This distinction matters particularly to people leaving salaried technical work. Your salary tells you the minimum economics of selling your time. It says almost nothing about what a small client will exchange for a report, installation or decision. Translate competence into a defined change for a defined buyer, then compare that change with the alternatives already costing them money, time or risk.

Put one proposal between the two

Now choose a number above the floor and present it. Not a range. A range merely announces that the buyer should choose the lower end while you reserve the right to wish for the upper. If scope is uncertain, price a paid diagnostic, a first phase, a day, or a clearly bounded quantity. The uncertainty should change the unit, not make the number disappear.

Keep that proposal stable long enough to learn. If every buyer receives a different price, every no can be blamed on the number and every yes celebrated as proof. Quote the same offer and price to the next ten comparable buyers. Record who bought, who did not, the alternative they named, and the work the buyers required after saying yes. Chapter eight's sales record now gains two columns: price quoted and reason.

A discount is a new offer, not an eraser. Reduce scope, change delivery time, require payment in advance, remove an option or ask for a larger commitment. Otherwise you learn only that a private lower price can rescue a conversation, while training yourself to distrust the public one. Equally, a quick yes is not proof that the price was too low. The customer may simply have a costly problem and trust you.

When hours are the honest unit

Pricing by effort is an error when effort is merely your private production method. It is not an error when the customer is deliberately buying uncertain access to that effort. Investigation, emergency repair, litigation and exploratory technical work can make fixed scope dishonest. An hourly or daily rate then assigns overrun risk to the buyer, who may also control the requests.

Say that plainly. Give a rate, an estimate, a spending limit and a point at which you will ask permission to continue. Fixed pricing transfers more delivery risk to you and should be priced with that risk present. Neither unit is more enlightened. The honest one is the unit whose uncertainty and responsibility match the actual work.

Boundaries

A crowdfunding pledge is not an ordinary retail price. Cosmonaut backers could see the common goal, communicate with one another, choose generosity, and lose the product if the group failed. The campaign also had scarcity, a deadline and press. Its average pledge is not a demand curve and should not be imported into a shop.

The floor will still be incomplete. Early estimates omit returns, sales time, warranties, rework, idle capacity and the founder's administration. That is why the number needs a review date and why Part III will calculate the business rather than the individual sale. The purpose now is to stop making sales whose visible direct costs already exceed them.

Price may not be why they said no. A buyer who says expensive may mean I do not trust you, this is not urgent, I cannot approve it, or I do not understand the offer. Do not lower the number until chapter eight's conversation has located the objection.

On Dutch ground · VAT is inside the consumer price

If you sell to consumers in the Netherlands, the price you advertise is not your pre-tax target with VAT added later. ACM says a consumer offer must state the price in euros including VAT and include unavoidable additional costs. Where a cost depends on the order, such as postage, the offer must explain or exemplify how it is calculated.

The arithmetic changes the floor. At the standard 21% rate, a consumer price of €121 contains €21 of VAT and leaves €100 before your costs. A price of €99.95 contains €17.35 of VAT; the Belastingdienst calculates the included amount as €99.95 × 21/121. VAT collected is not sales income available to pay the maker or absorb a discount.

Do not infer from this box which rate, exemption or cross-border rule applies to your offer. Business-to-business presentation and invoicing also have their own requirements. Ask a Dutch accountant which regime applies, then keep two explicit numbers in the price record: what the customer sees and what remains excluding VAT.

Monday

  1. Define one sellable unit. Not “consulting” or “a product”: one diagnostic, one installation, one month, one delivered object. Write what is included and what ends the work.
  2. Build its floor. List every cost caused by the next sale, including delivery, payment, failure and your labour at replacement cost. For a consumer price, remove VAT before comparing the price with that floor.
  3. Name three real alternatives. What does this specific buyer do instead, and what does each alternative cost in money, delay, effort or risk? Delete any value claim you cannot connect to one.
  4. Make a one-line price card. “For [defined unit], the price is [one number], including/excluding VAT, paid [when].” Put a review date on it. That card is the chapter's artifact.
  5. Use it for ten comparable asks. Record price, yes or no, stated reason, discount or scope change, and actual delivery burden. Do not optimise after one reaction. Review after ten.

The counter-case this chapter does not have

The missing case is a small business that calculated its floor honestly, located the offer against the buyer's real alternatives, held a clear price through enough comparable asks, and still failed because the price itself damaged the signal or the market. A useful version would publish both rejected quotes and delivery costs, not merely report that raising prices “worked.”

Studio Neat cannot fill that role. Its first price was chosen by buyers inside a promotional game, and the campaign succeeded after the format changed. It reveals the separation between demand and sufficiency; it does not establish that this chapter's three-part method finds a durable price.

Reading

The Cosmonaut dates, goal, total, backers, pay-what-you-want result, added tiers, fees, shipping surprise, fulfilment cost and delayed delivery come from this one long retrospective. Claims are checked against the 2015 archive snapshot, so later shop edits cannot change the record.
  • Arithmetic · Neither $16.67 nor the approximate $15 is a source figure. The first is this book's $50,000 ÷ 3,000. The second is a conditional illustration: roughly ($50,000 − $5,000) ÷ 3,000, valid only if every dollar pledged at that moment belonged to the initial slots. The source does not establish that condition, and the chapter's conclusion does not depend on it.
  • Official · Autoriteit Consument & Markt, “Prijzen vermelden” — current business guidance on displaying consumer prices including VAT and unavoidable costs.
  • Official · Belastingdienst, “Rekenvoorbeeld btw berekenen” — the official inclusive/exclusive VAT arithmetic used in the Dutch box.
  • Boundary · This chapter does not claim that Studio Neat used value-based pricing, that opening new tiers caused the final total, or that average pledge reveals willingness to pay. Those interpretations exceed the sources.
