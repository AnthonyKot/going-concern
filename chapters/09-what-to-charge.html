Part II · Will someone pay? · Chapter 9

What to Charge

Studio Neat sold every one of the Cosmonaut's first three thousand slots and was still short of the number required to make it. Demand had answered. Price had not.

The decision What you will charge the next buyer, what that price includes, and what you need to learn from quoting it.

Move 1 The situation

The last chapter ended with a person asking you the question you had been working to earn: how much? You now have to answer before you possess the information that would make the answer comfortable. There are too few customers to know demand at several price points, no mature cost history, and no trustworthy lifetime value. But refusing to name a price until those exist is circular. They are produced by selling, which requires naming a price.

A maker's first refuge is effort. You estimate twenty hours, multiply by an hourly rate, add the software licence and call the result a price. It feels defensible because every input belongs to you and can be put in a spreadsheet. A physical-product founder performs the same manoeuvre with material and freight. Add a percentage to cost, compare with two competitors, round to a pleasing nine.

Those calculations are necessary. None answers the buyer's question. The buyer is comparing your offer with doing nothing, doing it themselves, hiring somebody else, accepting the risk, or spending the money on an entirely different problem. Your hours do not appear in that comparison unless the buyer has specifically chosen to buy hours.

This is where pricing advice usually becomes theatrical. You are told to charge what the work is worth, as if value were a number waiting behind the sofa, or to raise prices until people complain, as if complaints paid invoices. Both slogans conceal the decision. A price has to pass two independent tests: the sale must be worthwhile for the buyer, and fulfilling it must be worthwhile for you. Neither test calculates the other.

You need a first quote before you have settled all of this. The useful question is how to choose it without pretending you already know what buyers will accept, and how to learn from the replies without changing the offer after every uncomfortable conversation.

Move 2 What actually happened

In March 2011, six months after the Glif campaign that appeared in chapter three, designers Tom Gerhardt and Dan Provost returned to Kickstarter with the Cosmonaut: a thick rubber stylus shaped more like a dry-erase marker than a pen. They set a $50,000 goal to manufacture it.

Instead of setting ordinary reward prices, they offered the first 3,000 Cosmonauts on a pay-what-you-want basis. The campaign was all-or-nothing. If the pledges did not reach $50,000, nobody would be charged and nobody would receive the product. That put the backers into a small collective-action problem: a cheap reservation helped use up the scarce supply while leaving somebody else to close the funding gap.

The arithmetic existed before the campaign opened. Fifty thousand dollars divided by three thousand units is $16.67 per unit. That division is this book's, not a number Studio Neat published. They let the buyers fill in the number, and staked the goal on what three thousand free choices would add up to.

All 3,000 slots were taken in the first forty-eight hours. The campaign was still about $5,000 short. The supply was exhausted while the total remained below the project's stated floor. Studio Neat had found demand and a pricing failure in the same result.

Sold out, still short, and all-or-nothing: they could hold the experiment pure and let the product stand or fall with it, or change the offer. They opened additional, unlimited reward tiers. By 20 April, 6,192 backers had pledged $134,236. The Cosmonaut eventually shipped in December, after an original June estimate and several manufacturing delays.

The final total cannot tell us which price worked. New tiers, additional backers, press, time and the Kickstarter deadline all arrived together. Nor were the first pledges ordinary purchases: backers could see the common goal and risked getting nothing if it was missed. Their average pledge cannot be read as a shop price. What the campaign does establish is that taking every available order can still leave a project unable to fund the work.

And $134,236 was not profit. In Studio Neat's later account of its five campaigns, Kickstarter took 5% and payment processing about 3%, and they had learned to allow for about 5% of backers whose payments fail. International shipping had been the large surprise on their first project. Their fulfilment provider cost around $2 an item before packing materials, postage and monthly storage. Manufacturing still had to be paid for, and more than six thousand people were now waiting through the Cosmonaut's delays.

This is cost of goods in its proper role. It does not tell you what a buyer will accept. It tells you which accepted prices are not sales you can afford to make.

Move 3 The reasoning

The quoting exercise below is my recommendation for a business with little sales history. Studio Neat supplies the reason to check costs before celebrating demand; its campaign does not test this exercise. You will still have to choose an opening price and decide which replies deserve to change it.

Build the floor before you negotiate above it

Start with the unit you actually sell. One pen delivered. One boiler serviced. One monthly account supported. One workshop for twelve people. One defined consultancy outcome. Then attach the costs caused by one more sale: materials, payment fees, delivery, returns, subcontractors, support and the labour required to fulfil the promise.

Labour belongs here even when it is yours. An hour spent on this customer cannot fulfil another order, find the next customer or be spent at home. Use the cost of replacing the work competently. Include an allowance for returns or rework where those are part of delivery, and mark the estimates you have not yet tested.

This gives you an initial floor for accepting the work. It does not cover the whole business: selling time, administration and idle capacity still need paying for. Part III returns to those costs. For now, the floor identifies an offer whose visible delivery costs already exceed its price. If you take that work to learn or enter a market, write down what you expect to gain and how much you can afford to spend on the attempt.

The floor is not the price. Studio Neat's $50,000 goal was an aggregate version of one. It could expose the pay-what-you-want result as insufficient; it could not make buyers pledge more. Cost tells you when to refuse a sale, not what creates the sale.

Name the alternative, not an abstract value

Next write down what this buyer does without you. For a local service, the alternative may be losing Saturday to doing it badly. For a professional practice, it may be a larger firm, an employee, a fine, a delay or living with uncertainty. For a product, it may be a cheaper object that works differently. These alternatives locate the price inside the buyer's real decision.

Do not turn that into a claim on all the value you can imagine. Saving a company €100,000 does not entitle you to €10,000 merely because ten per cent fits in a slide. You may not control the saving; the buyer may not believe it; procurement may compare day rates; the result may depend on their team. The alternative is evidence for a proposal, not a moral right to the buyer's upside.

This distinction matters particularly if you are arriving from salaried work. Your salary tells you the minimum economics of selling your time. It says almost nothing about what a small client will exchange for a report, installation or decision. Translate competence into a defined change for a defined buyer, then compare that change with the alternatives already costing them money, time or risk.

Put one proposal between the two

Choose a price for a defined piece of work and put it in front of the buyer. If you give a range because the scope is uncertain, explain what determines the final charge. You can also quote a paid diagnostic or a first phase, then price the rest once you know more. The buyer needs to understand what they are authorising and when another decision will be required.

For a repeatable offer, start with ten comparable quotes at the same price. Ten is a manageable first batch, not a reliable estimate of the whole market. Use the same unit and the same kind of buyer; a large bespoke project does not belong in the same batch as a small repair. Record the price, the response, the buyer's stated reason and the work you actually had to do. The sales record from chapter eight will serve if you already keep one.

Set a date to read the record even if you have not reached ten. If comparable enquiries are rare, a partly filled page may be all you have to work with. Check it for missing costs, repeated objections and differences between the customers who bought and those who did not. Where input costs change quickly, recalculate before quoting again; keeping the experiment tidy is no reason to keep offering work at a loss.

Ask what a buyer means by expensive. They may have a cheaper offer, lack the budget, need someone else's approval or doubt that the work will help. Sometimes they will not tell you. Record the reason as unknown in that case. A named competitor gives you something to compare; silence about competitors gives you no diagnosis at all.

Before discounting, ask whether a smaller scope, a different delivery date or payment in advance would make an offer you both prefer. Write the change beside the quote so that a sale of less work does not appear to validate the original price. If you cut the price for the same work, record that too. A quick acceptance deserves the same restraint: it may mean you undercharged, or that the buyer has a costly problem and trusts you to solve it.

When hours are the honest unit

Pricing by effort is an error when effort is merely your private production method. It is not an error when the customer is deliberately buying uncertain access to that effort. Investigation, emergency repair, litigation and exploratory technical work can make fixed scope dishonest. An hourly or daily rate then assigns overrun risk to the buyer, who may also control the requests.

Say that plainly. Give a rate, an estimate, a spending limit and a point at which you will ask permission to continue. Fixed pricing transfers more delivery risk to you and should be priced with that risk present. Neither unit is more enlightened. The honest one is the unit whose uncertainty and responsibility match the actual work.

Monday

  1. Define one sellable unit. Not “consulting” or “a product”: one diagnostic, one installation, one month, one delivered object. Write what is included and what ends the work.
  2. Build its floor. List every cost caused by the next sale, including delivery, payment, failure and your labour at replacement cost. For a consumer price, remove VAT before comparing the price with that floor.
  3. Identify the buyer's alternative. Ask what they would do without your offer and what that would cost them. Separate what they tell you from what you are assuming.
  4. Write the quote. State the work, price, payment terms and what would require a new quote. For a range, explain how the final charge will be determined.
  5. Start the record and book a review. Use ten comparable quotes as a first batch. Record the responses and delivery costs, leaving unknown reasons unknown. Set a review date even if enquiries are slow. At the review, choose one change you can explain from the record, or keep the offer while you gather more evidence.

What would prove this chapter wrong

Holding a price steady could itself be a mistake: the first buyers might be unusually price-sensitive, or the offer might lose credibility because it is too cheap. I have not found a documented small-business case that tests the proposed exercise and shows it failing. That leaves its usefulness unproved. In your own record, investigate why a batch was unsuccessful before treating it as a verdict on the market. A batch of ten helps you organise the evidence; it cannot make those ten buyers representative.

On Dutch ground · VAT is inside the consumer price

If you sell to consumers in the Netherlands, the price you advertise is not your pre-tax target with VAT added later. ACM says a consumer offer must state the price in euros including VAT and include unavoidable additional costs. Where a cost depends on the order, such as postage, the offer must explain or exemplify how it is calculated.

The arithmetic changes the floor. At the standard 21% rate, a consumer price of €121 contains €21 of VAT and leaves €100 before your costs. A price of €99.95 contains €17.35 of VAT; the Belastingdienst calculates the included amount as €99.95 × 21/121. VAT collected is not sales income available to pay the maker or absorb a discount.

Do not infer from this section which rate, exemption or cross-border rule applies to your offer. Business-to-business presentation and invoicing also have their own requirements. Ask a Dutch accountant which regime applies, then keep two explicit numbers in the price record: what the customer sees and what remains excluding VAT.
