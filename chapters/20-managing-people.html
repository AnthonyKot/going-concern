Part IV · Can it run without you? · Chapter 20

Managing People

Three months in, you know. Not that the person cannot do the work — they can — but that it is not going to work, and you have started arranging your week so that you do not have to say so. The judgment was the easy part. What this chapter is about is the gap between the judgment and the conversation, which is where the money goes.

The decision Where the date goes — the fixed point at which you must say yes, permanently or no, and here is how we part — and whether you set it when you hire, while you still can, or leave it to be set by the week you finally cannot stand it, when it will be later, costlier, and on Dutch ground a decision about a conversation the law has already shaped.

Move 1 The situation

The first employee from chapter nineteen has been in the business a while now, or the second one has, and something is off. The work gets done. The person is capable — you checked that before you offered the job, and you would say so in a reference. But the day is heavier with them in it than it should be. Things you explained are explained again. The customers who used to ask for you still ask for you. The rest of the team, if there is one, has quietly routed around them. You have noticed all of this, and what you have done about it is give yourself another month.

The month is not a plan. It is what an owner does when the decision has been made and the conversation has not, and it feels like fairness — they deserve more time — while actually being the opposite. The person is not being told what you already know. You are paying for work that is not what you need. And the part of your attention that should be on customers is on managing your own discomfort about a sentence you have not said.

So the question is not is this working. You know. It is whether you have a date by which you are required to act on what you know — one that was set before the doubt arrived, so that it is not you who decides that this is the week, but the calendar. That is a different thing to build than a management style.

Move 2 What actually happened

In January 2015, three years into Groove, a small helpdesk-software company that published its revenue as it went, Alex Turnbull wrote a post about the part of the job he says he has always had trouble with. The figure he starts with is his own: "But still, on a small team, a few employees is a lot. We've turned over nearly 40% of the team we launched with." By his own account, that is the cost side of three years of hiring, and the post is about the part of it he calls his own.

What he does not say is that they were bad hires. The opposite: "I can confidently say that every single person I've hired at Groove has been more than qualified to do the job they were hired for." The failure he owns is not one of skill. "My own hesitance to spot poor fits and wait too long" had cost, in his list: "Our team taking longer to hit milestones that we should have because we weren't collaborating as well as we could have. Our growth slowing because unhappy employees weren't working at optimal productivity. My own productivity dropping because I was preoccupied with trying to force a fit where there was none." Three costs, and the third is the one from Move 1: the owner's attention, spent on a decision already made.

What he built in response is a calendar, not a temperament. "We always — at every level — start with a 2-4 week paid trial run where the prospective employee joins the team on a temporary (and sometimes part-time, depending on their current work situation) basis." And: "a trial is not a guaranteed job, but a paid consulting gig to see if there's interest from both of us in moving forward." Which is to say, in chapter nineteen's terms, he buys a task before he offers a job — and he concedes the price of insisting on it: "This might lock us out of some very talented people who might be excellent fits for Groove. But it's a non-negotiable for us."

The trial's value is not the trial. It is that it ends on a date, and on that date something has to happen: "That's why we do the trial period: the end of the trial — where we have to either sign a permanent contract or part ways — acts as a forcing function that makes the difficult conversation inescapable." He does not claim the conversation got easier with practice. "‘The conversation’ doesn't get any easier. Not the third time, the fifth time or the tenth time." What changed was that it could no longer be avoided.

Inside the trial, he describes what he does now, starting with the thing he had got wrong. On feedback: "Early on, I used to wait until the trial period was over to give feedback. This helped nobody." Now he gives "direct and honest feedback often, even as often as daily during the trial period", and writes it down — "After two weeks, you won't remember every small thing you said to someone. But if you took notes, you'll be able to easily spot the trends in your feedback, and separate the true strengths and weaknesses from the anomalies." On whose judgment counts: "I trust my team's (who works much more closely with the new employee) judgment even more than my own." And on asking the person: "I've had several people, when I asked them how they felt about their trial, tell me that they didn't think they were as productive at Groove as they could be elsewhere, or that they simply didn't think there was a fit."

What transfers is the shape — a date set at the start, feedback during, other people's judgment before the decision — and the shape is what the rest of this chapter takes. What the trial ends is, in his words, "a paid consulting gig"; what ending an employment takes is a different question, and on Dutch ground it is the box's.

Move 3 The reasoning

The date goes in before the doubt

The expensive mistake in managing one or two people is not the wrong hire. It is the right judgment acted on late — and the remedy is a fixed date, agreed when the person starts, at which a decision has to be said out loud. Turnbull's costs are all costs of delay, and delay is what an owner produces when the only thing forcing the conversation is their own resolve. A date set in advance moves the decision off your willpower and onto the calendar, and it does something for the other person too: they know when they will be told, and that they will be.

The boundary matters as much as the rule. The date is a forcing function for a decision about fit; it is not a licence to end things quickly in general, and it is not a way to avoid chapter eighteen's finding — that a competent person will be slower than you for a while and that this gap is the transfer happening, not failing. A trial of two to four weeks judges whether the person and the work suit each other. It cannot judge whether they will be fast, and a business that ends trials for slowness will end all of them. And on Dutch ground the date is only partly yours: what a trial period can be, and what ending an employment after one takes, are in the box below.

Feedback during, in writing, from more than one person

Waiting for the end of the trial to say what you think "helped nobody", and the reason is structural: a person who hears nothing for four weeks and then hears no has been denied the one thing the trial existed to give them, a chance to change. Say it as it happens. Then write down what you said — not for them, for you, because after two weeks you will remember the last thing that went wrong and not the pattern, and Turnbull's distinction between a trend and an anomaly is the whole difference between a fair decision and a mood. Chapter eighteen already gave you the instrument: the handoff test, with its two numbers, is feedback in this sense, and the person's corrections written into the document are theirs.

The judgment should not be only yours. The person who works next to them sees more of them than you do, and Turnbull's "even more than my own" is not modesty; the owner sees the new hire least. And ask the person. Several of his told him it was not working before he did, which turned an ending into an agreement. That will not always happen, and the responsibility stays with you. But a decision the person has been asked about is a different event from one announced to them.

Fit — cultural, chemistry, Turnbull's own word for it — is a real thing and a dangerous one, because it is the word under which an owner's discomfort with someone unlike them gets recorded as a finding. The defence is the one this book gives every judgment: if you cannot write down what is not working in terms of the work — what was asked, what was done, what it cost — you do not yet have a fit problem. You have a feeling, and a feeling is not a finding in your notes, and it is not the ground the box quotes.

On Dutch ground · ending an employment

If you are not employing in the Netherlands, skip to Monday. If you are, read this before the conversation, not after it, because the date this chapter tells you to set is a date for a decision, not for a dismissal, and here the two are different acts.

There is no dismissal at will. Article 7:671 of the Civil Code: "De werkgever kan de arbeidsovereenkomst niet rechtsgeldig opzeggen zonder schriftelijke instemming van de werknemer, tenzij" — and the exceptions that follow include termination during the trial period and permission "als bedoeld in artikel 671a", which that article confines to grounds a and b of article 669: "op grond van artikel 669, lid 3, onderdeel a of b, verzoekt hiervoor schriftelijk toestemming aan het Uitvoeringsinstituut werknemersverzekeringen". For the ground this chapter is about, the door is the court, article 7:671b: "De kantonrechter kan op verzoek van de werkgever de arbeidsovereenkomst ontbinden: a. op grond van artikel 669, lid 3, onderdelen c tot en met i". Outside those, the employee's written agreement is what article 671 names. Article 7:669 sets what the employer must have first: "De werkgever kan de arbeidsovereenkomst opzeggen indien daar een redelijke grond voor is en herplaatsing van de werknemer binnen een redelijke termijn, al dan niet met behulp van scholing, in een andere passende functie niet mogelijk is of niet in de rede ligt." A reasonable ground, and no reasonable redeployment.

Unsuitability is a ground with conditions. Among the grounds, lid 3 onderdeel d covers the case in this chapter — unsuitability for the agreed work, other than through illness — "mits de werkgever de werknemer hiervan tijdig in kennis heeft gesteld en hem in voldoende mate in de gelegenheid heeft gesteld zijn functioneren te verbeteren en de ongeschiktheid niet het gevolg is van onvoldoende zorg van de werkgever voor scholing van de werknemer of voor de arbeidsomstandigheden van de werknemer". Told in good time, given a real chance to improve, and not left untrained. Feedback given as it arises, and a record that it was given, are here close to a precondition of the ground existing; notes kept only for yourself are not notice to the person.

The trial period is statutory and short. Article 7:652: "De proeftijd wordt schriftelijk overeengekomen"; on an indefinite contract "een proeftijd worden overeengekomen van ten hoogste twee maanden"; and none at all where the contract "is aangegaan voor ten hoogste zes maanden". Groove's two-to-four-week paid trial is not this — it is a contract for a task before any job exists, and chapter nineteen's box applies to it.

Notice and severance. Article 7:672 lid 2: "De door de werkgever in acht te nemen termijn van opzegging bedraagt bij een arbeidsovereenkomst die op de dag van opzegging: a. korter dan vijf jaar heeft geduurd: één maand", rising with service. Article 7:673 lid 2: "De transitievergoeding is voor elk jaar dat de arbeidsovereenkomst heeft geduurd gelijk aan een derde van het loon per maand", pro rata for part-years, and "bedraagt maximaal € 102.000,00 of een bedrag gelijk aan ten hoogste het loon over twaalf maanden indien dat loon hoger is dan dat bedrag". The cap "wordt telkens met ingang van 1 januari" adjusted, so in a later year the figure above is stale by construction. The transitievergoeding is owed on a dismissal the employer initiates on a valid ground; it is not a fine for doing it wrong, and paying it does not supply the ground.

None of this is advice. The box covers an employer ending an indefinite contract; a fixed-term contract's expiry, a termination by agreement, illness, and every other ground in article 669 are outside it. Before the first conversation that could be heard as a dismissal, pay an arbeidsrechtadvocaat for an hour with the contract and your notes in front of them.

Monday

  1. Write the sentence you are avoiding, in terms of the work. What was asked, what was done, what it cost. If it will not go into those words, you have a feeling, not a finding — and the next four items wait until it does.
  2. Put the date in. For anyone you hire from now on, the decision date goes in the offer, and the offer says what will be decided on it. For the person you already have, choose a date four weeks out, tell them today what will be decided then, and on Dutch ground call the lawyer before you say it.
  3. Give one piece of direct feedback this week, the day it arises, and write one line to yourself each day you gave any. At the date, read the lines before you decide; act on the trend, not the last one.
  4. Ask two people before the date: the one who works closest to them, and them. The question is the same for both — how is it going, and what would you change? — and the answers go in the notes beside yours.
  5. On the date, say one of two sentences. Yes, and here is what permanent looks like, or no, and here is how we part. A third sentence — let's give it another month — is only allowed if you write down what will be different about the month, and set the next date before the conversation ends. On Dutch ground, the second sentence is the one you have already put in front of the lawyer, in the words they gave you.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is an owner who did this properly — set the date at the start, gave feedback as it arose, asked the team and the person, said no on the date — and was wrong: the person who would have come good in the fourth month, and did, somewhere else, at a business that had not built the forcing function. Turnbull's own account offers only the other side — people kept too long — and that is the natural shape of the evidence, since an owner who ended a trial on schedule and later saw the person flourish elsewhere is describing a judgment they got wrong, and does not write that up either. The asymmetry to hold in mind is the one his list makes: the cost of ending on the date is one good person lost, visible, and recoverable by hiring again; the cost of not having a date is three kinds of slow, invisible until you count them, and by his account paid for three years.
