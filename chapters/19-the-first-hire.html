The First Hire

You have been buying hours for a while — a freelancer, a contractor, someone on a platform — and now you are about to give one person the same hours every week. The question you think you are asking is whether you can afford it. Two questions come before that one, and the second of them is answered by the state whether or not you ask it.

The decision Whether the work you are about to hand over is a task or a job — and therefore whether the person is a contractor or an employee — decided before the budget, because the first employee converts a cost you could stop into one you cannot, and because the law reads the arrangement from what actually happens, not from what the two of you called it.

Move 1 The situation

Something on the ranked list from chapter seventeen is recurring work — the same shape every week, no judgment in it, and yours only because nobody else is there. Until now you have handled that by buying it in pieces. A designer for the relaunch, a writer per article, a support person by the hour through a platform, an agency for the quarter. Each of those was a task: it had a scope, a price and an end, and when it ended so did your obligation.

Now the pieces have run together. The same person has been invoicing you every week for months, or the platform contractor has been on the shift so long that the customers know their name, or you have simply admitted that the work needs someone who is there on Monday whether or not you found anything for them to do. You have started to think about a salary, and the thought arrived as arithmetic: what does a person cost a month, and is there room for it.

Hold the arithmetic. The first question is whether this is a task or a job — whether you are buying an output or a person's regular time under your direction — because the two are different things to own and the difference is not a matter of what you call it. The second is what you owe once it is a job: not the wage, which you can look up, but the fact that it falls due every month from now on, including the months the revenue does not. Only after those two does the arithmetic mean anything, and by then it is a different sum.

Move 2 What actually happened

Eran Galperin has been in this book three times, and this chapter uses the one stretch of Gymdesk's history the earlier chapters had no use for: the two years in which a one-person business took on its first employee, told in a post he wrote in May 2023 — the same post chapter eighteen's source names as its predecessor. As before, the numbers are his own and are read here as a founder's account, not a filing.

Where he starts: Gymdesk "had reached $12k in MRR in the middle of 2019", enough for him to leave his job and work on it full time — chapter five's threshold, met. He spent the rest of that year and the start of 2020 on the product, relaunched with a new interface, and then the gyms that were his whole market closed. "We had significant churn from March to around August." The year still ended up — "having grown our MRR by 60% to around $19.5k" — but it ended with him doing every job, as he had for five years.

The first attempt to change that was not a hire. In 2021 he bought growth as a task, from people whose business was selling it: "After a few failed experiments engaging "growth" and advertising agencies, I came to the conclusion that outside consultants and agencies were never going to understand our audience like I did." The task did not fail because the agencies were bad at tasks. It failed because what he needed was not an output. It was someone inside the business, for as long as the business needed them.

So: "We made our first full time hire in June 2021" — a person who "used to write for us", with an SEO background, brought in-house as content marketer and editor to double down on the channel that already worked, organic search. Two things in that sentence matter more than the role. The person was already doing the work for him, as a freelancer, before the job existed. And he did not experience the change as a budget line. By his own account: "This was a big decision for me personally – it's different engaging freelancers for fixed term / cost work, than bringing on someone as a full time employee that you're now responsible to make sure they are paid every month. It was here that we started to make the transition from a one-man, owner run business to an actual company."

Later in 2021 came the second: "I hired my first full time customer service person as well." He had been buying that work by the hour for years — "We have engaged customer service people through Upwork previously as contractors, and it just wasn't working out." Note the order. The work that had been eating his own day — support, the thing chapter seventeen would have ranked first — was the second job he created, not the first, and it is the one he passes judgment on: "I still regret not doing this sooner."

Monthly revenue ended 2021 up "by around x2.5" on 2020. The business "passed $1M in ARR in mid 2022", and in 2022 kept hiring, "ending the year with 8 employees and 4 contractors." That last split is the quiet fact in the post. The task-or-job decision was not made once, at the first hire, and then settled. Eighteen months later a third of the people doing the work were still being bought as tasks, on purpose, next to the ones being paid as jobs. He does not say which work went which way. He does say what the jobs cost him that the tasks had not: "The more people I hire, the more work I need to do every day getting them “unstuck” when they encounter things outside of their comfort zone." That sentence is where chapter eighteen begins; this one stops before it.

Move 3 The reasoning

A task ends. A job does not.

A task is an output you buy: it has a scope, a price and a finish, and your obligation finishes with it. A job is a person's regular time under your direction, and it has no finish until one of you ends it. The test is not the hours, the contract, or the invoice, all of which can be arranged to say what you would prefer. The test is what happens if the person is not there on Monday. If a deliverable is late, you bought a task. If the day does not run, you have a job, whatever the paperwork says — and you probably had one some months before you noticed.

Galperin's agencies were tasks, bought correctly, and they failed for a reason the task form cannot fix: the work needed context that only accumulates in someone who stays. That is the honest signal that a piece of work has become a job. Not that it takes many hours — a real task can take hundreds — but that the hours are worth more the longer the same person does them, so that buying them fresh each time throws away the part you were paying for.

The failure mode is the mirror of the one in chapter eighteen. There, documentation was the comfortable way not to hand work over; here, keeping it a task is the comfortable way not to take on a person. The platform contractor who has covered the same shift for a year is a job wearing a task's contract, and the arrangement has already given you most of a job's dependence without any of its commitment. Galperin's support hires ran that way for years, and "it just wasn't working out" is the whole of what he says about what it cost. The regret he records is not about the money. It is about the time.

Both forms are legitimate. The four contractors still at Gymdesk at the end of 2022 are not a mistake in the post and are not one here. What is not legitimate is not knowing which one you have — and this is where a business in the Netherlands stops choosing its own words, because the state has a test of its own, in the box below, and it does not consult the label.

What the first job actually costs

Chapter one asked you to keep every cost you could variable until demand had been proved. The first employee is the first cost that cannot be varied: it falls due monthly, in the months revenue is down as well as the months it is up, and it cannot be cancelled in an afternoon. Galperin names exactly that as the thing that made June 2021 a big decision — responsible "to make sure they are paid every month" — and he names it, not the wage, as the moment the business became a company. The wage is the small part of the cost. The obligation is the large part, and it is what you are actually deciding to take on.

Which changes the sum. The question is not whether this month's profit covers a salary. It is whether the revenue that repeats — chapter eleven's second test — covers it after acquisition and service, at the level you have now rather than the level you are hiring in order to reach. Gymdesk made its first hire with monthly revenue near twenty thousand dollars and eighteen months of it recurring through a shutdown of its whole market. A hire funded by the growth it is supposed to produce is a task priced as a job: if the growth does not come, you are left with the obligation and not the output.

Take the cost from your accountant, not from the wage. What an employee costs the business in a month is not the figure on the offer, and in every jurisdiction there are charges above it that vary by form, sector and year; this chapter gives no figure because any it gave would be wrong for someone. Ask for the loaded monthly cost, then check it against the lowest month in chapter thirteen's weekly cash figure — the lowest, because that is the month the obligation will still fall due.

Convert before you recruit

The first person Galperin employed was someone whose work he had already been buying as a task. That is not incidental. A freelancer who has done the work for you for months has been on trial the whole time — you know the output, the speed, and what it is like to have them in the business, and none of that came from an interview. Where the job is growing out of work you already buy, offer it first to the person you already buy it from. Where it is not — where there is nobody whose tasks you could convert — you are recruiting a stranger for a job that has never been done, and the trial period, the expectations and the difficult conversation that follow belong to chapter twenty, which starts where this one ends.

One thing this case cannot tell you is where to hire first. Galperin put the first job on the growth channel and the second on his own bottleneck, and regrets the order; a single account of one sequence, judged by the person who chose it, does not establish that the reverse order is right — only that the support job existed for years before anyone was paid to hold it. Chapter seventeen's ranking is still the rule this book gives; this case is consistent with it and does not prove it.

On Dutch ground · who counts as an employee

If you are not hiring in the Netherlands, skip to Monday. If you are, the task-or-job question above has a statutory version, and it does not wait for your answer.

The definition. Article 7:610 of the Civil Code says what an employment contract is: "De arbeidsovereenkomst is de overeenkomst waarbij de ene partij, de werknemer, zich verbindt in dienst van de andere partij, de werkgever, tegen loon gedurende zekere tijd arbeid te verrichten." Three elements — work performed in dienst van the other party, for pay, over a period of time. The statute defines the relationship by those elements, not by the heading on the contract.

The presumption. Article 7:610a goes further, and this is the part to read twice: "Hij die ten behoeve van een ander tegen beloning door die ander gedurende drie opeenvolgende maanden, wekelijks dan wel gedurende ten minste twintig uren per maand arbeid verricht, wordt vermoed deze arbeid te verrichten krachtens arbeidsovereenkomst." Someone who works for you, for pay, for three consecutive months, weekly or at least twenty hours a month, is presumed to be doing so under an employment contract. A presumption is not a reclassification — it can be rebutted — but it moves the burden: past that threshold, whoever wants the arrangement to be something other than employment is the one who has to show it. The platform contractor on the same shift for a year is squarely inside it.

Enforcement. The government's own page on schijnzelfstandigheid — self-employment in name only — states that "De Belastingdienst controleert sinds 1 januari 2025 weer volledig op schijnzelfstandigheid." The consequence for the business that got it wrong is that it "betaalt de werkgever alsnog loonbelasting, premies werknemersverzekeringen, bijdragen Zorgverzekeringswet en soms pensioenpremies", and the same page notes that responsibility is shared: "Bedrijven en zzp'ers zijn samen verantwoordelijk om schijnzelfstandigheid te voorkomen."

None of this is advice. This box quotes the definition, the presumption and the fact of enforcement; it does not say how the assessment goes in your case, what the criteria weigh, or what a rebuttal would need. Before the first contract of either kind, pay an arbeidsrechtadvocaat for an hour with the actual working arrangement in front of them, and ask your accountant what the loaded cost of the employee form is before you compare it with the contractor's invoice.

The statute and the enforcement statement are quoted, not paraphrased; the administrative assessment framework the Belastingdienst applies is not quoted here and is what the lawyer is for.

Monday

  1. Take the top recurring item from chapter seventeen's list and write one sentence: what is missing on Monday if the person doing it is not there? A late deliverable means it is a task — keep buying it as one. A day that does not run means it is a job, and the rest of this list applies.
  2. Count the hours you have bought from the same person over the last three months. Weekly, or twenty-plus a month, for all three: you have already made the decision in practice, and if you are in the Netherlands the presumption in the box has too. Decide it on paper this week or accept that it has been decided for you.
  3. Ask your accountant for the loaded monthly cost of one employee at the wage you have in mind — the figure with every employer's charge in it, not the offer. Set it against the lowest month of the last twelve in your weekly cash figure. If the low month does not cover it, the hire is funded by revenue you have not yet had.
  4. Offer the job first to the person whose task work you already buy. If they exist, the trial has already happened. If nobody does, buy one task from a candidate before you offer them a job, and read chapter twenty before the offer.
  5. Write the date and the number by which you would know it was wrong. Chapter four's rule, applied to a person: what the role must have produced, by when, and what you do if it has not — with the answer being something short of ending it, because ending an employment is a different act with its own cost, and it is chapter twenty's.

What would prove this chapter wrong

What is owed is an owner who did this properly — identified a job rather than a task, funded it from revenue that already repeated, converted someone whose work they had already bought — and the business was worse for it: the fixed obligation that could not be shed in the bad quarter, the converted freelancer who was excellent on tasks and could not hold a job, the hire that turned an owner into a manager of one and slowed both. Galperin's account is the inverse: his regret is that the job was created too late, not that it was created. The case is hard to find for the reason this part keeps meeting — an owner who made a careful first hire and then let them go is describing a failure with a person in it, and does not write it up. Until it is found, note the asymmetry: this chapter's test is cheap to run and its errors are visible within a quarter, while the arrangement it argues against, a job kept on a task's contract, costs nothing on any given Monday and carries a liability the box above puts a date on.
