Being written in public, a chapter at a time. All twenty-eight are online; the last ten are first drafts that have been through one review and not yet through a reader. There is no mailing list and nothing for sale — the book simply appears here as it is written. If something in it is wrong, say so here. A book whose second chapter is about asking people questions should be reachable by the people reading it.

The sequence

A business does not present its problems in alphabetical order. It presents them in a sequence, and each one has to be survived before the next is even legible.

demand → payment → economics → operations → resilience

IIs there anything here?1–5
IIWill someone pay?6–10
IIIDoes the money work?11–16
IVCan it run without you?17–22
VWill it last?23–28

By the end you should be able to test an idea without overbuilding it, price it at something other than the cost of your own hours, read your own accounts well enough to see a cash problem three months out, hire someone without creating chaos, and decide on evidence rather than mood whether to grow, hold, sell, or stop.

Who it is for

This book is for you if:

  • you write software for a living and have done for fifteen or twenty years;
  • you are thinking about going out on your own — a consultancy, a small product, an agency, or buying something small that already works;
  • it will be self-funded, heading for somewhere between one and twenty people;
  • you will still be doing the work yourself for a long time;
  • and you have something to lose: a salary, a mortgage, possibly people who depend on you.

You arrive already fluent in systems, constraints, iteration and measurement, so nothing here explains what a feedback loop is. The gaps are somewhere else, and across a great many people they are the same gaps: building before proving demand, because building is the enjoyable part; reading interest as purchasing intent; avoiding direct selling and calling the avoidance a marketing strategy; pricing by effort rather than by what the result is worth; conflating revenue, profit and cash; automating a process before establishing that it should exist; remaining the indispensable technical bottleneck, which feels like responsibility; and underestimating distribution, management, and the value of capacity you are not using.

The book is organised around those, not around a curriculum.

Not for the venture-track founder. That reader is comprehensively served elsewhere, and several chapters here would have to say the opposite things. Growth is not assumed to be good. Cash matters more than valuation, because there is no valuation.

How a chapter works

Four moves — the situation, what actually happened, the reasoning, Monday — each anchored on one real company, named and dated, with figures.

Then a counter-case: a second story in which the same advice was followed, correctly and in good faith, and the outcome was bad. Business advice is unusually conditional — delegate, raise your prices, pick a niche, sell before you build are all excellent and all capable of producing disasters — and a principle you cannot see failing is one you cannot use, because you have no way of telling whether you are inside its domain or outside it.

Those cases are hard to find, because businesses that took good advice and died do not write about it. So far none of the thirteen published chapters has one that meets the definition. That is a gap, and it is carried as one: the device may only be satisfiable in something like half the chapters, and the alternative — quietly promoting a near-miss whenever the slot is empty — is the failure this book is trying to avoid. Where a chapter lacks one it says so in its own text and states what the missing case would have to be. The method page keeps the running score.

Chapters open with a provocation and then spend their length complicating it. Where a sentence here sounds like a universal law, it is a compression, and the chapter it belongs to will say so. Chapter one is a complete worked example of the form, if you would rather see it than read about it.

Where the answer depends on jurisdiction — what you may do while still employed, invoicing, what happens when you dismiss someone — chapters carry a short On Dutch ground box, dated and sourced, kept separate from the general argument so that neither contaminates the other.

Why the order matters

The closest thing this subject has to a standard text is Josh Kaufman's The Personal MBA, and its instincts are right: name real companies, keep the sections short, argue rather than lecture. But it is a glossary — two hundred and fifty alphabetised concepts with no accumulation, so you can read the last page first and lose nothing.

You cannot usefully ask whether your business can run without you until you know whether the money works, and you cannot ask that until somebody has paid you. Answering the questions out of order is not merely inefficient. It is how competent people build machines that lose money faster.

That said, some readers will not read in order, and it is better to give them a route than to pretend otherwise. If you read four: one, for the question every other chapter is a version of; four, for the only apparatus in the book that decides anything; thirteen, for the arithmetic that kills growing businesses; and sixteen, for the number that is not in the accounts at all. Each will send you back to the chapters it stands on.

The twenty-eight

Part I · Chapters 1–5

Is there anything here?

The cheapest failure available, and the one most often skipped. Everything in this part is about spending small amounts of money and time to avoid spending large ones — including the largest, which is a career. One ladder runs through all five: observation → conversation → commitment → stopping rule → leaving employment, and chapter five closes with the six things you should be able to point at before going on.

  • 01The Only Question That Matters Demand is not a fact about the world; it is a relationship between what people want, what they will pay, and what it costs you to serve them. Webvan, 1996–2001 · Instacart: the advice says proceed
  • 02Talking to People Who Will Lie to You Nobody is lying on purpose. A conversation cannot measure the future, so ask about the past. Everpix, 2011–2013 · counter-case owed
  • 03Selling It Before It Exists A preorder is evidence and a debt in the same movement, and the debt scales with how well the evidence goes. Studio Neat, 2010– · Coolest Cooler: the demand test passed 265 times over
  • 04Deciding Not To A rule whose only consequence is demolition is a rule you will break. Cydoc, 2018–2025 · Gymdesk: a rule that failed because it was badly written
  • 05Leaving Well You do not need courage to resign, you need evidence — and a clear idea of which evidence. Bingo Card Creator, 2006–2010 · Gymdesk: the runway said stop
Part II · Chapters 6–10

Will someone pay?

Part I proved that one person wanted it once. This part is about whether that repeats: a defined buyer, a way to reach them, the act of asking, a price that survives contact, and ten customers who are not a fluke. The gap between interest and money is where most technically strong businesses quietly stop.

  • 07Where They Already Are Two kinds of channel: one accumulates into something you own, the other stops when the payment stops. Bingo Card Creator, 2008–2011 · Appointment Reminder: the same playbook in the same hands
  • 08Asking for Money Every closed tab is a no with no reason attached. Asking is the only instrument that returns the reason. ConvertKit, 2013–2015 · Two weeks in Thailand: the part that did not scale was him

Does the money work?

The most common way a small business dies is not failure to sell. It is growth the cash cannot support, or a sale that never repeats.

Part IV · Chapters 17–22

Can it run without you?

There are two honest answers, and only one of them is a problem. A practice built to give its owner good work and a good income is a legitimate thing to own. So is an asset that runs without them. The danger is not knowing which one you are building.

  • 17You Are the Bottleneck A workload is what consumes your time. A bottleneck is what cannot move without you — and five hours a week can be one. Bingo Card Creator, 2010 · Pinboard, 2020 · Five hours a week, and one job nobody else could do
  • 18Writing It Down A document either shrinks what only you can do, or it is comfort that leaves the bottleneck exactly where it was — and only a named handoff, with you out of the room, tells you which. Gymdesk / Galperin, 2023 · counter-case owed
  • 19The First Hire Task or job is decided before contractor or employee, and long before the budget — and the state reads the arrangement from what happens, not from what you called it. Gymdesk / Galperin, 2021–2022 · counter-case owed
  • 20Managing People The expensive mistake is not the wrong hire but the right judgment acted on late — so the date goes in before the doubt, and on Dutch ground the law puts in its own. Groove / Turnbull, 2015 · counter-case owed
  • 21Meetings That Aren't Theater The theatre is the meeting you already have — got a minute? — and the cure is not fewer meetings but an owner, a clock on every segment, and the same shape every day. Groove / Turnbull, 2016 · counter-case owed
  • 22Measuring the Right Three Things Movement, loss, concentration — three figures someone other than you can read every week — and, from the same dashboard, how each one bends the moment it becomes a target. ConvertKit / Barry, 2019 · counter-case owed
Part V · Chapters 23–28

Will it last?

Businesses rarely die of the thing they were watching. This part is about the capacity to absorb what you did not forecast — and the paperwork that decides how bad it gets.

  • 23Slack Reserve is capacity you have paid for and not sold — sized against a shock you can name, bought in a unit you can stop — and it is spent silently, by growth, by quality and by you. WP Curve / Norris, 2014 · counter-case owed
  • 24What You Signed An unwritten agreement has terms anyway — the law's defaults and the other side's memory — and they are read out on the day of the dispute. Four agreements, the order they get expensive, and when to buy the hour. No company case — statute only · case and counter-case owed
  • 25What Protects You Protection is what changes the outcome on the day the thing happens, not what changes how you feel before it — and at this scale the one kind that cannot be copied is being chosen again. Studio Neat / Glif, 2011–2015 · counter-case owed
  • 26When the Ground Moves A shock nobody caused reaches you through what your customers can no longer do — so read it as their event first, offer the pause before the cancellation, and sell to them as they now are. Gymdesk / Galperin, 2020–2021 · counter-case owed
  • 27Built to Leave A business is built to leave when a stranger can run it and read it. One founder left cleanly; what the stranger found in the books is the chapter — and sellability is worth having whether or not you ever sell. Black Hops / Norris, 2022–2023 · counter-case owed
  • 28Knowing When to Stop Exhausted options are a date, not a feeling — written before the last big move — and stopping is done in order: whole, then parts, then who is owed, then the filing. On Dutch ground, what winding up cleanly requires. Binpress / Galperin, 2010–2016 · counter-case owed

Figures quoted in this book are registered against the document they came from, and a script re-checks that register against the original archives, because a book that claims to work from filings should let you confirm it without trusting the author. See the method page for how that works and what it has already caught, or go straight to the source documents, the claim register and the verification script on GitHub.

What that script does not check. It proves a quoted figure appears in the document it is credited to. It cannot tell whether the case actually supports the lesson the chapter draws from it, and that has been wrong more than once — a causal arrow pointing backwards, a rule stated before its qualifier, a category label claiming more than the evidence. Every one was found by reading rather than by the register, and every one is written up on the method page rather than quietly repaired.
