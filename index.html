The sequence

A business does not present its problems in alphabetical order. It presents them in a sequence, and each one has to be survived before the next is even legible.

demand → payment → economics → operations → resilience

IIs there anything here?1–5
IIWill someone pay?6–10
IIIDoes the money work?11–16
IVCan it run without you?17, 19–21
VWill it last?23–24, 26–28

By the end you should be able to test an idea without overbuilding it, price it at something other than the cost of your own hours, read your own accounts well enough to see a cash problem three months out, hire someone without creating chaos, and decide on evidence rather than mood whether to grow, hold, sell, or stop.

Who it is for

This book is for you if:

  • you write software for a living and have done for fifteen or twenty years;
  • you are thinking about going out on your own — a consultancy, a small product, an agency, or buying something small that already works;
  • it will be self-funded, heading for somewhere between one and twenty people;
  • you will still be doing the work yourself for a long time;
  • and you have something to lose: a salary, a mortgage, possibly people who depend on you.

You arrive already fluent in systems, constraints, iteration and measurement, so nothing here explains what a feedback loop is. The gaps are somewhere else, and across a great many people they are the same gaps: building before proving demand, because building is the enjoyable part; reading interest as purchasing intent; avoiding direct selling and calling the avoidance a marketing strategy; pricing by effort rather than by what the result is worth; conflating revenue, profit and cash; automating a process before establishing that it should exist; remaining the indispensable technical bottleneck, which feels like responsibility; and underestimating distribution, management, and the value of capacity you are not using.

The book is organised around those, not around a curriculum.

Not for the venture-track founder. That reader is comprehensively served elsewhere, and several chapters here would have to say the opposite things. Growth is not assumed to be good. Cash matters more than valuation, because there is no valuation.

How a chapter works

Each chapter starts from a business decision and ends with a practical review or action. The explanation may use a sourced company case, an explicitly invented worked example, or both. Sections follow the decision rather than a fixed number of moves; combined chapters carry one continuous exercise instead of repeating the introduction and conclusion.

Then a counter-case: a second story in which the same advice was followed, correctly and in good faith, and the outcome was bad. Business advice is unusually conditional — delegate, raise your prices, pick a niche, sell before you build are all excellent and all capable of producing disasters — and a principle you cannot see failing is one you cannot use, because you have no way of telling whether you are inside its domain or outside it.

Where a counter-case has not been found, the chapter identifies what evidence is missing. A near-miss does not fill that gap. The method page explains the distinction; a worked example demonstrates its arithmetic, not the effectiveness of advice.

Chapters open with a provocation and then spend their length complicating it. Where a sentence here sounds like a universal law, it is a compression, and the chapter it belongs to will say so. Chapter one is a complete worked example of the form, if you would rather see it than read about it.

Where the answer depends on jurisdiction — what you may do while still employed, invoicing, what happens when you dismiss someone — chapters carry a short On Dutch ground section at their end, dated and sourced, kept separate from the general argument so that neither contaminates the other. One sentence in the argument says the question exists and points there.

Why the order matters

The closest thing this subject has to a standard text is Josh Kaufman's The Personal MBA, and its instincts are right: name real companies, keep the sections short, argue rather than lecture. But it is a glossary — two hundred and fifty alphabetised concepts with no accumulation, so you can read the last page first and lose nothing.

You cannot usefully ask whether your business can run without you until you know whether the money works, and you cannot ask that until somebody has paid you. Answering the questions out of order is not merely inefficient. It is how competent people build machines that lose money faster.

That said, some readers will not read in order, and it is better to give them a route than to pretend otherwise. If you read four: one, for the question every other chapter is a version of; four, for the only apparatus in the book that decides anything; thirteen, for the arithmetic that kills growing businesses; and sixteen, for the number that is not in the accounts at all. Each will send you back to the chapters it stands on.

The chapters

Part I · Chapters 1, 2, 4

Is there anything here?

The cheapest failure available, and the one most often skipped. Everything in this part is about spending small amounts of money and time to avoid spending large ones — including the largest, which is a career. One ladder runs through all three: observation → conversation → payment → a rule → the next commitment, and chapter four closes with the four things you should be able to point at before going on.

  • 01The Only Question That Matters Demand is not a fact about the world; it is a relationship between what people want, what they will pay, and what it costs you to serve them. Webvan, 1996–2001 · Instacart: the advice says proceed
  • 02What They Say, What They Pay Ask about the past, ask for money, and check you could deliver what they would then have bought. Everpix, 2011–2013 · Studio Neat, 2010– · Coolest Cooler: the demand test passed 265 times over
  • 04What Would Justify the Next Step A number, a date and what failing it means, written before anything is sunk, then applied to the salary. Cydoc, 2018–2025 · Bingo Card Creator, 2006–2010 · Gymdesk: the date passed, and he went back to a salary
Part II · Chapters 6, 9, 10

Will someone pay?

Part I proved that one person wanted it once. This part is about whether that repeats: a defined buyer, a way to reach them, the act of asking, a price that survives contact, and early jobs followed through to delivery and use. The gap between interest and money is where most technically strong businesses quietly stop.

  • 06A Buyer You Can Name Pick one kind of buyer, name the customers you turn away, and ask the first few people you can name what frustrates them. ConvertKit, 2013–2015 · Plausible Analytics, 2019–2022 · Growth stalls out when the founder goes on holiday
  • 09What to Charge Selling every slot is not enough if the price cannot carry the promise. Studio Neat's Cosmonaut, 2011 · 3,000 buyers, and still short of the floor
  • 10From the First Sale to a Useful Result Follow the promise through delivery, actual use and correction before choosing the next offer. WP Curve, 2013 · A worked consultancy example and a record for your next decision
Part III · Chapters 12–14, 16

Does the money work?

Read the business as a whole, check the timing of commitments, choose what work to acquire and decide what the result needs to provide for your life.

  • 12Reading Your Own Business and Paying Yourself Trace the jobs into accounts, cost your work once and prepare the next owner payment. Lea’s worked financial review · A positive profit and an uncovered cost of owner time
  • 13Profit Is Not Cash Find the lowest cash balance before accepting the next jobs. Instacart’s S-1 and Lea’s forecast · Compare a deposit, later drawings and delayed payment
  • 14What a Customer Is Worth Choose which work to seek again and what to spend finding it. Cydoc and Lea's customer records · Separate economic return from cash recovery
  • 16Enough What the business must provide for your household, and whether you want to keep doing the work it requires. Pinboard, 2010–2020 · An adequate income and a continuing responsibility

The Business That Does Not Invoice: work through stock, profit and the next buying decision at a market stall. Read alongside chapters 12–13 if your business buys goods before making sales.

Optional chapter

Where the Next Ones Come From: which two ways of reaching buyers deserve your hours, and which keep working after you stop. Read after chapter 14, once you have a few customers and a record of how each arrived.

Part IV · Chapters 17, 19, 20

Can it run without you?

There are two honest answers, and only one of them is a problem. A practice built to give its owner good work and a good income is a legitimate thing to own. So is an asset that runs without them. The danger is not knowing which one you are building.

  • 17Taking Work Off Yourself Choose a dependency, prepare the person who will take it, and test what still comes back to you. A document is only part of the handover. Gymdesk · Bingo Card Creator · Pinboard · counter-case owed
  • 19Deciding to Add Help Define the work, compare arrangements, and test the full commitment against dated cash and the owner's support time before offering the role. Gymdesk / Galperin, 2021–2022 · counter-case owed
  • 20When Work Stops Between Two People Look at the attempt before the person, agree who decides what and by when they answer, and count what the routine costs. Groove, 2015–2016 · One blocked report, one missed response, 75 → 95 minutes
Part V · Chapters 23–24, 26, 28

Will it last?

Businesses rarely die of the thing they were watching. This part is about the capacity to absorb what you did not forecast — and the paperwork that decides how bad it gets.

  • 23Capacity for Things Going Wrong Name a disruption, check qualified cover at the required time, fund its commitments and test its limits. Restore cover when new work consumes it. WP Curve / Norris, 2014 · counter-case owed
  • 24Agreements and Protection Connect an exposure to the documents and protection meant to address it, the practical response required, and one prioritised correction or review. Studio Neat · WIPO · Dutch provisions · counter-case owed
  • 26When the Ground Moves A shock nobody caused reaches you through what your customers can no longer do — so read it as their event first, offer the pause before the cancellation, and sell to them as they now are. Gymdesk / Galperin, 2020–2021 · counter-case owed
  • 28Knowing When to Stop Decide whether another attempt is worth funding, a smaller business would work, or it is time to prepare an exit. On Dutch ground, what winding up requires. Binpress / Galperin, 2010–2016 · counter-case owed
Optional chapter

Built to Leave: what a stranger would need in order to run your business and read its books. Read it when selling, handing over or stepping back becomes thinkable. Black Hops / Norris, 2022–2023

Figures quoted in this book are registered against the document they came from, and a script re-checks that register against the original archives, because a book that claims to work from filings should let you confirm it without trusting the author. See the method page for how that works and what it has already caught, or go straight to the source documents, the claim register and the verification script on GitHub.

What that script does not check. It proves a quoted figure appears in the document it is credited to. It cannot tell whether the case actually supports the lesson the chapter draws from it, and that has been wrong more than once — a causal arrow pointing backwards, a rule stated before its qualifier, a category label claiming more than the evidence. Every one was found by reading rather than by the register, and every one is written up on the method page rather than quietly repaired.
