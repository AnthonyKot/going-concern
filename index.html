The Going Concern

A business does not present its problems in alphabetical order. It presents them in a sequence, and each one has to be survived before the next one is even legible. That sequence is this book.

Going concern is an accounting term: a business assumed to keep operating into the foreseeable future, rather than one being broken up and sold for parts. Auditors are required to say so out loud when they doubt it. The phrase does double duty here, because the thing that keeps going is also the thing that keeps you awake.

The closest thing this subject has to a standard text is Josh Kaufman's The Personal MBA, and its instincts are right: name real companies, keep the sections short, argue rather than lecture. But it is a glossary — two hundred and fifty alphabetised concepts with no accumulation, so you can read the last page first and lose nothing. This book is arranged the other way:

demand → payment → economics → operations → resilience

You cannot usefully ask whether your business can run without you until you know whether the money works, and you cannot ask that until somebody has paid you. Answering the questions out of order is not merely inefficient. It is how competent people build machines that lose money faster.

Who this is for

An experienced software developer — call it fifteen or twenty years in, salaried, probably in the Netherlands or somewhere like it — who is thinking about going out on their own. A consultancy, a small product, an agency, or buying something small that already works.

That reader arrives with an unusual profile. They already understand systems, constraints, iteration, measurement and technical risk better than most business books assume. Nothing here will explain what a feedback loop is. The blind spots are somewhere else entirely, and they are remarkably consistent:

  • building before proving demand, because building is the enjoyable part;
  • reading interest as purchasing intent;
  • avoiding direct selling, and calling the avoidance a marketing strategy;
  • pricing by effort rather than by what the result is worth to the buyer;
  • conflating revenue, profit and cash;
  • automating a process before finding out whether it should exist;
  • staying the indispensable technical bottleneck, which feels like responsibility;
  • underestimating distribution, management, and the value of unused capacity.

The book is organised around those, not around a curriculum. It also assumes you are old enough to have something to lose — a salary, a mortgage, possibly dependants — which is why it opens with five chapters on how to find out cheaply whether there is anything here at all, and closes with one on how to stop.

Where the answer depends on jurisdiction — employment law, invoicing, what you may do while still employed, what happens when you dismiss someone — chapters carry a short On Dutch ground box, dated and sourced, kept separate from the general argument so that neither contaminates the other.

How a chapter works

Four moves — the situation, what actually happened, the reasoning, Monday — then a counter-case: a second story in which the same advice was followed and it went badly.

The counter-case is not a disclaimer. Business advice is unusually conditional — delegate, raise your prices, pick a niche, sell before you build are all excellent and all capable of producing disasters — and a principle you cannot see failing is one you cannot use, because you have no way of telling whether you are inside its domain or outside it. Chapters open with a provocation and then spend their length complicating it. Where a sentence here sounds like a universal law, it is a compression, and the chapter it belongs to will say so.

Part IV · Chapters 17–22

Can it run without you?

There are two honest answers, and only one of them is a problem. A practice built to give its owner good work and a good income is a legitimate thing to own. So is an asset that runs without them. The danger is not knowing which one you are building.
