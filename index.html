The Going Concern

A business does not present its problems in alphabetical order. It presents them in a sequence, and each one has to be survived before the next one is even legible. That sequence is this book.

Going concern is an accounting term: a business assumed to keep operating into the foreseeable future, rather than one being broken up and sold for parts. Auditors are required to say so out loud when they doubt it. The phrase is doing double duty here, because the thing that keeps going is also the thing that keeps you awake.

The closest thing this subject has to a standard text is Josh Kaufman's The Personal MBA, and its instincts are right: name real companies, keep the sections short, argue rather than lecture. But it is a glossary — two hundred and fifty alphabetised concepts with no accumulation, so you can read the last page first and lose nothing. This book is arranged the other way. You cannot usefully ask whether your business can run without you until you know whether the money works, and you cannot ask that until somebody has paid you.

Each chapter runs four moves — the situation, what actually happened, the reasoning, Monday — and closes with a counter-case: a second story in which the same advice was followed and it went badly. The counter-case is not a disclaimer. It is the part most business books leave out, and leaving it out is why so much of the genre reads as though survivorship were a strategy.

Written for the owner of a self-funded business with somewhere between one and twenty people in it, who still works in the business as well as on it. Not for the venture-track founder; that reader is served elsewhere, and several chapters here would have to say the opposite things.
